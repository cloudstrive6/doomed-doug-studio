# Why the Oarfish Can Cut Off Its Own Body (draft 3)

[Opening frame: the thumbnail. A huge silver ribbon oarfish standing vertical in dark blue water, red crest at the top, filling the frame top to bottom. Tiny Doug at mid-height holding the cut-off end of its tail, looking at the camera. Text: IT LET GO. Hold 1.5 to 4 seconds.]

[Cut to Doug's living room. Doug on his couch with his phone. DOUG DEATHS: 425. Under it, the tape-measure readout: DISTANCE TO FISH: 9,000 KM.]

In Japan, many people believe that when this fish washes up, an earthquake is coming.

By the end, it cuts off its own body with Doug attached, and Doug is inside one, not alone.

## The Doomsday Fish

The Doomsday Fish.

[Doug's phone screen, huge: a crude news headline, "DOOMSDAY FISH SPOTTED", with a drawing of a long silver fish.]

In twenty nineteen, scientists tested the legend against eighty years of fish reports and earthquakes in Japan.

[A crude drawing of an undersea palace. A silver fish leaves it, heading up. Red curved arrow to the surface.]

The oarfish there is called ryugu no tsukai, meaning messenger from the sea god's palace, and legend says it rises from the deep to warn of disaster. One folklore researcher knows of no old tale where it predicts earthquakes.

Here's the problem. A team led by Yoshiaki Orihara of Tokai University lined up three hundred and thirty-six deep-sea fish reports against two hundred and twenty-one earthquakes of magnitude six or stronger. A match meant a fish within a month and a hundred kilometers of a quake.

[A long chart. Little fish icons along the top, little zigzags along the bottom. One single dotted line connects a fish to a zigzag.]

Only one earthquake could plausibly be linked to a fish sighting, in more than eighty years of records.

It's like checking a rooster against eight decades of weather forecasts and discovering that it got the weather right exactly once, on a Tuesday.

[Red X stamp over the chart.]

The fish predicts nothing.

[Doug zipping up a huge emergency backpack: a torch, tinned beans, a helmet. He puts the helmet on over the cap.]

Doug has read the headline and the whole study, and he has packed an emergency kit anyway, just in case. Doug is fine. Doug is prepared for a fish.

The fish itself is very real, though, and from here on, it only gets closer to Doug.

Far away.

## King of Herrings

King of Herrings.

[A wooden sailing ship on a grey sea. Doug on deck with a spyglass, an open logbook beside him. DISTANCE TO FISH: 1 KM.]

Fishermen in northern Europe called this fish the king of herrings, and one explanation is that it was seen near shoals of herring, so people thought it was leading them.

[A silver ribbon on the horizon, rippling. Herring shoal drawn as dots behind it. Label "KING?".]

Even its scientific name sounds like a promotion. The genus is Regalecus, from a Latin word meaning belonging to a king, and the giant oarfish's first described specimen came from a farm near Bergen, in Norway.

The English name has a story of its own. People once believed the fish rowed itself through the water with its long pelvic fins, like a pair of oars, and although it doesn't, the name stayed. In fact, it swims by rippling the long red fin that runs down its back.

[Doug writes "SEA SERPENT" in the logbook in big letters.]

The strange part is how often it got to be the monster. Its size, its long flat body and the way it ripples through the water have made it a likely source of some of the old sea serpent sightings.

Picture a two-storey house lying on its side, painted silver, swimming past your boat with a red mane on top. You would write something down too.

[The ship's cook walks over and takes the spyglass from Doug. Doug leans out over the rail to see without it. Cut away. DOUG DEATHS: 426. The cap floats on the grey water beside the ship.]

Doug leaned out over the rail for a better look, and Doug has died. Again. The logbook says sea serpent, and the cook says nothing at all.

The next oarfish comes much closer, because it has already reached the beach.

## The Strandings

The Strandings.

[A sunny Southern California beach. A long silver fish lies on the sand at the waterline, a crowd around it. DISTANCE TO FISH: 100 M.]

In twenty twenty-four, three oarfish turned up on Southern California beaches in a single year, which is a lot for a fish the state had only recorded about twenty times since nineteen-oh-one.

[Calendar pages flip: AUG, SEP, NOV. A small fish icon drops onto each.]

The first one surfaced at La Jolla Cove in August. It was a three point seven meter adult male, and scientists at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography took it back to their lab for a necropsy.

Another one washed up at Huntington Beach in September, and a third was found on Grandview Beach in Encinitas in November.

[A small Scripps van parks beside the fish. Two stick scientists with a cooler.]

That's roughly a cargo van's worth of fish, parked on the sand in the spot where people normally put their towels.

Here's the catch. Nobody knows why they come up. Scripps says it is generally thought to be injury, illness or disorientation, and the La Jolla beach sits beside two underwater canyons that bring deep water close to shore.

A Scripps fish expert has also said it may reflect changes in ocean conditions.

[Big red "?" over the fish.]

Even after the necropsy, the scientists could not find a cause of death.

[Doug with a news microphone at the waterline, standing beside the fish. The crowd gathers and lifts the whole fish for a photo, the middle of it directly over Doug. Cut away. DOUG DEATHS: 427. The cap sits on the stranded fish's red crest.]

Doug was there to report on the fish, but the crowd lifted it for a group photo, and Doug was standing under the middle. Doug has died.

All of those fish arrived dead, but the next one is alive, and it is standing up.

Close.

## The Standing Fish

The Standing Fish.

[Dark blue water. A tiny round submarine with one porthole. Doug inside. A silver oarfish hangs straight up and down ten meters away. DISTANCE TO FISH: 10 M.]

Between two thousand eight and two thousand eleven, robot submarines working for the oil industry in the Gulf of Mexico filmed five live ones. A team led by Mark Benfield of Louisiana State University published the footage in twenty thirteen.

[Label "5 SIGHTINGS". Five small ROV drawings with spotlights.]

Doug met one before, briefly, hanging upright with its red fin rippling, and he did not ask it anything.

This time there is more to see. The fish hangs head up and tail down, and Benfield said it can move backward and forward, up and down, quite easily, just by rippling that one fin.

Imagine a broom handle balanced on its end, floating in the dark, that can reverse whenever it feels like it.

[A tiny krill drawn above the fish, black against a pale glow from above. Red curved arrow from the fish's eye up to it.]

Here's the clever part. Scientists think the upright pose may help it find food, because faint light comes down from above, so anything swimming overhead shows up as a dark shape.

The fish also did not flee from the robot's lights, and Benfield said that could mean it has few natural predators.

[Close-up on the fin. A tiny isopod clings to one spine. Label "PASSENGER".]

One of the five was carrying a parasitic isopod on its fin, the first time anyone had recorded one on this species.

[Doug tilts his whole submarine vertical to match the fish, to be polite. The fish ignores him. Nothing happens.]

Doug tilts his submarine upright, to be polite. The fish ignores him completely, and Doug survives.

[The narrator's hand slowly lowers a pre-drawn DOUG DEATHS: 428 sticker back into a drawer.]

The narrator is quietly disappointed. Doug's next stop is the mouth, and on a fish this big, the strangest thing about it is what isn't there.

## The Mouth

The Mouth.

[Close-up: a long silver head, big eyes, a small mouth. No teeth drawn. Doug, in a diving mask, at arm's length. DISTANCE TO FISH: 1 M.]

The longest bony fish in the world has no visible teeth.

Its mouth is small, and it can push the jaw forward to scoop up food. Inside, comb-like gill rakers, thirty-three to forty-seven on the first arch in big adults, strain small animals out of the water. A gill raker works like the teeth of a comb. Water flows out through the gaps, and anything too big to fit through stays behind.

[Label "NO TEETH". Then a pile of tiny krill appears beside the head.]

In every stomach the Australian Museum could verify, the food was krill, the small shrimp-like crustaceans, and most kinds of krill are only one or two centimeters long as adults.

That's a city bus that lives on rice, one grain at a time.

But that's not the strange part. One oarfish caught off California was found with about ten thousand krill inside it, because this fish does not bite its food, it sieves it.

[The narrator's hand holds up a krill costume next to Doug. Hesitates. Puts it away.]

The narrator did consider a krill costume this time, and then decided Doug already has enough problems.

[Doug reaches toward the mouth with a hopeful smile. Speech bubble: "hi friend?". The fish does not react.]

The longest bony fish on Earth has no way to hurt Doug, and yet it keeps happening anyway.

[A cartoon air gauge on Doug's chest drops to E. Doug looks at it. X eyes. Cut away. DOUG DEATHS: 428.]

Doug was three hundred meters down and forgot to check his air. The fish did nothing, and it is extremely good at doing nothing.

The fish has never laid a fin on Doug. Doug is about to lay a hand on the fish, though, and it leaves a mark.

Touching.

## The Silver

The Silver.

[Doug, new air tank, mask on, puts one hand flat on the oarfish's side. DISTANCE TO FISH: 0 M.]

The oarfish has no scales. Instead, its skin is covered in a coat of silvery guanine, the same kind of crystal that makes many other fish shine. The crystals sit in thin stacked layers, and each layer bounces back a little light, so together they work like a mirror.

[A close-up of the skin. Tiny stacked crystal plates drawn as glints. Label "GUANINE".]

At the surface that skin is soft and easily damaged. In twenty fifteen, a New Zealand conservation officer described a stranded oarfish as smooth, like tinfoil, and said that if you rubbed it, the silver came off onto your hand.

So it's a little like fresh paint on a park bench, except that the bench is longer than your living room and still moving.

[Doug takes his hand away. It's bright silver. He looks at it.]

The worst part is the family history. The Florida Museum notes that a close relative of the oarfish, called the streamer fish, can give a mild shock when it's handled by people.

[Red "?" stamp between the oarfish and a small drawing of a streamer fish.]

Whether the oarfish can do the same thing is, in the museum's own word, unclear, and nobody has stayed in the water long enough to settle it.

[Doug touches the fish with his other hand, just to check. Now both hands are silver. He holds them up to the camera, pleased.]

Doug touches it again with the other hand, just to check. Doug is fine. Doug is now shiny on both hands.

He has a very good grip now. The title fish trick is next, and Doug is about to be holding most of it.

## The Missing Half

The Missing Half.

[Doug grabs the oarfish's tail end with both silver hands. DISTANCE TO FISH: 0 M (HOLDING).]

Nearly every large oarfish ever found has been missing the back end of its body, which finishes in a rounded scar instead of a tail.

For a long time, people blamed bites. Then, in a twenty twelve monograph on the genus, the ichthyologist Tyson Roberts argued that the scar comes from the fish cutting off its own body.

[A clean cartoon stump, rounded and healed, no red. Label "SCAR".]

This is called autotomy, like a lizard dropping its tail, and FishBase says every recorded oarfish over one and a half meters long has a shortened body.

The cut happens behind the vent, toward the back of the fish, while its organs sit toward the head end, so it can lose a long stretch of itself without losing anything vital.

[A tape measure along a silver body. Notches marked at several points.]

Here's the disturbing part. It can do this again and again, sometimes a few bones at a time and sometimes a great deal more. Roberts wrote that it may continue until only the head and abdomen are left.

The lost part never grows back. That means even the eight-meter giants in the record books were already missing a piece.

It's like leaving the whole back of your car on the road and driving home in the front seat.

That is why it can do it. Everything vital sits in the front. Why it chooses to is still unknown.

[The oarfish lets go. The front half drifts calmly up and out of frame. Doug keeps about two meters of silver tail, holding it like a prize. He sinks with it. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 429. DISTANCE TO FISH: 0 M (HOLDING), then the readout blanks.]

Doug held on tight. The fish simply let go of about two meters of itself, and Doug went down with it. Doug has died. Again.

The fish is completely fine, and for the last two items, we go inside one.

Inside.

## The Last Meal

The Last Meal.

[A crude beach, October 2013. A long silver fish being dragged ashore by a line of fifteen stick figures. Label "15 PEOPLE". DISTANCE TO FISH: -1 M, stamped red.]

In October twenty thirteen, a marine science instructor named Jasmine Santana was snorkeling off Catalina Island, California, when she found a dead oarfish about five and a half meters long.

She tried to pull it out by herself, and in the end it took fifteen people to get it onto the beach.

[Doug, cartoon-shrunk to the size of a thumb, wearing a diving mask and holding a torch, walks into the open mouth of a huge oarfish.]

For this part of the trip, Doug has been shrunk to the size of a thumb. Doug did not agree to this.

He walks in through the mouth and past the gill rakers, looking for all of those krill.

[Inside: a long, dim, pink-grey corridor. Doug's torch beam. Nothing on the floor.]

The gut of an oarfish is long and narrow, like a train corridor, and it very often has nothing in the dining car.

Here's the problem. Most oarfish stomachs that get examined are empty, or close to it, and that same month, scientists examined a four-meter oarfish that had washed up at Oceanside, near San Diego.

Its stomach was almost empty, with just a few krill inside.

[Label "ALMOST EMPTY". Doug shines his torch around. One krill. He waves at it.]

But in the researcher's words, it was filled with parasites.

[Doug stops. His torch points down the dark corridor. Something long and pale shifts at the far end.]

Doug survives this one, for now, and the gut is quiet. But the stomach was never the busy part of the fish, and the busy part is further down.

## The Tenants

The Tenants.

[Deeper in the gut. Pale cartoon ribbons with dot eyes hang from the walls and lie along the floor, plain, no segments. Tiny Doug with his torch among them. DISTANCE TO FISH: -1 M.]

Back to the Catalina oarfish, the one fifteen people dragged ashore. It was not living alone.

Parasite scientists at the University of California, Santa Barbara, led by Armand Kuris, looked at only a small piece of its gut. In it, they found about two dozen larval tapeworms of two kinds, two young roundworms and a thorny-headed worm.

[Label "TWO DOZEN". A red arrow to the longest ribbon. Label "15 CM".]

One of those tapeworms was about fifteen centimeters long, and Kuris said that these are majorly parasitized fish.

At the time, he said, there were only two papers ever written on oarfish parasites. Then, in twenty twenty-one, researchers in Mexico counted sixty-three tapeworm larvae in the intestine of a single giant oarfish.

Picture a long hallway where every single room is rented, and the landlord never comes downstairs to check.

[Doug holds up his torch to the nearest ribbon. Speech bubble: "roommates?". The ribbons do not move.]

Doug introduces himself to the nearest one. "roommates?"

The most disturbing part is what they're waiting for. These are only larvae, and the scientists think the adult form of that tapeworm lives in the shortfin mako shark, so the worms are not planning to stay in the oarfish.

They are waiting for something bigger to eat it. This fish is just the waiting room.

[Doug sits down on the gut floor among the ribbons, torch in his lap, like a commuter waiting for a train. The torch flickers and goes out. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 430. DISTANCE TO FISH: -1 M in red.]

Doug sat down to wait with them, but the torch ran out long before the shark arrived. Doug has died.

[Closing: the surface ship's monitor, red LIVE tag. On the camera feed, a healthy oarfish drifts upright past the lens, the end of its body a neat rounded stump. The cap drifts in, catches on the long red fin, rides it all the way down, and settles on the seabed.]

The oarfish drifted past the camera, short one tail and perfectly fine. The cap rode its fin all the way to the bottom, and then the oarfish let go of that too.

Doug deaths: four hundred and thirty. The oarfish can let go of things. This number can't. Subscribe and watch it hold on.
