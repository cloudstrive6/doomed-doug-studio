## Platypus

Platypus.

It looks like a joke that nature built out of spare parts: a duck's bill, a beaver's tail and webbed feet. It is also one of the very few mammals on Earth that makes venom.

[A detailed platypus on the bank. Red arrow to its back ankle. Label: "SPUR".]

Only the adult males carry it, in a hollow spur on the inside of each back ankle that is about fifteen to eighteen millimeters long, a little longer than a staple.

The spur is connected to a venom gland up in the thigh, and venom production peaks in the breeding season, which tells you who the weapon is really meant for: other male platypuses.

Here's the problem. Morphine barely touches the pain.

[A pill bottle labelled "MORPHINE" with a red X over it.]

In a case written up by Australian doctors in nineteen ninety-two, a man was spurred in the hand, and morphine gave him little relief.

The pain and swelling from a spur can last for weeks, and some weakness in the area can hang around for months after that.

The venom is not known to be life threatening. It just makes very sure that you remember the platypus.

[Doug kneels at the water's edge, hand out. Speech bubble: "hi friend?" The field guide's "FRIEND?" box gets a tick. Cut: Doug, hand bandaged like a boxing glove, thousand-yard stare. The guide page gets "MAYBE". DOUG DEATHS: 84.]

Doug tried to pet the duck-billed one, and the duck-billed one used a weapon designed for other platypuses, which worked perfectly well on Doug.

Doug is fine, according to Doug, who will not be using that hand for several weeks.

## Bullet Ant

Bullet Ant.

Its sting does not stop when the ant lets go. When the ant lets go, the sting is barely getting started.

[A large, dark, glossy ant on a rainforest tree trunk. Label: "BULLET ANT".]

The bullet ant lives in the rainforests of Central and South America, and a worker is about two centimeters long, roughly the length of a paperclip, which is very large for an ant.

[A paperclip next to the ant for scale.]

The entomologist Justin Schmidt spent much of his career getting stung by insects and ranking the pain on a scale from one to four. The bullet ant sits at the very top.

He compared the sting to walking over flaming charcoal with a long nail stuck in your heel.

The main toxin is called poneratoxin, and it stops the sodium channels in your nerves from switching off, so the pain signal just keeps on firing.

But that's not the disturbing part. It can keep firing for up to twenty-four hours.

[A clock face spinning through a full day. Label: "24 HOURS".]

The Sateré-Mawé people of the Brazilian Amazon use these ants in a coming of age ceremony. The ants are first calmed with a plant mixture, then woven into gloves with their stingers pointing inward, and young men put the gloves on. The ceremony is repeated many times over a lifetime.

[Doug, still holding his bandaged platypus hand, reaches for a leaf with the other. The ant is on it. Both hands bandaged now. The guide page gets "MAYBE". DOUG DEATHS: 84.]

Doug, still nursing his platypus hand, used the other one to move a leaf, and now both of his hands are out of service.

## Asian Giant Hornet

Asian Giant Hornet.

It is the largest hornet in the world, and these days it usually goes by the name northern giant hornet, which does not make it any smaller.

[A huge hornet, orange head, black and yellow body. Label: "UP TO 5 CM".]

It can grow to about five centimeters long, the length of a matchbox, with a wingspan of about seven and a half centimeters, and its stinger is longer than a honeybee's.

Unlike a honeybee, it can sting more than once, and its venom is stronger than that of any bee or wasp native to Washington State, where it briefly turned up a few years ago.

In late summer, a group of these hornets can attack a honeybee hive and completely destroy a healthy colony in a matter of hours.

In Japan, government statistics counted one hundred and ninety-one deaths from wasp and bee stings between two thousand and eight and two thousand and eighteen, about seventeen a year. An earlier Japanese review found that most deaths like these come from allergic reactions.

And then it gets worse. The venom calls for backup.

[A cartoon scent cloud rises off the hornet. Red "!" marks pop up on other hornets in the distance.]

In two thousand and three, Japanese scientists found that this hornet's venom carries an alarm pheromone, a chemical signal that sends the other hornets nearby into attack mode, and swatting one can release it.

## Komodo Dragon

Komodo Dragon.

For decades, the textbook story was that this lizard killed with a filthy mouth. The textbook was wrong, and the real story is worse.

[A massive Komodo dragon on a dry hillside. Label: "LARGEST LIVING LIZARD".]

The Komodo dragon is the largest living lizard on Earth, and the longest one ever verified measured about three point one meters, roughly a meter longer than a double bed.

The old theory came from field observations. Large animals bitten by a dragon often seemed to die later from infection, so the biologist Walter Auffenberg, who spent a year watching them, suggested that bacteria in its mouth were doing the killing.

The worst part is that it was venom all along.

[A cross-section of the dragon's head. Red arrow to the lower jaw. Label: "VENOM GLAND".]

In two thousand and nine, a study that scanned a dragon's head found venom glands in its jaw, and the venom stops blood from clotting and sends blood pressure crashing.

A later study of sixteen captive dragons found no dangerous bacteria in their mouths at all, and although researchers haven't completely ruled the bacteria out, the dirty mouth is no longer the headline.

Wild adults typically weigh about seventy kilograms, roughly as much as a grown man. Attacks on people are rare. In the thirty-five years up to two thousand and nine, four people were killed.

[Doug walks up holding a giant bottle labelled "MOUTHWASH". Speech bubble: "open wide?" Cut to a dust cloud. Doug with X eyes. The bottle rolls away. The guide page gets "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 86.]

Doug had read the old textbook, so he brought the dragon some mouthwash. The dragon did not want mouthwash.

## Indian Red Scorpion

Indian Red Scorpion.

It is described as one of the deadliest scorpions in the world, and it lives across India and the countries around it.

[A reddish-orange scorpion, tail raised, on a stone floor at night. Label: "5 TO 9 CM".]

The Indian red scorpion is about five to nine centimeters long, roughly the length of your finger, and it comes out to hunt at night.

Its venom triggers a flood of stress hormones in the body, and that flood puts a heavy strain on the heart.

Doctors have linked these stings to inflammation of the heart muscle and to a heart that struggles to pump. It is a small animal with a very specific target.

[World map with seven regions highlighted. Label: "1.2 MILLION STINGS A YEAR".]

Scorpions as a group are a much bigger problem than most people realize. One review estimated more than one point two million scorpion stings a year worldwide, leading to more than three thousand two hundred deaths. The seven hot spots it identified are home to about two point three billion people.

The strange part is that scorpions glow.

[The room goes dark. Blacklight on. The scorpion lights up a vivid blue green.]

Shine an ultraviolet light on a scorpion, and its hard outer shell glows an eerie blue green, thanks to compounds built into the exoskeleton itself.

Nobody knows why they do it. Scientists have tested ideas like finding each other, luring prey and blocking sunlight, none of them has held up, and some researchers think the glow may not do anything at all.

[Doug in a dark room holding a UV torch. Speech bubble: "where are you?" He points the torch down at his own boot. The boot glows. Several glowing tails rise out of it. Cut to X eyes. The guide page gets "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 87.]

## Saw-Scaled Viper

Saw-Scaled Viper.

If you were asked to pick the snake that kills the most people on Earth, you would probably not pick something this small.

[A small, sandy-brown viper coiled in tight loops on dry ground. Label: "UNDER 90 CM".]

Most saw-scaled vipers are under ninety centimeters long, and some are barely thirty, the length of a school ruler.

They range from West Africa all the way across to India, and their venom stops the blood from clotting properly.

When one feels threatened, it coils into loops and rubs its rough side scales against each other, which makes a dry rasping sound, a bit like water sizzling in a hot pan. The saw in its name comes from those rough, serrated-looking scales.

[Sound wave lines come off the coils. Label: "RASP".]

Here's what nobody expects. This little snake probably bites and kills more people than any other species of snake.

[A hospital ward drawing. One bed in ten is highlighted red.]

That is how a team of snakebite doctors put it after studying patients in Nigeria in the nineteen seventies, where in one region its victims could fill about one hospital bed in ten.

According to the World Health Organization, about five point four million people are bitten by snakes every year. Snakebites of all kinds kill somewhere between eighty-one thousand and one hundred and thirty-eight thousand of them.

[Doug, hand to his ear, scanning the horizon with binoculars for something huge. The viper is right by his foot. Cut to X eyes. The guide page gets "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 88.]

Doug heard the rasping and looked around for something big, so he was looking in the wrong direction, and for the wrong size.

The snake that probably kills more people than any other is very easy to step on, and Doug stepped on it.

## Blue-Ringed Octopus

Blue-Ringed Octopus.

It is about the size of a golf ball, and for most of the day it is a dull brown. You only see the blue once it's upset.

[A small brown octopus in a rock pool. Its rings flash electric blue. Label: "WARNING".]

When it is disturbed, bright blue rings flash across its body in under half a second, as a warning to predators, and most predators take the hint.

[A golf ball next to the octopus for scale.]

Its venom contains tetrodotoxin, the same toxin found in pufferfish, and the octopus doesn't even make it itself, because bacteria living in its salivary glands do. By one estimate, a single adult weighing about twenty-five grams carries enough venom to paralyze ten adult humans.

Tetrodotoxin blocks the signals that tell your muscles to move, including the muscles you use to breathe, and there is no antivenom.

The disturbing part is that you might not feel the bite at all, since bites are usually reported as painless.

Some people who have been bitten describe being fully conscious, but unable to speak or move.

Several species live around Australia, and people have been bitten after picking up an empty-looking shell with one hiding inside. There have been several deaths in Australia from this octopus.

[Doug lifts the octopus out of the rock pool in cupped hands, delighted. Speech bubble: "so pretty". The rings flash. Doug's face goes flat. He sits down very slowly, eyes open. Cut to X eyes. The guide page gets "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 89.]

Doug saw the flashing blue rings and took them as a greeting, so he picked it up because it was pretty, and then he sat very still for a while.

Doug has met a venomous snail before, and it was a lot slower than this.

## Black Mamba

Black Mamba.

The first thing to know about the black mamba is that it is not black. The second thing is how you find out where the name comes from.

[A long, slim, olive-grey snake in a tree on the savanna. Label: "UP TO 4.5 M".]

It can grow past four meters, about the length of a family car, although about two and a half meters is more typical, which is still longer than a sofa.

Its skin is olive to grey, and it can move at up to about twenty kilometers an hour in short bursts, roughly the speed of a relaxed bike ride.

[A family car next to the snake for scale.]

Given the chance, a mamba would rather escape, but if it is cornered it can strike again and again. Its venom attacks the nerves, and breathing can become difficult within half an hour of a bite, roughly the length of a lunch break. Before antivenom existed, a bite was considered almost always fatal.

Here's the catch. The name comes from inside its mouth.

[The mamba gapes. The inside of its mouth is inky blue-black. Label: "BLACK".]

When a black mamba feels threatened, it opens its mouth wide to show a lining of deep blue-black, and that is the black in black mamba.

So the one part of this snake that is actually black is the part you see last.

[Doug has painted himself grey from cap to shoes, holding a sign: "NOT A MOUSE". He leans in close, waiting. The mamba opens its mouth. Speech bubble: "oh, there it is". Cut to X eyes. The guide page gets "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 90.]

Doug painted himself grey to blend in, held up a sign saying he was not a mouse, and waited patiently, very close, to see the black. He saw the black.

Doug has died. Again. The grey paint did not help, and neither did the sign, which the mamba could not read.

## Sydney Funnel-Web Spider

Sydney Funnel-Web Spider.

With this spider, the female mostly stays at home in her burrow. The one to worry about is the male, and he comes looking.

[A glossy black spider rearing up, fangs out, on a suburban back step at night. Label: "MALE".]

On warm nights between about November and April, the males leave their burrows and wander in search of females, sometimes straight into people's houses.

Legs and all, a big male can stretch to around ten centimeters, about the width of your palm, and when it feels threatened it rears up and shows its fangs.

Funnel-web spiders as a group have thirteen recorded deaths. Males caused all of them, and the male of this species probably caused most. Bites from females have not killed anyone. An antivenom was introduced in nineteen eighty-one, and no one has died from a bite since then.

Before that, one museum arachnologist says, a death once came within fifteen minutes.

The odd part is who the venom works on.

[A row of animals: a dog, a cat, a rabbit, each with a green tick. Then a monkey and a person, each with a red X.]

The key toxin in the male's venom hits the nervous systems of humans and monkeys hard, but not other mammals. Dogs, cats and rabbits mostly shrug it off, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a cat.

This spider has never eaten a primate or been eaten by one. Scientists think the toxin evolved to fight off other predators, and it just happens to work best on us.

[Doug zipped into a full cat costume, red cap on top of the cat head, sitting smugly on the back step. The spider walks up. Cut: the costume lies flat with X eyes. The guide page gets "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 91.]

It does not matter. Doug zipped himself into an entire cat, put his cap on top, and sat on the back step looking smug.

Doug is a primate. The costume could not change that, and the spider checked.

## Inland Taipan

Inland Taipan.

In laboratory tests on mice, this snake has the most toxic venom of any snake tested. Doug has been sent to find it.

[A vast, cracked, sunbaked floodplain. A tiny Doug walks across it with a backpack. Label: "CHANNEL COUNTRY".]

The inland taipan lives in a remote, semi-arid corner of Australia called the Channel Country, in south-western Queensland and north-eastern South Australia.

It is about two meters long, about as long as a door is tall. It also changes color with the seasons, going darker in winter to soak up heat and paler in summer to stay cool.

[Two snakes side by side, one dark, one pale. Labels: "WINTER", "SUMMER".]

That ranking comes from a nineteen seventy-nine study that compared snake venoms on lab mice, and mice are not people, which turns out to matter here.

In the wild, it eats almost nothing but small mammals, especially the long-haired rat.

Its other common name is the fierce snake. Scientists describe it as shy and relatively placid, and when it is provoked, it puts on a warning display first.

Only a handful of people have ever been bitten by one, mostly people handling captive snakes, and every one of them survived.

The surprise is that the most toxic snake on Earth has no confirmed human deaths.

[A big red "0" with a question mark.]

Remember the saw-scaled viper, the small one Doug stepped on earlier? That is the kind of snake that kills people. Toxic venom is one thing, but being common, nearby and easy to step on is something else.

[Doug, sunburnt, finally meets the taipan. They look at each other. The taipan turns and slides away. Doug stands alone. Speech bubble: "bye friend?" The guide page gets "MAYBE". DOUG DEATHS: 91.]

Doug walked for days across the outback to find it, and when he finally did, it looked at him once and left.

Doug is fine. The narrator had already written the death for this one and is now quietly deleting it.

## Irukandji Jellyfish

Irukandji Jellyfish.

Some jellyfish are big enough to spot from a boat, but this one is barely the size of your fingernail, and that is the first problem.

[Dusk over tropical water. A tiny, nearly invisible box-shaped jelly with four long threads. Label: "1 TO 2 CM".]

Its bell is about one to two centimeters across, its tentacles can stretch to about a meter, roughly the length of a guitar, and it lives in the waters off northern Australia.

Its bell is transparent and shaped like a tiny box, so in the water it is practically invisible. The sting itself is usually mild. It can easily go unnoticed.

[A clock with the minute hand sweeping. Label: "30 MIN LATER".]

Then, around thirty minutes later, a whole set of severe symptoms can arrive at once. Doctors call it Irukandji syndrome, and it can strain the heart and send blood pressure soaring. The syndrome was named in nineteen fifty-two, and several small box jellyfish species are now thought to cause it.

What's truly unsettling is that impending doom is literally a symptom.

[The words "IMPENDING DOOM" in red handwriting, underlined twice.]

People stung by these jellyfish describe a powerful feeling that they are about to die, and one researcher says it isn't really fear, because patients become completely certain of it.

In two thousand and two, two people in Australia died after stings that caused Irukandji syndrome, and doctors linked both deaths to dangerously high blood pressure.

[Doug floating on his back at dusk, relaxed, hopeful smile. Nothing happens. Label: "+25 MIN". His face goes flat, then to gritted teeth. Cut to the cap floating alone on dark water. The guide page gets "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 92.]

Doug went for a swim at dusk, felt a tiny prickle and ignored it. Twenty-five minutes later, Doug had a feeling that something terrible was about to happen.

For once in his life, Doug's instincts were completely correct.

## Box Jellyfish

Box Jellyfish.

This is the animal most people picture when they hear the words deadly jellyfish, and it has earned every bit of that reputation.

[Night. Black water. A faint outline glows red, then disappears. Label: "MOST VENOMOUS IN THE SEA".]

The United States National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration calls the Australian box jellyfish the most venomous marine animal, with a sting that can cause paralysis, cardiac arrest and death within a few minutes.

Its bell can be up to thirty centimeters across, a bit bigger than a basketball, and it is almost completely transparent in the water.

From the four corners of that bell hang up to sixty tentacles, and each one can stretch to about three meters, as tall as a basketball hoop, packed along its length with stinging cells.

[A basketball hoop drawn next to a single tentacle. Label: "3 M".]

Since eighteen eighty-four, it has caused at least sixty-three recorded deaths in tropical Australian waters.

But the worst part is that it is not drifting.

[The jellyfish revealed in full: four clusters of eyes at its corners light up. Label: "24 EYES".]

Most jellyfish float wherever the water takes them, but this one swims. It has twenty-four eyes in four groups of six, and some of them have real lenses that focus light.

In two thousand and five, a study in the journal Nature looked at a different, smaller species of box jellyfish. It found that its eye lenses are remarkably well made, even though the eyes seem tuned to see a slightly blurry picture.

It doesn't have a brain, yet box jellyfish use those eyes to steer around obstacles, and this one can swim toward movement.

It can reach about seven kilometers an hour, which is faster than most people walk.

Everything else on this list was dangerous when Doug got too close to it, but this is the only animal that closes the distance by itself.

[Doug wades into black water with a torch, knee deep. Speech bubble: "anyone there?" Nothing in the beam. Then twenty-four tiny lights blink on, right in front of him. One frame of black. DOUG DEATHS: 93. The red cap rises slowly into the torch beam.]

Doug waded in at night with a torch, looking for anything with eyes, and he found twenty-four of them.

The cap floated up into the torch beam on its own.
