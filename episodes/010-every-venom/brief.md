## 2. Escalation axis and items
**Axis: severity to a healthy adult who gets no help.** First venoms that only hurt, then venoms that kill slowly (hours to days), then
venoms that can kill fast (minutes to a few hours). The final item is the most famous and the fastest killer of all: the box jellyfish.

Spoken section headers (2 words or fewer; they must not repeat 004's "Hours." / "Minutes." / "Seconds."):
**"Pain only."** (before item 1, folded into the opener), **"Slow killers."** (before item 3, about 15%), **"Fast killers."** (before
item 7, about 52%, the midpoint zone shift). The background ramp goes from warm sand and jungle green (pain), to dusty orange and red-brown
(slow), to dark teal and near-black (fast; the last two items are at night in the sea). The art director defines the ramp.

Each item lists 2-4 facts to research. The writer must find and cite the exact paper or authority, or cut the claim. **Don't trust
citations in this brief: they are leads.** "Feel like" lines are one short, non-graphic sensation per item, taken from documented
accounts (for example "pain that painkillers don't touch", "you can't move but you can hear everything", "a sense of impending doom").
Never a list of symptoms, and never injury description.

### Pain only.

**1. Platypus** (the hook item; Doug survives)
- Facts: adult males have a spur on each hind leg linked to a venom gland, and venom production peaks in the breeding season (sourced, for
  example the Australian Museum or Whittington & Belov reviews); it's one of very few venomous mammals (sourced); human cases describe pain
  that lasted for weeks or months and that **morphine did little to relieve** (Fenner, Williamson & Myers 1992, *Med J Aust*, "Platypus
  envenomation, a painful learning experience", verify); there are no recorded human deaths (sourced, or say "no known deaths").
- Analogy idea: the spur's size against a sourced everyday object (a fingernail, a thumbtack), against weeks of pain.
- Twist (must land by 0:45, about 145 words; aim for 25-35 s): **the strongest painkillers barely touch it.** The animal people think is
  a joke made by nature has a weapon built for other platypuses, and it works fine on people.
- Doug beat: Doug tries to pet the duck-billed one ("hi friend?"). No death. "Doug is fine." Doug is not fine (a bandaged hand, a thousand-yard
  stare in the next item's first shot). The counter stays at 84. This is the near-death the style bible requires for item 1.

**2. Bullet Ant** (Doug survives)
- Facts: tops Justin Schmidt's sting pain index at 4 (or "4+"), and Schmidt's own description of it (quote at most one short phrase, credited)
  (Schmidt 2016, *The Sting of the Wild*); pain can last up to about 24 hours (sourced); the main toxin is poneratoxin, which affects nerve
  signalling (Piek et al. 1991 or later, verify); the Sateré-Mawé coming-of-age ritual with gloves of these ants (sourced to an
  anthropology or reputable outlet; state it once, respectfully, no jokes about the people).
- Analogy idea: the ant's length against a sourced everyday object (a paperclip, a fingertip), against a whole day of pain.
- Twist: **the most painful insect sting known isn't even trying to kill you.** It's a warning, and it works.
- Doug beat: Doug, still holding his platypus hand, gets stung in the other one. "Doug is fine." (said with less conviction than last time).
  No death. Counter still 84.

### Slow killers.

**3. Asian Giant Hornet** (Vespa mandarinia)
- Facts: the largest hornet in the world, with a body of up to about 4-5 cm (sourced); deaths in Japan from hornet, wasp and bee stings
  number in the tens per year (sourced to a Japanese government or peer-reviewed figure, with the year; say "hornets, wasps and bees"
  if the figure isn't hornet-only); its venom carries an **alarm pheromone** that marks the target and calls more hornets (Ono et al. 2003
  or a later source, verify); a group can wipe out a honeybee hive in hours (sourced).
- Analogy idea: a hornet as long as a sourced object (a matchbox, an adult's thumb).
- Twist: **the sting tags you.** Being stung once is how you get stung by the rest. State the cause of human deaths honestly
  (multiple stings and/or allergic reaction), in one flat line.
- Doug beat: Doug swats the first one with `fly_swatter.json`. Cut to a cloud of hornets. Death 85. The cap lands on the swatter.

**4. Komodo Dragon**
- Facts: the largest living lizard, up to about 3 m (sourced); for decades it was taught that the dragon killed with **toxic mouth bacteria**,
  and a 2009 study found **venom glands** in its lower jaw instead (Fry et al. 2009, *PNAS*); the venom stops blood from clotting and
  lowers blood pressure (same paper; say only that); recorded human deaths are few (sourced, count with years; no victim details).
- Analogy idea: length against a sourced object (a sofa, a canoe, `canoe.json`).
- Twist: **the textbook explanation was wrong**, and the real one is worse: it's venom, not dirty teeth. Note: a later debate
  (for example Weinstein et al. 2012) argues about how much the venom matters. Mention the debate in one line if the writer finds it; don't overclaim.
- Doug beat: Doug arrives with mouthwash for the dragon, ready for the bacteria theory. Death 86 (off-screen, cartoon only: a dust cloud,
  X eyes). **No bite or wound description.**

**5. Indian Red Scorpion** (Hottentotta tamulus; the writer may swap in the deathstalker only if the fatality sources support it better)
- Facts: scorpion stings are estimated to cause **thousands of deaths a year** worldwide (Chippaux & Goyffon 2008, *Acta Tropica*, about
  3,250 a year; verify); the Indian red is often named the most dangerous scorpion species (sourced); the venom overstimulates the
  heart and circulation (one clinical line, sourced); scorpions **glow under ultraviolet light** (sourced).
- Analogy idea: body length against a sourced object (a lipstick, a house key, `house_key.json`).
- Twist: **the glow.** Under a UV torch, every hidden scorpion in the room lights up. Nobody fully knows why they glow (sourced if true).
- Doug beat: Doug turns on a UV torch to look for scorpions. He finds all of them at once, inside his boot. Death 87.

**6. Saw-Scaled Viper** (Echis)
- Facts: often under about 60-90 cm long (sourced range); the genus is thought to cause **more snakebite deaths than any other snake** (WHO
  or a review such as Warrell, verify the wording and say "thought to" if the source hedges); it warns by **rubbing its scales together
  to make a rasping sound** (sourced); its venom stops blood from clotting (one clinical line only; **no bleeding description**).
- Analogy idea: a snake no longer than a sourced object (a baseball bat, a school ruler times three).
- Twist: **the snake that kills the most people is one of the smallest.** It's common, it lives near people, and it's easy to step on.
  Keep the human toll as a number, not a story.
- Doug beat: Doug hears the rasping and looks around for something large. Death 88.

### Fast killers.

**7. Blue-Ringed Octopus** (Hapalochlaena)
- Facts: about the size of a golf ball (body) with arms up to about 10-20 cm (sourced range); its venom contains **tetrodotoxin**, the same
  toxin as pufferfish, made by bacteria (sourced); the bite is often **painless**, and the paralysis can leave a person unable to move or
  breathe **while still aware** (sourced to a clinical review); there is no antivenom (sourced; state as a fact, **no first-aid instructions**).
- Analogy idea: a golf ball (sourced), or a body smaller than a sourced object, against a toxin dose that can kill an adult (only if a reputable
  source gives it; otherwise cut).
- Twist: **you might not feel the bite at all.** The rings flash bright blue as a warning (sourced); Doug reads it as a greeting.
- Doug beat: Doug picks it up in a rock pool because it's pretty. "Feel like" line: awake and unable to move. Death 89. Optional callback
  to 001's cone snail (one line). Keep the paralysis beat calm and deadpan, not frightening.

**8. Black Mamba**
- Facts: one of the longest venomous snakes, typically about 2-3 m (sourced); named for the **black inside of its mouth**, not its skin,
  which is grey or olive (sourced); one of the fastest snakes, with a sourced speed (cut any "outruns a horse" myth); before antivenom, a
  bite was considered almost always fatal (sourced; if no reliable figure exists, cut the claim).
- Analogy idea: length against a sourced object (a double bed is about 2 m, `bed.json`).
- Twist: **it's not black.** The name comes from the one part you see right before it bites.
- Doug beat: Doug paints himself grey to blend in (or holds up a "not a mouse" sign). He waits to see the black. He sees the black. Death 90.

**9. Sydney Funnel-Web Spider** (Atrax robustus)
- Facts: **the male is the dangerous one**, roaming in summer looking for mates (sourced); its toxin (delta-hexatoxin, formerly
  "atracotoxin") is **especially dangerous to primates**, while many other mammals, such as dogs and cats, are much less affected
  (sourced; check the exact wording); there have been **no recorded deaths since the antivenom arrived in 1981** (Australian Museum or
  similar; the 13 recorded deaths before that, verify); the fastest recorded death was within about 15 minutes (sourced; **do not mention the
  victim's age**).
- Analogy idea: leg span against a sourced object (a palm, a coaster).
- Twist: **the venom is mainly a primate problem.** Dogs and cats get off lightly. Doug is a primate.
- Doug beat: **costume gag, inverted.** Doug zips into a cat costume (new drawing; `cat.json` is the base), red cap on top, sure he's safe.
  The narrator states the primate fact once: "which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a cat. It does not matter." Death 91. The costume
  goes flat (X eyes, cartoon only). Played deadpan, no mascot voice.

**10. Inland Taipan** (the planned survival; the narrator is quietly disappointed)
- Facts: by the standard mouse lethality test, it has **the most toxic venom of any land snake** (Broad, Sutherland & Coulter 1979, *Toxicon*,
  verify); it lives in remote semi-arid central-east Australia and is described as **shy** (sourced); recorded bites are very few, and
  **there are no confirmed human deaths** (sourced; if no reliable source says this, reframe as "very few bites, all survived with
  treatment" or cut); it changes colour with the seasons (sourced, optional).
- Analogy idea: its habitat is remote: compare its range or the distance to the nearest town with a sourced everyday distance; or the
  venom's lab potency in a "one bite, N people" claim **only if a reputable source states it**.
- Twist: **the most toxic snake in the world has never killed anyone** (as sourced). One-line callback to item 6: the snake that kills
  the most people was the small one Doug already met.
- Doug beat: Doug walks for days through the outback looking for it, finds it, it looks at him and leaves. "Doug is fine." The narrator had
  already written the death. The counter stays at 91.

**11. Irukandji Jellyfish** (Carukia barnesi and relatives)
- Facts: the bell is about 1-2 cm, the size of a fingertip (sourced); the sting is often barely felt, and severe symptoms start about
  20-30 minutes later (sourced); **Irukandji syndrome** can include **a feeling of impending doom**, which is a documented symptom (sourced
  to a clinical review); deaths are rare, with two cases reported in Australia in 2002 (Fenner & Hadok 2002, *Med J Aust*, verify).
- Analogy idea: a bell no bigger than a fingertip (`fingertip.json`) or a sourced small coin, with tentacles much longer than the body
  (sourced length, compared with a sourced object).
- Twist: **"impending doom" is literally a symptom.** The body knows before the brain does. That's our narrator's favourite fact; let him say it flatly.
- Doug beat: Doug swims out at dusk. Nothing happens. He relaxes. 25 minutes later his face changes (flat to gritted teeth). Death 92. The
  cap floats on dark water.

**12. Box Jellyfish** (Chironex fleckeri; the boss)
- Facts: often called **the most venomous animal in the world** (sourced; attribute the claim, e.g. "considered by many scientists");
  up to about 15 tentacles from each of the bell's 4 corners, each up to about 3 m long, carrying millions of stinging cells (sourced
  numbers); deaths have been recorded **within minutes** of a sting (sourced, e.g. Fenner & Williamson 1996, *Med J Aust*, "Worldwide deaths
  and severe envenomation from jellyfish stings", verify the time); it has **24 eyes**, some with lenses, and it **actively swims and steers**
  rather than drifting (sourced, e.g. Nilsson et al. 2005, *Nature*).
- Analogy idea: tentacles each as long as a sourced object (a car, a double bed and a half), all trailing from a bell the size of a sourced
  object (a basketball, `basketball.json`). It's nearly transparent.
- Twist: **it isn't drifting. It can see.** 24 eyes, and it steers. Everything on this list was dangerous when Doug got close. This one
  comes to him.
- Doug beat: the shortest death of the episode. Doug wades in at night with a torch; the box jellyfish is invisible until the last beat
  (silhouette rule: a faint outline with a red glow, then reveal). Death 93. The cap floats up in the torch beam.
- **Closing image:** Doug's field guide (see 4) lies open on the beach with every page crossed out in red "NO", except the platypus page,
  marked "MAYBE". The cap sits on top. Kicker about the cap or the guide.

Deaths: 9 (85 to 93). Survivals: Platypus, Bullet Ant, Inland Taipan.

## 7. Risks
- **Medical/safety advice (HIGH, the main risk of this episode):** **no first aid, no treatment steps, no "what to do if bitten", no
  "you should" lines.** Antivenom may be named as a historical fact only (funnel-web 1981, "there is no antivenom" for the octopus). No
  dose numbers framed as guidance. No claims that anything is "safe". Add one line to the description: "Edutainment only, not medical
  advice." (the titler owns the description).
- **Gore (medium-high):** venom effects are where gore creeps in. **Use one clinical line per item at most** ("stops blood from clotting",
  "affects the nerves", "can stop breathing"). Never describe wounds, swelling, tissue death, bleeding, vomiting or other symptoms in bodily
  detail. Every Doug death is cartoon only: X eyes, a dust cloud, a ghost Doug, the floating cap. No real photos of bites or injuries,
  ever. Real photos of the animals are fine with a verified licence.
- **Kid-appeal risk (HIGH):** this is an animal topic, with a platypus, an octopus and a cat costume. The `animals` playlist is the most
  kid-adjacent lane we have. The narrator stays dry and adult: the jokes are about wrong textbooks, Doug's bad decisions and the narrator's
  indifference, never about cuteness. Creatures stay at the detailed cartoon tier and look dangerous, not plush. No baby animals. The cat
  costume is played straight and grim. The thumbnail must read as nature horror, not a zoo poster.
- **Human deaths:** state counts as numbers with a source and a year. No named victims, no ages, no stories of individual deaths. No children
  in any example (the funnel-web 15-minute record is stated without age).
- **Cultural respect:** the Sateré-Mawé ritual is stated once, factually, with no joke aimed at the people. Doug doesn't wear the glove.
- **Facts that need strong sources (cut the claim if it can't be sourced):** platypus morphine resistance and pain duration; Schmidt index
  value and bullet ant pain duration; hornet alarm pheromone in venom and Japan death figures (with year and scope); Komodo venom glands
  vs. the bacteria theory and the later debate; scorpion global death estimate and UV glow; saw-scaled viper "most snakebite deaths"
  (exact hedge); blue-ringed octopus tetrodotoxin, painless bite and "aware but paralysed"; black mamba mouth-colour naming, speed and
  pre-antivenom fatality; funnel-web primate sensitivity, male danger, the 1981 antivenom and the 15-minute record; inland taipan LD50 ranking
  and "no confirmed deaths"; Irukandji delay, "impending doom" and the 2002 deaths; box jellyfish tentacle numbers, time to death, 24 eyes
  and swimming. Every size and count is "about" or a range.
- **Myths to avoid:** "a platypus can kill a dog" (unsourced), "a mamba outruns a horse", "Komodo bacteria" stated as current fact,
  "one inland taipan bite can kill 100 men" (cut unless a primary source states it), "daddy longlegs is the most venomous spider".
- **Duplication:** don't reuse quack doc's, Simple Paint's or any "most venomous animals" list's wording or order. Our twists (painkillers
  don't work, the sting tags you, the textbook was wrong, the glow, the smallest snake kills the most, it's not black, a primate problem,
  the most toxic snake never killed anyone, impending doom is a symptom, it can see) carry the video.
