# Why the Tsavo Lions Hunted Railway Workers for Nine Months (draft 2)

[Opening frame: the thumbnail. Night, a lantern-lit canvas tent seen from outside. Two big maneless lion heads push through the tent flap, eyes lit. Tiny Doug in a camp bed inside, blanket to his chin, looking at the camera. Text: NO MANES. Hold 1.5 to 4 seconds.]

[Cut to a dusty railway line at sunset. Two lean, maneless lion silhouettes on the embankment. DOUG DEATHS: 430. Beside it, a crude safety board: DAYS WITHOUT AN INCIDENT: 0.]

Two lions stopped a Kenyan railway in eighteen ninety-eight. The man who shot them said they killed a hundred and thirty-five people.

By the end, Doug is up a tree with the second lion.

## The Man-Eaters of Tsavo

The Man-Eaters of Tsavo.

[A crude map strip: MOMBASA on the coast, a dotted line inland, a red dot at TSAVO, the line running on toward LAKE VICTORIA.]

In March eighteen ninety-eight, an engineer named John Henry Patterson arrived at the Tsavo River to build a railway bridge, and within days he heard that two lions were hunting near the camp.

[A thick book labelled "1907". Beside it, a thin museum leaflet labelled "1925". The number 28 on the book, 135 on the leaflet.]

In his book, Patterson counted twenty-eight Indian workers, plus scores of Africans that nobody officially recorded, and years later his number grew to a hundred and thirty-five.

Here's the twist, because in two thousand nine, scientists measured the chemistry locked inside the lions' own hair and bones, to estimate how many people they had actually eaten in their final months.

[Two lion icons. Label "ABOUT 11" under one, "ABOUT 24" under the other.]

One lion ate about eleven people, and the other ate about twenty-four. That makes about thirty-five. The study could only count the people the lions ate, not everyone they killed, but it is still about a quarter of his number.

It was still about thirty-five real people, and the railway kept going anyway.

[A long row of plain stick-figure workers and tents beside the line. Label "THOUSANDS".]

The line was the Uganda Railway, a British colonial project from the port of Mombasa to Lake Victoria. Patterson wrote that some thousands of workers were camped at Tsavo, many of them brought over from British India.

The whole line ran more than nine hundred kilometers, about the distance from Land's End to John o' Groats.

[Doug arrives with a lunchbox and a clipboard, and pitches a small tent at the very edge of camp, facing the bush. DAYS WITHOUT AN INCIDENT: 1.]

Doug starts work on day one with a lunchbox, a clipboard, and a tent pitched at the very edge of camp, because the view is nicer from there. Doug is fine. Doug has a view.

The lions, unfortunately, already know exactly where the edge of camp is.

Week three.

## The Tent

The Tent.

[Night. Rows of canvas tents, small campfires between them. Doug's tent in the foreground, lantern glowing inside.]

Patterson wrote that the first attack he could not explain away was on a foreman named Ungan Singh, about three weeks after he arrived. The foreman shared his tent with half a dozen other workers, and one night, a lion put its head in at the open door and seized him.

[A big lean lion silhouette walking between the tents, past a fire. Label "190 KG".]

These lions walked straight into a camp full of people, at night, and they kept doing it. A grown male lion weighs about a hundred and ninety kilograms on average, and it walks on soft, silent pads.

Picture two fridge-freezers strolling past your tent in total silence, and neither of them is looking for milk.

Here's the problem: a tent is really just a wall made of cloth. Patterson later described a lion jumping onto one tent and breaking straight through it, and another lion making off with the mattress a man was lying on.

[A lion silhouette trots away with a striped mattress. A stick figure sits on the bare ground where it was, blinking. Red "?" stamp.]

The man survived that one, but the mattress never came back.

[The narrator's hand holds up an empty costume hanger. Puts it away.]

Normally, the narrator would dress Doug up as whatever the animal likes to eat, to save everyone some time. There is no costume today, because these lions did not need a reason.

[Doug inside his tent zips the flap shut and gives the zip a thumbs up. The lantern light shows a lion head silhouette pressing against the canvas. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 431. DAYS WITHOUT AN INCIDENT: 0. The cap hangs on the tent pole.]

Doug zipped the tent shut and gave the zip a confident thumbs up. Doug has died. Again.

The cap is still hanging on the tent pole, and the camp has started building walls.

Months in.

## The Thorn Fence

The Thorn Fence.

[A camp ringed by a high, spiky thorn-bush fence. Fires inside. A watchman bangs a row of empty oil tins hung from a tree. Small "CLANG" text.]

Soon every workers' camp was surrounded by a fence of cut thorn bush, which Patterson called a boma, and inside those fences, fires were kept burning all night long.

On top of that, someone on watch kept clattering half a dozen empty oil tins hung from a tree, which is the eighteen ninety-eight version of an alarm system.

On paper, it is a good plan, with thorns, fire and noise, all night, every night.

[Red arrow over the fence. Label "THORNS".]

The worst part is that it barely slowed them down. Patterson wrote that the lions still managed to jump over the fences or simply break through them.

A thorn hedge against a lion is a garden hedge against a delivery van. It really only matters to whoever is standing inside it.

[Close-up of the fence. A gap appears, as if pushed open from outside. Red "?" stamp.]

So the fences got thicker, and the fires got bigger, and the lions kept getting in. One evening, workmen even saw a lion stalking the camp doctor as he walked back from the hospital at dusk.

[Doug proudly finishes the thorniest fence in Kenya, then steps back into it and is stuck, arms and legs out. He gets free with a small white ankle bandage. The cap sits on top of the fence. DAYS WITHOUT AN INCIDENT: 1.]

Doug builds the thorniest fence in Kenya, admires it, and then backs straight into it. Doug is fine. The fence has caught exactly one thing, and it is Doug.

He has a bandage now, and what he would really like is a description of what he is keeping out.

## No Manes

No Manes.

[A plain wanted-poster sketch pinned to a post: a big fluffy-maned lion, labelled "LION". Beside it, the two real Tsavo lions, lean, short-haired, almost bald around the neck.]

Both of the Tsavo man-eaters were full-grown males, and neither of them had much of a mane at all.

Most people picture a male lion wearing a big, dark collar of hair. These two looked more like very large lionesses with the shoulders of a bull.

The strange part is that this is completely normal in Tsavo, where many male lions have thin manes, or almost none.

[A thermometer climbing. Dry thorn scrub. Label "HOT".]

In two thousand two, two zoologists, Roland Kays and Bruce Patterson, tested an old idea that a male's mane grows with the size of his pride. In Tsavo, the prides were just as big as anywhere else.

Their more likely answer was the place itself, because Tsavo is hot, dry and full of thorn scrub, so a big mane may cost a lion far more than it is worth.

Think of wearing a thick wool scarf through a thorn bush, at noon, every single day of your life.

[Doug on night watch at the camp gate with a lantern, holding the fluffy lion sketch. Two maneless lions stroll past. Doug checks the sketch, checks them, and waves them through. Cut away. DOUG DEATHS: 432. DAYS WITHOUT AN INCIDENT: 0.]

Doug is on night watch, holding a sketch of a lion. Two very different lions stroll past, and Doug politely waves them through. Doug has died.

He was looking for the wrong face entirely, and the next clue is hiding in the teeth.

## The Broken Tooth

The Broken Tooth.

[A lion skull drawn clean and simple. Red circle around one broken lower canine. Label "BROKEN".]

One of the Tsavo lions had a badly broken canine tooth, with an infection at its root.

When researchers described the damage in two thousand, they suggested that an injury like that could make big, struggling prey very hard to kill.

Call it the toothache theory.

[A clipboard titled "WHY?". Lines appear one by one: "BAD TOOTH", "NO BUFFALO", "THE OLD ROUTE".]

It is only one theory of several, though. A cattle disease called rinderpest had swept through the region in the early eighteen nineties, killing huge numbers of buffalo, a favourite prey of lions in Tsavo.

The railway also followed an old caravan route, where sick and dying porters were sometimes left behind. Some researchers think the local lions may have learned there that people were food.

But that's not the disturbing part. In twenty seventeen, scientists studied the tiny scratches on the lions' teeth, because starving lions crunch bones to get every last scrap of food, and these two hadn't been crunching anything.

[Red X stamp over a bone icon. Label "NO BONES".]

Their wear looked strikingly like the teeth of zoo lions, which are fed soft meat. People seem to have been added to an already varied diet, not eaten as a last resort.

[Doug in a white coat with a tiny dental mirror and a sign "FREE CHECK-UP". He leans toward a lion. Speech bubble: "hi friend?". Doug: "open wide". Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 433.]

Doug opens a free dental clinic in the middle of the camp and asks his first patient to open wide. Doug has died.

The lions were not desperate, just choosy, and the workers had run out of patience.

December.

## The Walkout

The Walkout.

[A steam train on the line. A crowd of plain stick-figure workers stands across the rails in front of the engine.]

On the first of December, the workers stopped work. Patterson wrote that some hundreds of them halted the first passing train by throwing themselves onto the rails in front of the engine.

[The train leaves, packed with people. Label "LEAVING".]

Then they swarmed onto the trucks with their belongings and left. For the next three weeks, Patterson wrote, practically nothing was built except lion-proof shelters.

The men who stayed slept wherever a lion could not easily reach. Beds were lashed into every good-sized tree in camp, and others were perched on top of water tanks, roofs and girders.

[Trees full of camp beds. A water tank with beds on top.]

What nobody expected was how far the story would travel. It reached London, where the Prime Minister, Lord Salisbury, told the House of Lords that lions had stopped the railway.

Picture an office where everyone leaves at the same moment, and you are still at your desk, holding a shovel.

Patterson could not leave, because the bridge was his job, and a bridge does not get built while the whole workforce is either on a train or up a tree.

[Doug alone in the empty camp, holding a shovel, waving at the departing train. The cap is on the last carriage. A paw reaches in and wipes the safety board. DAYS WITHOUT AN INCIDENT: 0. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 434.]

Doug is the last worker in camp, waving goodbye to the train with his shovel. Doug has died. Again.

The cap left on the train, but Patterson stayed, and he still had one trick left: a room built for lions.

## The Trap

The Trap.

[A heavy cage of railway sleepers and iron rails, split into two compartments by a wall of bars. A lamp burns in the small side.]

Patterson built a lion trap with two rooms, one for the men and one for the lion. A wall of iron rails divided them, only three inches apart, which is about seven and a half centimeters.

[Label "BAIT" over the small compartment. Two plain stick figures sit inside with a lamp.]

The bait was people, because two soldiers sat in the men's room with rifles and a burning lamp, under orders to shoot the moment a lion stepped through the door.

It is like waiting in a bus shelter, at night, after someone has promised you that it is lion-proof.

On the third of December, at around nine o'clock at night, the door clattered down, and a lion was inside, throwing itself against the bars.

Here's the catch: Patterson wrote that at first the two men were too frightened to fire, which, locked in a box with a lion, seems fair.

[Off-screen: cartoon "BANG" text, many times, scattered across the frame. Nothing is aimed at anything on screen.]

Once an officer nearby shouted at them to shoot, they fired more than twenty shots in every direction. Mostly, they hit one bar of the door, and the lion walked out through the gap.

[Doug sits in the men's room behind the bars, lunchbox on his knees, eyes shut. When he opens them, the lion is gone. The lunchbox has one neat hole in it. DAYS WITHOUT AN INCIDENT: 1. The field guide page TSAVO LIONS (x2), FRIEND?: "MAYBE".]

The narrator volunteered Doug as the third man in the cage. Doug did not agree to this. The only casualty of the night was his lunchbox.

[The narrator's hand slowly lowers a pre-drawn DOUG DEATHS: 435 sticker back into a drawer.]

Doug survived the whole thing, and the narrator is quietly disappointed. Patterson decided the only way to win was to go up.

## The Platform

The Platform.

[Night. A rickety platform on four thin poles. A plain stick figure with a hat sits on it. Doug sits beside him. Nothing is pointed at anything.]

On the night of the ninth of December, Patterson climbed onto a machan, a wooden platform on four poles, about twelve feet up, or roughly three and a half meters.

Below it lay a donkey the lion had already killed, wired to a stump so that it could not be carried away.

[The platform from below. Tiny label "FLIMSY".]

Patterson called it his crazy structure, and he worried that one of the rather flimsy poles might snap.

Picture a wobbly stepladder in total darkness, except that the ladder also has a lion underneath it.

Here's the disturbing part: the lion did not go for the donkey at all. It went for Patterson.

[Two glowing eyes circle the base of the platform in the dark. Dotted circle path. Label "2 HOURS".]

For about two hours, Patterson wrote, it crept slowly round and round the platform in the dark, while he sat above it, waiting for a clear shot.

He shot it later that night, and by his own measurement, it was nearly three meters long from nose to tail, and well over a meter tall at the shoulder.

[Doug on the platform, gripping the edge, holding very still. The platform creaks. Small "CREAK" text. Nothing happens. DAYS WITHOUT AN INCIDENT: 2.]

Doug spends the same two hours on the platform, holding perfectly still. For once in his life, it works, and Doug survives.

The whole camp celebrated that night, which was a little early. There were two lions.

## The Last Lion

The Last Lion.

[A darker night than any before. Tall dry grass. One thin tree. Two lit eyes in the grass.]

The second lion came back on the night of the twenty-seventh of December. On the morning of the twenty-ninth, twenty days after the first one died, Patterson tracked it into the bush.

The lion charged, and Patterson and his gun-bearer got out of its way by climbing a tree.

[Close-up: a thin tree. Two tiny stick figures up it, among the branches. Label "UP".]

The most disturbing part is how long it kept coming. By Patterson's account, it was hit again and again, and it still kept charging.

It is like a car that keeps driving after the brakes, the engine and two of the wheels have already gone.

Patterson wrote that it finally dropped barely five yards from him. Work started again, and he later watched the first train cross the finished bridge.

[Night again. Doug alone at the very top of a thin tree, hugging the trunk. Below, the second maneless lion sits and looks up. It doesn't leave. The field guide page gets a second red "NO".]

Doug is up a thin tree in the dark, with the second lion sitting right underneath it. The lion does not climb, because it doesn't need to. It waits.

[The safety board is wiped to 0, wobbles, and falls flat on its face. The lion's eyes stay lit. Cut to black on the eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 435.]

Doug has died.

[Closing: a quiet museum hall. Two maneless lions in a glass case, mounted. The red cap lies on the floor of the case. A small museum label beside it.]

For more than twenty-five years, the two lions were Patterson's floor rugs. In the nineteen-twenties, he sold them to the Field Museum in Chicago for five thousand dollars, and they are still on display there.

In twenty twenty-four, scientists pulled old hair out of the lions' broken teeth and read its DNA: giraffe, human, oryx, waterbuck, wildebeest and zebra. They found just one buffalo hair, and no buffalo DNA at all.

The lions are finally behind glass, and so is the cap, and nobody is checking their teeth.

Doug deaths: four hundred and thirty-five. Somewhere, a new red cap is already on order. Subscribe before it hits four thirty-six.
