## Mantis Shrimp

Mantis Shrimp.

This rainbow-colored crustacean lives in a burrow on tropical coral reefs, and where you would expect claws, it keeps a pair of folded clubs.

[A peacock mantis shrimp peeks out of a burrow, clubs folded under its chin. Doug-in-snail inches past.]

Every predator on this list has a favorite kind of prey, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a snail.

[Label on the shrimp's club: "23 m/s". A small speedometer next to it hits "80 km/h".]

The clubs are specialized for breaking shells, and biologists at Berkeley filmed the strike with a high-speed camera and clocked the club at twenty-three meters per second. That's more than eighty kilometers an hour, highway speed, underwater.

Here's the twist. It hits twice.

[Slow-motion panel: the club swings, a silver bubble forms between the club and the shell.]

The club moves so fast that the pressure in the water in front of it drops, and the water briefly boils into a bubble. A fraction of a millisecond later, that bubble collapses with a tiny flash of light, a burst of heat and a second blow. The process is called cavitation, and it is powerful enough to gradually damage hard surfaces.

[Force graph with two spikes. Label: "1. CLUB", "2. BUBBLE".]

When scientists measured strikes against snail shells, every hit showed two separate spikes of force, less than half a millisecond apart. The first spike is the club, and the second is the bubble, which sometimes hits even harder than the club does.

[Cutaway of the shrimp's arm: a saddle-shaped spring loading up like a catapult.]

The power comes from a saddle-shaped spring in the arm, which the shrimp loads up and then releases all at once. In captivity, these animals have been known to break the glass walls of their own tanks.

[The club swings at Doug. Doug ducks. The club misses. Doug looks up and gives a small, hopeful smile. Speech bubble: "missed!"]

The club swings, Doug ducks, and it misses him completely. He looks up with a small, hopeful smile.

[The bubble pops. The snail shell cracks down the middle, cartoon style. Doug lies flat, X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 66]

Then the bubble pops.

Doug has died. Again. He dodged the punch, which, it turns out, is only half of a mantis shrimp.

## Giant Moray

Giant Moray.

The giant moray can grow to approximately three meters long, longer than a bed, and it spends its resting hours tucked inside cracks in the reef.

[A long spotted moray in a coral crack. A bed drawn next to it for scale, shorter than the eel.]

Doug is dressed as a small reef fish today, which is exactly the kind of animal morays hunt.

[Doug in a small striped fish costume, red cap on top, hovering near the coral.]

Most fish swallow by sucking prey into their mouths with a quick gulp of water. A moray's head is long and narrow, which makes it terrible at that, so it has an alternative.

[X-ray cutaway of the moray's head: a second, smaller set of jaws sitting in the throat.]

It has a second set of jaws, called pharyngeal jaws, hidden in its throat. When the moray bites down on something, those throat jaws shoot forward into its mouth, grab the prey and haul it back toward the stomach. Biologists in California filmed the whole thing at high speed and published it in two thousand seven.

[Diagram: the throat jaws slide forward and back, drawn like a tape measure snapping back into its case.]

But that's not the disturbing part. The moray has a partner.

[Red Sea label. A big red-spotted grouper swims up to the moray's crack and shakes its head. Motion lines.]

In the Red Sea, a fish called the roving coral grouper swims up to a resting moray and shakes its head at it, three to six times a second. More than half the time, the moray comes out, and the two of them go hunting together across the reef.

When a small fish escapes into a crack the grouper can't reach, the grouper does a headstand above the hiding spot and shakes its head again, which brings the moray straight to the fish.

[Doug-in-fish swims alongside the grouper, both smiling. Speech bubble from Doug: "best friend".]

Doug meets the grouper first, and it's incredibly friendly, and it follows him everywhere. When things get scary, Doug hides in a narrow crack, and his new friend floats over and does a headstand directly above him.

[The grouper upside down over Doug's crack, shaking its head. The moray's face appears at the other end of the crack. Cut to the empty crack, the flattened fish costume, X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 67]

Doug has died. Again. His best friend on the reef was the informant.

## Giant Pacific Octopus

Giant Pacific Octopus.

The giant Pacific octopus is the largest octopus species known, and from the tip of one arm to the tip of the opposite one, it can reach about five meters.

[A huge red octopus spread across the screen. A parking space painted beneath it for scale, the octopus just as long.]

That's about the length of a parking space, on an animal with absolutely no skeleton.

[Doug in a crab costume, red cap on top, scuttling sideways across cold green rocks.]

Doug is dressed as a crab, because crabs are near the top of its shopping list, along with clams, scallops, shrimp and fish.

[Diagram: the octopus body with one small hard dot circled. Label: "BEAK".]

The only hard part of its body is its beak, which means that anywhere the beak fits, the rest of the octopus can follow. The Alaska Department of Fish and Game says a twenty-two kilogram octopus can squeeze through a hole about five centimeters wide, which is smaller than a tennis ball.

[The octopus pours itself through a hole next to a tennis ball for comparison.]

It's also remarkably intelligent. In aquariums and labs, giant Pacific octopuses solve mazes, open childproof bottles, use shells and stones as tools, recognize individual keepers and unscrew jar lids to get at the food inside.

[Doug-in-crab climbs into a big glass jar and screws the lid on from the inside. Speech bubble: "safe".]

So Doug climbs into a glass jar and screws the lid on tight from the inside, where nothing can possibly get in.

[One arm wraps around the jar. The lid turns. Cut to the lid on the rocks, the empty crab costume flat beside it, X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 68]

Doug has died. Again. He did the packaging himself.

Here's the problem, and it isn't Doug's anymore. This octopus eats sharks.

[A small grey shark labeled "SPINY DOGFISH" with an octopus arm curling around it. Monitor-style frame: "AQUARIUM CAMERA".]

The Monterey Bay Aquarium says giant Pacific octopuses eat sharks when the chance comes up. At the Seattle Aquarium, one was caught on camera taking a spiny dogfish, a small shark, inside its tank.

An animal with no bones eats sharks, and we are only on step three.

## Giant Trevally

Giant Trevally.

The giant trevally is a big silver fish that can weigh up to eighty kilograms, about the weight of a grown man, and it often ranks higher on the food chain than many sharks.

[A huge silver fish with a blunt head and a scowl. A stick-figure man on a bathroom scale next to it, same weight.]

It normally eats fish, squid and crustaceans, but Doug is dressed as a seabird.

[Doug in a lumpy sooty tern costume, wings too big, red cap on top. He stands on a tiny sandy island.]

He is a sooty tern, which makes no sense for a fish until you visit Farquhar Atoll in the Seychelles.

[Map pin on a speck of sand. Label: "ILE AUX GOELETTES, FARQUHAR ATOLL".]

On one tiny island there, about two hundred thirty thousand pairs of sooty terns nest every year, and their chicks learn to fly over the lagoon in August and September. Some land on the water, and the trevally take those from the surface.

Now for the impossible bit. Some birds are still flying.

[A trevally explodes out of the water, mouth open, toward a tern flying low over the waves. Splash lines.]

In September two thousand twenty-two, researchers watched the island from a lookout for sixteen days and recorded two hundred thirty-nine encounters between giant trevally and seabirds. Most birds were taken off the water, but at least twenty were taken in flight, by fish launching themselves clear out of the lagoon.

This has only ever been recorded at this one island. The researchers suspect that so many trevally gather there that some of them learned to hunt the sky to beat the competition, even though most of their leaps miss.

[Doug flaps desperately. The costume is so badly made that, somehow, he rises far higher than the real birds. A trevally leaps below him, misses, and takes the tern flying next to him. Doug looks down. Speech bubble: "wait what".]

Doug flaps as hard as he can, and the costume is so heavy and badly made that, somehow, it sends him higher than every real bird in the sky. A trevally leaps, misses Doug, and takes the tern next to him instead.

Somewhere in the Indian Ocean, a population of fish has decided that the sky is also part of the sea.

## Goliath Grouper

Goliath Grouper.

The goliath grouper is the biggest grouper in the Atlantic. It can grow more than two meters long and weigh up to around three hundred sixty kilograms, which is heavier than a baby grand piano.

[A massive mottled brown fish hovering over a wreck. A baby grand piano next to it, smaller.]

[Doug in a cheap grey shark costume, fin on top, red cap tucked under the fin. He swims with his chest out. Speech bubble: "fear me".]

Today Doug is dressed as a shark, and for the first time in this video, he feels dangerous.

[Diagram: the grouper's mouth opens, arrows of water rushing inward. Label: "SWALLOWED WHOLE".]

Goliath groupers don't really chew, because their teeth are mostly there to stop prey from escaping. The fish opens its huge mouth, water rushes in, and whatever was in front of it is swallowed whole. The Florida Museum lists lobsters, crabs, fish, octopus and young sea turtles on the menu.

[The grouper flares its mouth and quivers. Sound-wave lines and a label: "BOOM".]

When something bothers it, the grouper opens its mouth, quivers its whole body and makes a low rumbling boom with its swim bladder. That sound travels great distances underwater, and it also helps goliath groupers locate each other.

The worst part is that the menu also includes sharks.

[A fishing line runs from a boat down to a small blacktip shark. Below it, a huge shadow rises.]

In two thousand fourteen, off Bonita Springs, Florida, a fisherman was reeling in a blacktip shark about one point two meters long. Then a goliath grouper rose up from below and took the whole shark in a single bite. The fisherman kept the video. He did not keep the shark.

[Doug-in-shark hovers above the wreck, posing. Low boom. Cut to the grouper, cheeks bulging, the shark fin sticking out of its mouth for a second. The red cap drifts down. DOUG DEATHS: 69]

Doug is cruising over the wreck in his shark costume, feeling powerful, when he hears a low boom somewhere below him.

Doug has died. Again. Dressing as a shark only helps if nothing down there eats sharks.

## Tiger Shark

Tiger Shark.

Tiger sharks commonly grow three to four meters long, about the length of a small car, and they are famous for eating almost anything.

[A striped tiger shark cruising over seagrass. A small car outline next to it, same length.]

[Doug in a sea turtle costume, shell on his back, red cap on top, paddling over seagrass.]

Doug is dressed as a sea turtle, which is a fairly regular meal for a tiger shark.

[Menu board, chalk style: "SEA TURTLES, RAYS, OTHER SHARKS, FISH, SEABIRDS, DOLPHINS, SQUID, CRABS, CARRION".]

According to the Florida Museum, its menu commonly includes sea turtles, rays, other sharks, bony fish, seabirds, dolphins, squid, crustaceans and dead animals. In Shark Bay, Western Australia, where researchers have tagged or captured more than six hundred tiger sharks since the late nineteen nineties, sea turtles and dugongs are among their favorite prey.

[Map of Hawaii, a pin on a small atoll. Label: "FRENCH FRIGATE SHOALS". Albatross chicks wobbling on the beach.]

They also keep a calendar. At French Frigate Shoals, an atoll in the northwestern Hawaiian Islands, young albatross learn to fly every summer, and some of them end up on the water. Tracking studies found tiger sharks arriving at the atoll in that season to feed on them, then swimming off thousands of kilometers and coming back in later years.

Here's the strange part. It doesn't stop at food.

[An X-ray of a shark stomach. Inside: two cans, a plastic bottle, two sacks, a squid, a fish. Price tags on everything.]

One tiger shark caught in the Red Sea had two empty cans, a plastic bottle, two burlap sacks, a squid and a fish in its stomach. That's less of a diet and more of a garage sale.

[Doug-in-turtle paddles bravely. The shark circles. Doug looks at the camera.]

In our last ocean episode, sharks never hurt Doug. That streak is over.

## Saltwater Crocodile

Saltwater Crocodile.

The saltwater crocodile is the largest living reptile on Earth, and big males can grow past six meters long and weigh more than one thousand kilograms.

[A huge crocodile on a mudbank. Doug, no costume, stands next to it for scale and comes up to its eye.]

Doug doesn't get a costume this time. Saltwater crocodiles don't need one.

[A gauge labeled "BITE FORCE" maxed out. A family car drawn pressing down on a set of teeth.]

In two thousand twelve, scientists measured bite force across every living species of crocodile, alligator and their relatives, and a big saltwater crocodile came out on top, at around sixteen thousand four hundred newtons. That's roughly the weight of a family car, pressing down through its teeth.

Here's the problem with standing on the beach. Saltwater crocodiles travel by sea.

[Map of northern Australia. A croc icon rides a current arrow down the coast of Cape York. A road sign: "LOS ANGELES TO SAN FRANCISCO".]

They don't swim hard, they surf. Researchers tagged twenty-seven adult crocodiles in Queensland's Kennedy River and found they only set off on long trips when the tide was going their way, then hauled out on the bank when it turned. Satellite positions from crocodiles traveling in the open ocean lined up with the direction of the surface currents.

One male traveled five hundred ninety kilometers in twenty-five days, roughly the driving distance from Los Angeles to San Francisco. Another waited four days in a sheltered bay for the current to switch before it crossed the Torres Strait.

[A crocodile with a shark in its jaws, drawn simply, no detail. Label: "BULL SHARK, 2014".]

Sharks are on the crocodile's menu as well. In two thousand fourteen, tourists on a river cruise in northern Australia photographed a big saltwater crocodile catching and eating a bull shark.

[Doug on the sand, well back from the waterline, holding a tide table and a sign: "SAFE". Speech bubble: "safe".]

Doug stands on the sand, well back from the water, holding a tide table that he has carefully checked twice. The tide is going out.

[A crocodile surfs in on a wave, perfectly calm. Cartoon splash. The tide table floats. The cap sits on the sand. DOUG DEATHS: 71]

Doug has died. Again. The crocodile had checked the tide table too.

## Polar Bear

Polar Bear.

Under United States law, the polar bear counts as a marine mammal, alongside whales, dolphins and seals, because it spends most of its life on the sea ice.

[White sea ice, blue-black water. A polar bear walks into frame, then lies flat beside a small round hole.]

Doug is dressed as a seal, because seals are what polar bears eat, mostly ringed seals and bearded seals.

[Doug in a grey seal costume, whiskers, red cap on top, underwater below the ice.]

Seals breathe through openings in the ice, so the bear picks a hole, lies down beside it and stops moving completely. This usually takes less than an hour, but a polar bear will wait much longer if it has to. When a seal eventually comes up for air, the bear grabs it and flips it out onto the ice.

[A clock above the bear. The hands spin. The bear does not move.]

The disturbing part is how far a polar bear will go when the ice runs out.

[Map of the Beaufort Sea. A dotted line runs from Alaska far out to the pack ice. Sixteen tiny marathon finish banners line the route.]

In two thousand eight, government scientists tracked a female polar bear wearing a radio collar in the Beaufort Sea, north of Alaska. She swam for nine days without stopping and covered six hundred eighty-seven kilometers in near-freezing water, which is more than sixteen marathons, back to back.

Afterwards she kept going, swimming and walking across the sea ice for roughly another eighteen hundred kilometers. By the time scientists caught up with her again, she had lost twenty-two percent of her body weight, and the researchers concluded that long-distance swimming may carry a serious energetic cost.

[Doug-in-seal pops his head up through a breathing hole. A large white shape is lying beside it, very still. Doug waves. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug pops up through a breathing hole in his seal costume. There's a large white shape lying right next to it, very still, and Doug waves at it.

[Cut to the hole. The red cap spins slowly on the ice beside it. The seal costume is flat next to it, X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 72]

Doug has died. Again. An animal that will swim for nine days straight was never going to let a free seal go home.

## Great White Shark

Great White Shark.

Everyone recognizes the great white shark, but fewer people realize that some of them hunt by leaving the water entirely.

[False Bay, South Africa. A rocky island crowded with seals. Label: "SEAL ISLAND".]

[Doug in the seal costume, now covered in patches and tape, red cap on top, sitting in a rubber ring.]

Doug is back in the seal costume, which has been patched.

[Cross-section of the water: a shark low and dark beneath a seal at the surface. A six-story building drawn beside the water column for scale. An arrow shoots straight up.]

At Seal Island in South Africa, great whites hunt young Cape fur seals at the surface, especially lone ones swimming back to the island. The shark starts deep, sometimes as deep as twenty meters, which is about the height of a six-story building.

Then it rushes upward for about seven to sixteen seconds, speeding up as much as sixfold, and hits the seal so fast that both of them leave the ocean. Most attacks are finished in less than a minute.

They hunt best in the low light just after sunrise, when more than half of their attacks succeed, and as the sun climbs higher, their success rate falls and they stop hunting.

Averaged over every attack, it's closer to a coin toss. When a seal realizes it's being chased, it zigzags, or does a headstand underwater to look for the shark.

How do scientists know all this? Partly by cheating. Along with more than two thousand real attacks, they recorded over a hundred strikes on fake seals, seal-shaped decoys towed behind a boat.

[A boat towing Doug-in-seal on a rubber ring at a steady speed. Label on Doug: "DECOY".]

Doug is towed behind a research boat on a rubber ring, at exactly the right speed, and without meaning to, he has become a decoy.

[A great white launches out of the water. Doug and the rubber ring fly high into the sky. A pause. The red cap lands on the water a few seconds later. DOUG DEATHS: 73]

Doug has died. Again. The cap lands a few seconds after him.

[Map of California, a pin on the Farallon Islands. A white shark icon zooms off the edge of the map. An orca fin appears.]

And yet, off California's Farallon Islands, great whites leave within minutes when orcas arrive, and stay away for the rest of the season.

## Sperm Whale

Sperm Whale.

The sperm whale is the largest predator with teeth on the planet, and big males reach fifteen to eighteen meters long and weigh more than thirty tonnes.

[A sperm whale, huge square head, sinking into the dark. Six Eiffel Towers stacked beside it, the whale still below the lowest one.]

It hunts more than two kilometers down, which is deeper than six Eiffel Towers stacked on top of each other.

[Doug in the squid costume, tentacles dangling, red cap on top, drifting in black water.]

Doug is dressed as a squid, which makes up most of a sperm whale's diet.

[Total darkness. Small white arcs radiate from the whale's head. Label: "CLICK".]

There is no light at that depth, so it hunts by sound, sending out clicks and listening for the echoes. Scientists measured those clicks at up to two hundred thirty-six decibels, one of the loudest sounds ever recorded from an animal, and the clicks are concentrated forward, like the beam of a flashlight.

Underwater decibels use a different scale, so you can't compare that number with a rock concert, but it is still extremely loud, and there is even some evidence the clicks can stun prey.

[Callback panel: a giant squid with a worried face, and a tiny label "SEE EPISODE 1".]

Its menu includes giant squid, and Doug has met the giant squid. It was the squid that should have worried.

Here's the twist. Even this enormous animal has a defensive strategy.

[Seven sperm whales in a ring, heads together in the middle, tails pointing out like the petals of a daisy. A calf in the center. Label: "MARGUERITE".]

When killer whales attack, a group of sperm whales can form a circle with heads together and tails pointing out, keeping calves and injured whales in the middle. Biologists call it a marguerite, after the daisy it looks like from above.

[Doug-in-squid drifts in the dark, eyes wide. He can't see a thing. A white click arc rolls toward him.]

Doug drifts in total darkness and can't see anything, but something can detect him perfectly. The click arrives first.

[Cartoon shockwave. The cap blows off and tumbles upward. The squid costume goes limp, X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 74]

Doug has died. Again. The largest predator with teeth on Earth has a defense formation, and the animal that makes it necessary is next on this list.

## Orca

Orca.

The orca, or killer whale, is the largest member of the dolphin family, and it can grow to almost ten meters long and weigh around ten tonnes.

[A big male orca with a tall dorsal fin, black and white, filling the frame.]

United States government ocean scientists call it the ocean's top predator, and nothing on this list hunts it, so Doug doesn't get a costume. He gets a small sailing boat.

[Doug at the wheel of a tiny sailboat with a single sail, life jacket over his stick body, red cap on.]

[South Africa map. Two orcas with bent dorsal fins, labeled "PORT" and "STARBOARD". A shark icon with a red X over it.]

Off South Africa, two orcas known as Port and Starboard began killing great white sharks around two thousand seventeen. Eight sharks washed up near Gansbaai, and seven had had their livers removed.

[Western Australia map. A blue whale drawn next to two school buses parked end to end, the same length.]

Off Western Australia in two thousand nineteen, scientists watched a group of orcas hunt and kill an adult blue whale, the largest kind of animal that has ever lived. This one was about twenty-two meters long, close to two school buses end to end, and it was the first time anyone had documented orcas killing one. They saw it happen twice more by two thousand twenty-one.

[Map of Spain and Portugal. Small sailboats along the coast. One orca nudging a rudder.]

Doug's boat is off the coast of Spain and Portugal, where since two thousand twenty, about fifteen orcas, from a population of only around forty, have been approaching sailboats and going for the rudders. They push them, rotate them and sometimes break them off entirely, and several vessels have sunk. Some scientists believe it is essentially a game.

But this is the strangest part.

[Split screen: a shark with an X, a blue whale, a ring of sperm whales. In the middle, a stick-figure person with a big question mark.]

The animal that kills great whites, hunts blue whales and makes sperm whales form a circle has no record of ever killing a person in the wild. Not one.

[Three orcas surface around Doug's boat. Doug grips the wheel. The orcas take the rudder, spin it around, toss it to each other and swim off with it. Doug drifts, alive, holding a wheel that steers nothing. DOUG DEATHS: 74, unchanged.]

The orcas arrive and Doug grips the wheel, but they just take his rudder, play with it for a while and swim away with it. Doug drifts off, alive, steering nothing.

Doug survived the top of the food chain. We are as surprised as you are, and slightly less pleased.

[Cut to the surface ship's monitor: grey sea, a red "LIVE" tag in the corner. Far off, Doug's boat drifts. An orca surfaces near the camera with Doug's red cap sitting on its head.]

The live video feed picks up one more detail, an orca surfacing near the camera with something red on its head.

[Inset: an orca with a salmon on its head. Labels: "1987" and "2024".]

In nineteen eighty-seven, killer whales off the northwest coast of North America started carrying dead salmon on their heads for weeks, like hats, and then stopped. In two thousand twenty-four, a male in Puget Sound was photographed wearing one again.

[Back to the LIVE feed. The orca with the red cap rolls slowly and swims out of frame. Doug, far off, waves at it.]

Doug is fine. The cap has survived again, but it has a new owner.
