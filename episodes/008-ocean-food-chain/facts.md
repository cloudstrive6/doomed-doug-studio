## 1. Mantis Shrimp
- Peacock mantis shrimp is a "smasher" with hammer-like clubs used on hard-shelled prey such as crabs and snails; lives
  in burrows on tropical reefs. https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/mantis-shrimps.html (search snippet: smashers "bludgeon
  hard-shelled prey, including crabs, snails and molluscs"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odontodactylus_scyllarus
- Rainbow coloring: common knowledge (species name "peacock"); https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/invertebrates/facts/mantis-shrimp
- Strike filmed with a high-speed camera (borrowed from a BBC crew) by Berkeley biologists; peak speed 23 m/s; strikes on
  snail shells. Patek, Korff & Caldwell 2004, *Nature* 428: 819-820.
  https://newsarchive.berkeley.edu/news/media/releases/2004/04/21_shrimp.shtml (verified: "peak speeds of 23 meters per
  second" while crushing snail shells; "saddle-shaped structure in the striking appendage" stores and releases elastic
  energy); https://ib.berkeley.edu/labs/patek/shrimpMechanics/Pateketal2004Nature.pdf
  - Arithmetic: 23 m/s x 3.6 = 82.8 km/h, "more than eighty kilometers an hour", highway speed (common knowledge).
- Cavitation: the strike lowers the pressure in front of the club, the water forms vapour bubbles that collapse with
  heat, light (sonoluminescence) and sound, and a second force peak. Two force peaks per strike, 390-480 µs apart; the
  first is the limb impact, the second is the bubble collapse; cavitation forces may exceed limb impact forces by up to
  280%; cavitation damages hard surfaces. Patek & Caldwell 2005, *J. Exp. Biol.* 208: 3655-3664.
  https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/208/19/3655/15838 (verified abstract)
  https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/the-mantis-shrimp-has-the-worlds-fastest-punch (verified: water
  "boil[s]", bubbles collapse "unleashing tremendous amounts of energy")
- "In captivity, these animals have been known to break the glass walls of their own tanks." Berkeley release (verified,
  wording: "in captivity, these club-equipped stomatopods have been known to break the glass walls of their tank");
  National Geographic (verified: Tyson, April 1998, quarter-inch glass).

## 9. Great White Shark
- Seal Island, False Bay: white sharks target lone, incoming young-of-the-year Cape fur seals at or near the surface;
  2,088 natural predations (1997-2003) and 121 strikes on towed seal-shaped decoys recorded ("more than two thousand real
  attacks", "over a hundred strikes on decoys"; corrected in draft 3, draft 2 wrongly had 210); most attacks last less than one minute; success greatest (55%) within one hour of
  sunrise, falling with increasing light; sharks cease active predation when success drops to about 40%. Martin et al.
  2005, *J. Mar. Biol. Assoc. UK* 85: 1121-1135.
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-marine-biological-association-of-the-united-kingdom/article/abs/predatory-behaviour-of-white-sharks-carcharodon-carcharias-at-seal-island-south-africa/0B567A5205A431DC464BAF1686065371 (search snippet)
  Full text (verified, read in draft 3): https://sharkresearch.earth.miami.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/predatory_behaviour_of_white_sharks_carcharodon_carcharias_at_seal_island_south_africa.pdf
  - Same paper, full text: "Mean predatory success rate was 47.3%" across all 2,088 interactions → "over a whole day, it's
    closer to a coin toss" (contrasted with 55% in the first hour after sunrise).
  - Same paper, Methods: attacks were detected partly by seals "switching from directional porpoising to either zigzag
    evasive manoeuvres or head-stand subsurface scanning, with indications of a shark in pursuit" → "a seal that realizes
    it's being chased zigzags, or does a headstand underwater to look for the shark".
- Breaching begins at depths up to 20 m, with a 7-16 s ascent; swim speed increases up to 6.5-fold. Semmens et al. 2019,
  *Marine Biology* 166: 95. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00227-019-3542-0 (search snippet)
  - 20 m ≈ a six-story building at about 3.3 m per story (common knowledge).
- Farallon Islands: white sharks vacated Southeast Farallon Island within minutes of orca visits and did not return until
  the following season. Jorgensen et al. 2019, *Scientific Reports* 9: 6153.
  https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/04/190416085525.htm (verified); https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-39356-2

## 10. Sperm Whale
- Largest toothed predator / largest toothed whale; adult males 15-18 m, 32-42 tonnes ("more than thirty tonnes"); diet
  primarily squid including giant squid; "some evidence" clicks can stun prey.
  https://www.aquariumofpacific.org/onlinelearningcenter/species/sperm_whale (verified);
  https://www.livescience.com/sperm-whales (search snippet: "the world's largest toothed predator ... hunting giant squid

- Dives deeper than 2 km: Live Science (above); Aquarium of the Pacific gives 2,987 m (verified). Script: "more than two kilometers".

- Clicks: source levels up to 236 dB re 1 µPa (rms); highly directional (directionality index 27 dB → "concentrated

  https://www.marinebioacoustics.com/files/2003/Mohl_et_al_2003a.pdf (search snippet)

  https://dosits.org/science/sounds-in-the-sea/how-does-sound-in-air-differ-from-sound-in-water/ (search snippet)

- Marguerite / rosette defence against killer whales: heads together, tails out, calves or injured whales in the centre.
  Pitman et al. 2001, *Marine Mammal Science* 17: 494-507.
  https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/context/usdeptcommercepub/article/1506/viewcontent/Pitman_MMS_2001_Killer_whale_predation.pdf (search snippet:
  35 killer whales attacked nine sperm whales off California in 1997; the sperm whales' "main defensive behavior was the
  formation of a rosette ('marguerite' - heads together, tails out)"; killer whale predation "potentially ... an
  important, and underrated, selective factor" in sperm whale evolution);
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1748-7692.2001.tb01000.x
