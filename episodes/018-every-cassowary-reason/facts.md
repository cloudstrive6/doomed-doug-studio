## Thesis check (brief section 2, item 10): HOLDS, fallback not needed
The brief's thesis line, "both times, the person had come to the bird: once to attack it, once to keep it", is supported by sources:
- **1926:** National Geographic: a teenager in Australia died in 1926 "after hitting a cassowary with a bat" (fetched). Scientific American
  (Naish, Tetrapod Zoology): the victim had struck the bird intending to kill it, then fled, so it was not an unprovoked attack (fetched).
  Guinness: the victim was a hunter who tripped while fleeing from the bird in April 1926 (fetched). Kofron's case history (via search):
  "in the single fatal cassowary attack recorded in Australia the victim was trying to kill the cassowary".
- **2019:** National Geographic: a Florida man was killed in 2019 "by a cassowary he kept in captivity" (fetched). AP via ABC30, April 2019:
  the man was the bird's owner and "was apparently breeding the birds, state wildlife officials said"; Alachua County Fire Rescue
  deputy chief: "It looks like it was accidental", the man "was in the vicinity of the bird and at some point fell. When he fell, he was
  attacked" (fetched).
- So "once to keep it" is accurate (he kept and bred the birds on his property). The script attributes "breeding" to wildlife officials
  and "fell" to fire rescue officials. No names, no ages, no injury details are spoken.

## Overstated claims avoided (brief section 7, myths)
- No "disembowel", no injury talk, no bite or kick force numbers. The kick is described only as forward/downward and chest height (SciAm).
- "Most dangerous bird" is attributed to Guinness World Records and headlines, then undercut by the two-death record.
- Casque: "battering ram" appears only as the old idea being corrected (Naish & Perron review: bony core fragile, percussive origin unlikely; draft 2 wording).
- No "closest relative of Velociraptor" claim. Only the digit II comparison, plus "every bird is a dinosaur", is stated.
- The bird is never called venomous. The plum is a plant toxin. No symptoms, no dose, no statement that any fruit is safe for people.
- No safety instructions anywhere (no "don't run", no "never feed", no driving advice). The Road states conservation facts only.
- The First Pet: "may have", "the researchers suggest", "possibly the earliest", as the authors frame it.

## Cut or changed during research
- Brief: "down to about 23 Hz" for **southern** cassowaries. The Mack & Jones abstract gives 32 Hz for the southern cassowary (one wild
  recording) and 23 Hz for the **dwarf** cassowary (captive). Script states both, correctly attributed.
- Brief: sound produced "with the neck inflated or the body vibrating": not in any source I could read. Cut from narration (the stage
  direction "neck slightly puffed" is a drawing note only; the director may drop it).
- Brief: casque height against an everyday object: no sourced casque height in cm found. Replaced with the sourced 5 to 36 degree test
  range against body temperature.
- Brief: Naish & Perron "not well supported": the abstract (via search) rates the weapon idea extremely unlikely and the resonance-box idea
  most likely; Eastick et al. say anatomy argues against resonance. Script says only (draft 2): the review found the bony core fragile and judged the
  weapon idea unlikely, and could not settle its purpose (Eastick et al. cite Naish & Perron for "purpose remains unclear").
- Brief: cassowary plum "toxic to many animals, but cassowaries eat it": no reliable source for toxicity to other animals or for
  cassowary "immunity". Script keeps only: favourite cassowary food (hence the name) and toxic to people (NParks Flora & Fauna Web).
- Brief: "spread the seeds of many plants; some fruit too big for any other animal": kept with Westcott et al. 2005 (238 species) and the
  Wet Tropics Management Authority figure (more than 70 tree species), attributed.
- Brief: river width against a pool: no source for a particular river width. Replaced with body weight against bags of rice (arithmetic).
- Brief: speed analogy "racehorse/sprinter": replaced with the Queensland built-up-area default speed limit (50 km/h).
- Brief: The Quills "5-7 bare, spiny feather shafts whose function is unclear": the 2014 anatomy study counts **four** quills; no source
  states the function is unclear. Script says four quills and adds the sourced wing claw. No function claim.
- Brief: claw "same toe" as Deinonychus: kept. Fowler et al. 2011 (D-II claw in Deinonychus/dromaeosaurids) + SciAm (cassowary
  digit II claw). Not stated: that the cassowary claw is used like the raptor claw. "Held off the ground" clause cut (only weak sources).
- Brief: ratites (emu, ostrich, kiwi, moa): not used in the script (no need; not added).
- Brief: First Pet "older than the pyramids": cut (no strong source fetched). Kept "thousands of years before the chicken" (lead author's
  own words, Sci.News).
- 50 km/h has no primary field measurement that I found; it is the figure used by Sydney Zoo, National Geographic (31 mph) and the Library
  of Congress. Script says "usually reported at up to fifty kilometers per hour".
- Kofron's 150-attack study and "fed birds attack": not reused (brief, 011 overlap). The 12 cm claw (used in 011) appears only as part of
  a "ten to twelve centimeters" range with a new analogy (paring knife), as the brief allows.
