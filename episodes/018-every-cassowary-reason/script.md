## The Helmet

The Helmet.

[Side view of the cassowary's head. A red arrow to the tall crest. Label: "CASQUE".]

It isn't armour. It's a radiator.

## The Quills

The Quills.

## The First Pet

The First Pet.

[Near-dark forest. A rock shelter in the New Guinea highlands. A pile of eggshell fragments on the floor.]

You would think anyone who had met a cassowary would keep their distance. People in New Guinea, at least eighteen thousand years ago, seem to have done the opposite.

[Eggshell fragments under a magnifying glass. Label: "18,000 TO 6,000 YEARS OLD". A counter: "1,019 FRAGMENTS".]

In twenty twenty-one, a team led by researchers at Penn State studied more than a thousand cassowary eggshell fragments from two rock shelters in New Guinea. The shells were between eighteen thousand and six thousand years old.

The team worked out how far along each egg had been when someone collected it, and a large majority had been taken late, when the chicks inside were nearly ready to hatch. A good number of those late shells showed no burn marks at all.

[A crude stick figure in a rock shelter holding an egg up to the light. Later, the same figure walking with a small striped chick behind it.]

Cassowary chicks imprint on people readily and are easy to raise to full size, and they are still traded in New Guinea today. ## The Toe

The Toe.

[Close on a cassowary foot on the dark forest floor. Three toes. A red arrow to the long inner claw.]

Every bird alive today is a dinosaur. Birds sit inside the theropods, the group on the dinosaur family tree that includes the raptors. They are the only branch of it that made it through the extinction at the end of the age of dinosaurs. With most birds you would never guess, but the cassowary's feet make it a lot easier.

[The foot with each toe numbered. The inner toe is labelled "TOE 2". A paring knife lies next to the claw, about the same length.]

Each foot has just three toes, because the small first toe that most birds have is simply missing. The inner one of the three, which anatomists call digit two, carries a long, dagger-like claw. On a big bird it can reach about ten to twelve centimeters, roughly the length of a long paring-knife blade.

[A museum skeleton of Deinonychus. Its foot is enlarged. The second toe carries a big curved claw. Label: "TOE 2".]

Now look at the raptor dinosaurs, like Deinonychus. Their famous enlarged killing claw, the one in every museum hall and every dinosaur movie, was also on digit two.

Scientists still argue about how those raptors used theirs, and the old movie image of slashing is not the favourite answer. One study compared their feet to those of hawks and eagles, which hold prey down with an enlarged talon and use their body weight to pin it. That study suggested the raptor claw was mostly used the same way, to grip prey and keep it there.

Put the two feet side by side, and the unnerving part jumps out.

[The cassowary foot and the Deinonychus foot side by side. Both inner toes glow red.]

It's the same toe.

[Doug holds a plaster claw cast up next to the cassowary's foot to compare. Speech bubble: "same?". The cassowary looks down at the cast, then at Doug. Cut away to the treetops. A dust cloud rises from below. DOUG DEATHS: 153.]

Doug brought a plaster museum cast of a raptor claw, held it up against the cassowary's foot, and asked whether the two of them matched. The bird agreed, briefly.

The dinosaurs never really left. Some of them just gave up hunting and took up fruit.
