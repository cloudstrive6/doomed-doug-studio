The Snowplow.

The Record.

## The Jam

The Jam.

[Calendar: JUL. A long park road through a wide valley. A line of cars, all stopped. At the very front, a herd walking slowly down the middle of the road.]

In Yellowstone, a traffic jam often has a bison at the front of it, walking down the middle of the road at whatever speed it likes.

[Label: "HAYDEN VALLEY". The herd crosses in front of the first car, then turns and walks along the road instead.]

Herds walk along and across the park roads, especially in late summer, when the herds move into the Lamar and Hayden valleys.

[A tally board: "2018: 25 BISON". Small car icons beside it.]

The danger runs both ways. In twenty eighteen, park data counted twenty-five bison killed by vehicles on park roads, which was more than any other large animal that year. Drivers killed seventy-six large animals on park roads in total, so bison were about a third of them.

[A wall calendar with a small bison icon on every other Friday.]

Twenty-five in a year is about one bison every two weeks, as regular as a fortnightly paycheck.

[A stopped car in a jam. A bison walks up and bumps the car door with its head. The car rocks. Label: "AUGUST 2022".]

And in August twenty twenty-two, at the height of the mating season, a bison walked up to a car stuck in a jam and rammed it with its head. Nobody had even stepped outside.

[A road sign that just says "YIELD", with a bison painted on it.]

The part nobody puts on a sign is who has right of way. In Yellowstone it is the bison, and nobody gave it to them.

## The Wallow

The Wallow.

[Calendar: AUG. Hayden Valley, dry and gold. Dust clouds rise from the herd on the horizon.]

In August, dust clouds rise over the valleys, and the dust is coming from bison throwing themselves onto the ground.

[A bull rolls onto its back in a patch of bare dirt, hooves in the air. Dust everywhere. Label: "WALLOWING".]

Bison roll in the dirt all year round. It is called wallowing, and it helps keep biting flies away and rubs off old tufts of winter fur.

[The same bull, rolling harder. A red "!" pops above it.]

In the rut, which here runs from late July through August, the bulls wallow far more often, and they roll violently on the ground to show how aggressive they are.

[A bull with its head up, mouth open, sound rings rolling out across the whole valley.]

They bellow too, and the deep bellow of a bull can be heard right across the landscape, which is the point. The park says bulls show high amounts of aggression during the rut.

[A dog rolling on a living-room rug, then the rug becomes a valley and the dog becomes a bull.]

Picture a dog rolling on the rug, then scale the dog up to nine hundred kilograms and put it in a very bad mood.

The thing to understand about that dust cloud on the horizon is that it is not weather. It is a crowd of very large animals, all angry with each other.

[Doug in the next dust patch over, rolling on his back, cap still on. Label: "TO FIT IN".]

Doug rolls in a wallow as well, to fit in.

[A bull stops and watches Doug for a long moment. Flat face. It turns and walks away.]

A bull watches him for a while, decides that Doug is not competition, and leaves.

Doug is fine.

The narrator finds this result insulting, on Doug's behalf.

[Far off, another bull bellows. The first bull turns toward the sound.]

That bull had somewhere more important to be, because a rival on the far side of the valley had just bellowed back.

## The Rut

The Rut.

[Calendar: AUG, the last page. A bull standing beside a cow, very close, head low. Label: "TENDING".]

During the rut, a bull can lose almost ninety kilograms, about one whole Doug, because he barely stops to eat.

[A bathroom scale under a bull. The needle drops. A small Doug outline floats away from the number.]

That is about twelve percent of his body weight, spent on guarding, bellowing, and fighting.

[The bull circles the cow. Another bull approaches; the first one bellows, paws, swings his horns.]

When he finds a cow who is nearly ready to mate, he stays right beside her for anywhere from a few minutes to a few days. Rival bulls get bellows, wallowing and threats.

[Two bulls lock horns and shove. Dust erupts around them.]

If the threats fail, the two bulls lock horns and shove each other back and forth, kicking up dust across the valley.

[A simple map of the United States. Yellowstone glows. A counter: "1902: ABOUT 24" climbs to "2025: NEARLY 5,300".]

There are a lot of bulls to do this. Yellowstone is the only place in the United States where bison have lived since prehistoric times, and poachers cut the herd to about two dozen by nineteen oh two. In twenty twenty-five there were nearly five thousand three hundred.

[A calm grey card. "2000 TO 2015: 25 HURT, 0 DIED".]

And to be fair to the bison, they very rarely kill anyone. From two thousand to twenty fifteen, bison hurt twenty-five people in the park, and none of them died.

Bears have killed more people here than bison have. The bison holds the injury record because so many people walk up to it.

[The tear-off calendar's last page is ripped away. A red "RUT" stamp slams down on the blank backing.]

Which brings us to the referee.

[Two huge bulls square up head to head in the dust. Doug steps into the gap between them, in a striped referee shirt, whistle in his mouth.]

Two bulls, each about as heavy as two grand pianos, square up in the dust, and Doug steps into the gap between them with a whistle.

[Close on the whistle. A small "TWEET" label.]

[Both bulls charge at once. Cut to black on the moment of impact. DOUG DEATHS: 274.]

Both bulls charge at the same moment, and Doug is the only thing either of them hits.

Doug has died. Again.

[The field guide AMERICAN BISON page: seven red NOs. A yellow sticky note in the narrator's handwriting is pressed on beside them: "PLANT EATER."]

The narrator has added a sticky note to the field guide, and all it says is plant eater.

[Closing image: the herd moves off across the valley at dusk, through gold dust. Hooked on one bull's horn, high above the dust, rides the red cap.]
