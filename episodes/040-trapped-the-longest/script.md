## Nutty Putty Cave

Nutty Putty Cave.

[TIME TRAPPED: blank. A cave cross-section, narrow passages branching through rock. One branch is drawn dashed and unlabelled. Label: "NOVEMBER 2009".]

In November two thousand nine, an experienced caver slid head-first into a gap ten inches by eighteen inches, barely bigger than the end of a carry-on suitcase.

[Doug's suitcase stood on end beside a dotted rectangle of the same size. Label: "10 x 18 INCHES".]

He thought he was in a squeeze he knew, one that cavers call the Birth Canal. He had actually taken a wrong turn into an unmapped crack that pointed downward and then simply stopped.

[The dashed branch lights up. A label points at it: "UNMAPPED".]

Here's the problem.

[A red arrow slides down into the crack. A second red arrow tries to go back up and stalls.]

Going in, gravity helped him, but coming out meant pushing uphill, head-down, with almost no room to move. The same shape that let him slide in was the shape that held him.

[A crowd of identical helmet heads at the cave entrance. Label: "100+ VOLUNTEERS".]

More than a hundred volunteers worked through the night, and for hours, getting him out still looked possible. Five years earlier, rescuers had freed a stuck teenager from this same cave after fourteen hours. The cave had already been shut once for safety, in two thousand six, and had only reopened that May.

[A crude rope and pulley rig. It pulls; a red "!" appears; one anchor pops out of the rock.]

This time they rigged ropes and pulleys, and for a moment, it worked, until an anchor drilled into the rock gave way.

[A different cave, drawn in a different colour. Doug with a helmet torch and his suitcase stands at a small dark hole. A clipboard behind him reads "ONE MORE PASSAGE".]

Doug has been volunteered to check one more passage, in a different cave entirely. Doug did not agree to this.

[Doug goes in head-first. Only his boots and the suitcase are visible. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 309. The cap sits at the passage mouth. Sticker "NUTTY PUTTY" slaps onto the suitcase.]

Doug has died.

[Sober card, plain grey, no Doug, no drawing of a person: "NUTTY PUTTY CAVE, UTAH, 2009. SEALED." TIME TRAPPED: ABOUT 27 HOURS.]

The real caver was stuck for about twenty-seven hours, and in the end the strain was more than his body could take.

Bringing him out was judged too dangerous, so his family and the landowner agreed to leave him where he was. Within a week, the cave was sealed with explosives and cement.

[The grey card holds. TIME TRAPPED ticks from HOURS toward MONTHS.]

That cave held him for about a day. The next trap took months to let go.

Months.

## The Snowbound Car

The Snowbound Car.

[TIME TRAPPED: ABOUT 2 MONTHS. A white forest track. Two snowmobiles stop beside a smooth white hump. One window has been scraped clear. Label: "SWEDEN, 2012".]

In February twenty twelve, snowmobilers in northern Sweden dug into what looked like an abandoned car on a forest track, and found a man alive inside it.

[A calendar: DECEMBER 19 circled, then pages flip to FEBRUARY.]

He told police he had been there since the nineteenth of December, which is about two months.

[A crude thermometer outside the drift drops to "-30°C".]

He'd had no food at all, and he survived by eating snow while the air outside dropped to around minus thirty degrees Celsius.

The strange part is that the snow wasn't just the trap. It was also the reason he lived.

[Close-up of snow: tiny bubbles between ice crystals. Red arrows show heat trying to get out and slowing down.]

Snow is full of tiny pockets of air, so heat leaks through it very slowly, and the hospital's chief doctor said the buried car had become the equivalent of an igloo.

[A cartoon snowdrift with a duvet pattern drawn on top. Label: "SNOW DUVET".]

Think of the drift as a giant duvet, freezing on the outside and much warmer underneath, and he also had warm clothes and a sleeping bag.

[A sleeping bear icon beside a question mark. Label: "A DOCTOR'S IDEA".]

Another doctor suggested that his body may have cooled to around thirty-one degrees and slowed down into something like hibernation, a bit like a bear, using very little energy. That's an idea, not a proven finding.

[Doug in a car under a huge drift, suitcase on the back seat. Calendar pages fall past the window. Doug gives a thumbs up.]

Doug spent two months under a drift just like it, in the same kind of sleeping bag. Doug is fine.

[The suitcase on the back seat. No new sticker. The narrator's hand puts a sticker roll back in a drawer.]

The suitcase stays empty, which was not the plan.

[The cap sits on the car roof in the snow.]

He is the only person on this list who walked out, after two months. After this, the longer nature holds on, the stranger the thing that comes back out.

Thousands of years.

## Ötzi's Hollow

Ötzi's Hollow.

[TIME TRAPPED: ABOUT 5,300 YEARS. A high mountain pass, ice and rock. Two tiny hikers stop beside a dark shape in the melting ice. Label: "SEPTEMBER 1991".]

In September nineteen ninety-one, two hikers in the Alps saw a body melting out of the ice, and at first everyone assumed a climber had died there recently.

[A crude stamp "RECENT?" in red, then a second stamp over it: "5,300 YEARS".]

He was about five thousand three hundred years old.

[A map of the Austrian and Italian border with one red dot on the ridge. Label: "3,210 M".]

He lay at three thousand two hundred and ten meters, high on the border between Austria and Italy, in a rocky gully about three meters by seven.

[A cross-section of the pass: a narrow rock gully with ice filling it. Beside it, a garage floor outline of the same size. Label: "3 x 7 M".]

That's roughly the floor of a small one-car garage, and for thousands of years, ice sat on top of him in that hollow and kept him.

[Two crude scientist heads argue with red "?" marks between them.]

Scientists still argue about exactly how the ice behaved up there. And the cold isn't what killed him, because researchers believe another person did.

But that's not the disturbing part.

[A crude calendar: "5,300 YEARS" on one side, "4 DAYS" on the other. Red arrow between them.]

Nature kept him for about five thousand years, and people damaged him in four days.

[Ice picks and a crude pneumatic drill beside the ice. A red X over the drill. A broken bow beside it.]

Because he was taken for a recent death, he was cut out of the ice with picks and a pneumatic drill, and the drill damaged him. His bow was broken too, and part of it only came out of the ice a year later.

[A crude museum cold room, frost on the glass, a thermometer at "-6°C". Label: "BOLZANO".]

Today he lies in a cold cell at a museum in Bolzano, in Italy, kept at minus six degrees Celsius, as close to glacier conditions as the museum can manage.

[Doug lies in the same kind of hollow at a different pass, suitcase beside him, waving. A crude time-lapse: ice slides over the top. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 310. Sticker "ÖTZTAL ALPS".]

Doug lay down in a hollow at the pass to see what it was like. Doug has died. Again.

[Two tiny hikers find the red cap on the ice. They look at each other.]

Five thousand three hundred years later, two hikers found the cap first, and they assumed it was recent.

[The cap on the ice. The pass fades to dark cave rock.]

Ice keeps things by sitting still. The next trap needed the sea to rise.

## Hoyo Negro

Hoyo Negro.

[TIME TRAPPED: ABOUT 12,000 YEARS. A diver's light in black water. Below it, a huge dark bell-shaped chamber. Label: "MEXICO, 2007".]

In two thousand seven, cave divers in Mexico swam into a flooded pit called Hoyo Negro, which is Spanish for black hole, and found a skeleton at the bottom.

[Sober card, plain grey, no drawing of a person or bones: "HOYO NEGRO, YUCATÁN. ABOUT 12,000 YEARS AGO."]

It is the most complete human skeleton older than twelve thousand years ever found in the Americas, a young woman who lived between twelve and thirteen thousand years ago.

[A dry cave, a thin trickle of water at the very bottom of a shaft. Label: "FRESH WATER".]

Researchers think she went underground looking for fresh water, and fell into the pit. Her DNA links her to modern Native Americans, which helped show that the earliest Americans and today's Native Americans share the same ancestry.

[Silhouettes only, no bodies: a sabre-toothed cat, a giant ground sloth, an elephant-like gomphothere. Label: "26+ ANIMALS".]

She wasn't alone. Sabre-toothed cats, giant ground sloths and an elephant relative called a gomphothere fell in too, more than twenty-six large animals in all.

[The chamber floor beside an American football field outline. The chamber is wider. Label: "61 M ACROSS".]

The chamber at the bottom is about sixty-one meters across, which is wider than an American football field.

The worst part is the timing.

[A sea level line far below the cave. Then it rises, up through the cave, until the whole chamber is blue.]

When they fell, the sea was far lower and the cave was dry. Then the ice age ended, the sea rose, and roughly ten thousand years ago, the water filled everything.

[Low-oxygen water, drawn as still dark blue. Label: "LOW OXYGEN".]

That water was low in oxygen, so it protected the bones from the tropical climate above, and kept the pit sealed until divers swam in.

[Doug in a dark dry cave with a torch and his suitcase, stepping forward. The torch spins down into the dark. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 311. Sticker "HOYO NEGRO".]

Doug went in with a torch, looking for the water. He found the pit first. Doug has died.

[Blue water. The red cap floats up past a diver's light.]

The trap was dry when it caught them, and the sea only made sure nobody left.

Tens of thousands of years.

## The Permafrost Worms

The Permafrost Worms.

[TIME TRAPPED: ABOUT 46,000 YEARS. A lab dish under a magnifier. A tiny worm, curled, then uncurling. Label: "WOKE UP".]

Scientists thawed tiny worms that had been frozen for about forty-six thousand years, and two of them woke up.

[A frozen riverbank cliff in Siberia, a fossil rodent burrow marked deep in it. Label: "KOLYMA RIVER".]

They came out of a fossil rodent burrow in the permafrost cliffs of the Kolyma River in Siberia, about forty meters below the surface.

[A ten-storey building outline beside the cliff. The burrow sits below its base.]

That's deeper than a ten-storey building is tall, in ground that had never thawed.

[A Neanderthal silhouette with a crude date card: "STILL AROUND".]

Plant material from the burrow was radiocarbon dated to between about forty-six and forty-eight thousand years ago, so when these worms last moved, Neanderthals were still alive.

[A freezer labelled "2002", then a calendar flipping to "2018".]

The frozen soil was dug out in two thousand two, and the living worms were first reported in twenty eighteen.

[A crude magnifying glass over the dish with a red "?" beside it. Label: "OR MODERN?".]

Not everyone is convinced. One outside biologist says the team still has to rule out modern worms sneaking in from the surface, while the team says it trusts its clean methods.

Here's the part nobody expected.

[One worm. Then two. Then many. Label: "100+ GENERATIONS".]

They didn't just survive. This species reproduces without males, and the revived worm's family has now been kept going in the lab for more than a hundred generations.

[The worm and a slab of frozen soil labelled "-80°C" in a lab freezer.]

The team also found that drying out a little before freezing helps these worms survive deep freezing.

[Doug frozen in the permafrost wall next to a worm, cap on, suitcase beside him. Both thaw in a lab dish. The worm wriggles off. Doug does not. DOUG DEATHS: 312. Sticker "KOLYMA".]

Doug and a worm were thawed in the same dish. One of them woke up. Doug has died. Again.

[The cap sits in the empty lab dish.]

A worm is an animal, but the next thing out of the permafrost isn't even a cell, and it still works.

## The Permafrost Virus

The Permafrost Virus.

[TIME TRAPPED: MORE THAN 48,500 YEARS. A cross-section of a Siberian lake, a sample tube deep in the frozen ground below it. Label: "16 M BELOW A LAKE".]

A giant virus sat in frozen ground sixteen meters under a lake in Siberia for more than forty-eight thousand five hundred years, and it could still infect.

[A crude oval virus particle with a small opening at one end. Label: "PANDORAVIRUS YEDOMA".]

It's called Pandoravirus yedoma, and a team in France revived it from permafrost at Yukechi Alas, in the Yakutia region of Siberia.

[An older trophy labelled "ABOUT 30,000 YEARS" pushed aside by a bigger one labelled "48,500".]

The same team had already revived a giant virus about thirty thousand years old, so this one pushed their own record back by more than eighteen thousand years.

[A bank card seen edge-on, with a row of tiny ovals along it. Label: "ABOUT 760 IN A ROW".]

Each particle is about a thousandth of a millimeter long, so about seven hundred and sixty of them in a row would match the thickness of a bank card.

[A light microscope with one oval visible in the eyepiece circle.]

That's enormous for a virus, because pandoraviruses are big enough to see through an ordinary light microscope.

Here's the catch.

[An amoeba, a blob with one nucleus. A red arrow from the virus to the amoeba only. A person icon far away with a red X between them.]

The researchers chose it on purpose. It only infects amoebae, single cells that are about a billion years of evolution away from people, and the team called the risk from reviving these amoeba viruses totally negligible.

[A crude ranking board: "STILL ABLE TO INFECT SOMETHING" with one entry.]

It's the one thing on this list that was trapped and can still infect anything at all, and it just can't infect Doug.

[Doug in a flat, unsmiling amoeba costume, a grey blob with one nucleus, red cap on top. He drifts toward the virus. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 313. Sticker "YUKECHI".]

It only infects amoebae, which is exactly why Doug is now dressed as one. Doug has died. Again.

[The flattened costume in the dish, cap on top.]

The next trap was picky too. It mostly took mammoths, and mostly the young males.

Hundreds of thousands of years.

## The Mammoth Sinkhole

The Mammoth Sinkhole.

[TIME TRAPPED: 160,000 TO 232,000 YEARS. A clean museum dig layout seen from above: bones in the ground, grid strings, no struggling animals. Label: "HOT SPRINGS, SOUTH DAKOTA".]

One sinkhole in South Dakota has given up the bones of sixty-one mammoths, fifty-eight Columbian and three woolly, plus giant short-faced bears. A construction worker found the first bones in nineteen seventy-four, while the ground was being cleared for new houses.

[A cross-section: a limestone cavern collapses, leaving a steep pit. Warm water rises into it. Label: "WARM SPRING WATER".]

A limestone cavern collapsed and left a steep pit, warm spring water rose into it and made a pond, and the mammoths came to drink and feed.

[The pit beside a six-storey building outline. Label: "20+ M DEEP".]

The pit was at least twenty meters deep, about as deep as a six-storey building is tall, and its walls were slick shale, so anything that slipped in often couldn't climb out.

[An old museum card "ABOUT 26,000 YEARS" with a red X. A new card beside it: "160,000 TO 232,000 YEARS".]

For decades, the bones were thought to be about twenty-six thousand years old, because that's what the old radiocarbon dates on the bone said. In August twenty twenty-six, a new study dated the sediment itself to between a hundred and sixty thousand and two hundred and thirty-two thousand years ago.

The uncomfortable part is who fell in.

[A row of mammoth pelvis outlines, almost all labelled "YOUNG MALE".]

Measurements of the pelvic bones show that most of them were young males.

[A herd led by an old female, walking past. One young male wanders off alone toward the pond.]

Researchers have suggested that young males who had left their family herds had no experienced older female to lead them, so they walked into traps more often.

[Doug in a mammoth costume, flat and unsmiling, red cap on top, suitcase in his trunk, walks confidently to the edge for a drink. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 314. Sticker "HOT SPRINGS".]

The sinkhole mostly took mammoths, so Doug went as one, young, male and unsupervised. Doug has died. Again.

[The cap at the edge of the pit. The ground below turns to dark seafloor mud.]

About two hundred thousand years is a long time to be stuck, and the last trap makes it look like a lunch break.

A hundred million years.

## The Seafloor

The Seafloor.

[TIME TRAPPED: 101.5 MILLION YEARS, stamped red. A lab dish of grey mud. Crude dots in it, then twice as many. Label: "STILL ALIVE".]

Up to ninety-nine percent of the microbes in seafloor mud laid down a hundred and one and a half million years ago were still alive.

[A cross-section: the South Pacific, nearly six kilometers of dark water, a drill pipe from a ship going a hundred meters into the seafloor. Label: "SOUTH PACIFIC GYRE".]

The mud was drilled from under the middle of the South Pacific, through nearly six kilometers of water and about a hundred meters into the seafloor.

[A dinosaur silhouette on a crude date card: "CRETACEOUS".]

When it settled, it was the Cretaceous, and large dinosaurs were still walking around.

[A near-empty ocean, a single crumb drifting down. Label: "FEWEST NUTRIENTS".]

The water above is the part of the ocean with the fewest nutrients, so little falls to the bottom that the mud piles up extremely slowly, and oxygen seeps all the way down.

[A crude battery icon with one tiny bar. Label: "MILLIONS OF TIMES LESS".]

The microbes down there get millions of times less energy than microbes near the surface, which should be nowhere near enough to stay alive for that long.

[A clock face for one day. A tiny red sliver at the end. Label: "WRITTEN HISTORY: LAST 4.5 SECONDS".]

If that whole hundred and one and a half million years were squeezed into a single day, all of written history would fit in its last four and a half seconds. On that clock, Ötzi would be from about four and a half seconds ago.

Here's the part that should bother you.

[Lab droplets fall into the dish. The dots double. Then double again.]

In the lab, the team gave them simple food, and they ate and divided. They hadn't been dead. They had been waiting for a meal.

[Doug in a sealed pocket of mud next to a few microbes, cap on, suitcase beside him. The core comes up into the lab. Food drops go in. The microbes multiply. Doug does not. DOUG DEATHS: 315. Sticker "SOUTH PACIFIC GYRE".]

Doug was drilled out of the same mud, next to a few of them, and they woke up first. Doug has died. Again. Possibly in the Cretaceous.

[The core is sliced open. The red cap comes out clean.]

The longest trap on this list held them for a hundred million years, and all it took to get out was lunch.
