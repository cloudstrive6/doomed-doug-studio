## Blood Falls

Blood Falls.

[Map of Antarctica, red dot on the McMurdo Dry Valleys. Cut to a white glacier front with a rust-orange stain running down it into a frozen lake. A five-storey building drawn beside the stain, same height.]

In the McMurdo Dry Valleys, a white glacier called Taylor Glacier has a dark red stain pouring down its face, about five storeys high.

Here's the strange part. It isn't blood. The liquid comes out of the glacier perfectly clear, packed with dissolved iron, and the moment it touches the air, that iron rusts, like an old nail left out in the rain.

[Clear liquid trickles out of the ice. It turns orange, then red. Label: "RUST".]

It isn't new, either. The water was sealed under the glacier an estimated one and a half to two million years ago, under about four hundred meters of ice, with no sunlight and no oxygen.

[Cross-section: a dark pool deep under the glacier, a network of salty channels running inland from it. ICE ABOVE DOUG flashes 400 m for this shot only, then returns to 0 m.]

It is at least twice as salty as seawater, so it never freezes, and in twenty fifteen a helicopter survey traced it at least twelve kilometers inland.

And it isn't empty. Microbes live in it, breathing iron and sulfur instead of oxygen, alone in the dark, the entire time.

[Doug stands in front of the red falls with his suitcase, holding up a phone for a selfie, thumbs up.]

The last time Doug was in Antarctica, he stood on top of it. This time he took a selfie at the edge, and the glacier front joined the photo.

[A block of ice drops. Flat Doug under it, X eyes. Ghost Doug rises. The cap sits on top of the block. Sticker "BLOOD FALLS" slaps onto the suitcase. DOUG DEATHS: 131.]

Doug has died. Again.

The microbes have lasted more than a million years under that glacier, and Doug lasted one selfie.

## Erebus Ice Caves

Erebus Ice Caves.

[Map, red dot on Ross Island. A tall white volcano, steam rising from small ice chimneys on its slopes. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 25 m.]

Mount Erebus is Earth's southernmost active volcano, almost three thousand eight hundred meters tall, and at the top it keeps a lake of lava that stays there, year after year. Every so often, a giant gas bubble bursts through it and throws molten rock up to two hundred and fifty meters.

On its slopes, hot volcanic gas leaks out through cracks in the ground and melts tunnels and caverns into the ice from below. Where the steam reaches the surface, it freezes into hollow ice chimneys that stand on the snow like smokestacks.

[Doug rappels down into a cavern. Label: "UP TO 25 M".]

Some of the caverns are up to about twenty-five meters tall, and the only way in is on a rope. Outside, during one expedition, the air sat at about minus twenty-five degrees Celsius.

The catch is what happens inside. Some of these caves get up to about twenty-five degrees Celsius, warm enough, as one researcher put it, to be comfortable in a T-shirt.

[A thermometer: outside minus twenty-five, inside plus twenty-five. A T-shirt on a hanger.]

So the warmest room on this trip is not on the ice. It's inside it, on a volcano.

And something has already moved in. Microbes live in these caves, and in twenty seventeen, scientists sequenced DNA from the cave soil and found traces of mosses, algae and small animals. The researchers were careful about it. DNA is not proof that anything is alive in there. It's only proof that something was.

[Doug takes off his jacket, folds it neatly, and lies down on the warm cave floor with his hands behind his head.]

Doug took his jacket off, folded it into a pillow, and lay down on the warm floor for a nap.

Doug is fine.

[DOUG DEATHS stays at 131. The narrator's caption bar flickers once.]

The air in volcanic caves is often the dangerous part. Nobody should ever trust a cave this comfortable.

## Thwaites From Below

Thwaites From Below.

[Map, red dot on West Antarctica. A huge glacier front meeting dark sea. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 587 m.]

Thwaites Glacier in West Antarctica has a nickname in the media. They call it the Doomsday Glacier.

In twenty nineteen, airborne radar found a cavity growing under part of it, covering about two thirds of the area of Manhattan. It stood nearly three hundred meters tall, almost the height of the Eiffel Tower.

[An Eiffel Tower drawn inside a dark cavity under the ice, its tip just touching the ceiling.]

That space had once held about fourteen billion tons of ice, and most of it had melted in only about three years.

So in twenty twenty, a team melted a hole straight through five hundred and eighty-seven meters of floating ice at Thwaites and lowered a long, narrow robot called Icefin into the dark water underneath. One of the scientists said that until then, nobody had data from the grounding line of any major glacier.

[The rope drops through a fresh borehole. A long yellow cylinder robot on a cable drops out of the bottom into dark water.]

The worst part is what it saw on the underside. The bottom of the ice was not smooth at all. It was carved into steps and slots, with crevasses tens of meters deep cut upward into it, and warmer, saltier water was flowing into those slots and melting their walls sideways.

The flat parts were melting slowly. The steep parts were melting fast, and although they make up only about a tenth of the underside, they were responsible for more than a quarter of the melting. The glacier is being eaten from underneath, where nobody can see it, one crevasse at a time.

[Doug swims into an upside-down crevasse with a torch, looking up at the ice ceiling.]

Doug swam into a crevasse with a torch to get a closer look at the ceiling.

[A creak. A chunk drops. Flat Doug, X eyes. The cap floats up in the water and bobs against the ice. Sticker "THWAITES". DOUG DEATHS: 132.]

The ceiling got a closer look at Doug.

According to NASA scientists, Thwaites holds enough ice to raise the world's oceans by about sixty-five centimeters.

## The Fish Under the Ice

The Fish Under the Ice.

[Map, red dot deep in the Ross Ice Shelf. The rope drops. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 740 m.]

In January twenty fifteen, a team in West Antarctica melted a hole through about seven hundred and forty meters of ice. That's more than two Eiffel Towers stacked on top of each other.

[Two Eiffel Towers stacked inside the borehole, with room to spare.]

The spot was about eight hundred and fifty kilometers from the front edge of the ice shelf, right where the ice stops resting on the ground and starts to float. Underneath, there was a gap of seawater only about ten meters deep, roughly the height of an Olympic high-diving platform.

[A diving platform drawn in the gap, the board touching the ice ceiling.]

The team sent a small camera robot down the hole to look at the seabed. The water was around minus two degrees Celsius, and it was permanently dark.

Here's what nobody expected. Something swam up to the camera. Pale, see-through fish about twenty centimeters long hovered right in front of the lens, along with swarms of small crustaceans called amphipods, and reports said they seemed curious about it.

[Pale fish and amphipods crowd around the camera lens, looking straight into it.]

They were the farthest south that fish had ever been found. The scientists drilled to the edge of the world, and something was already waiting at the camera.

The ice above this gap does not sit still, either. Near the grounding line, where it starts to float, the ice flexes up and down with the tides.

[Doug floats in front of a fish, waving. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug waved at a fish, and the fish looked back, which Doug took as a yes.

[The ice ceiling lowers with the tide. The gap shrinks. Flat Doug, X eyes, between ice and seabed. The fish swims off. Sticker "ROSS ICE SHELF". DOUG DEATHS: 133.]

Doug has died. Again.

Nobody yet knows exactly what feeds the fish down here, this far from sunlight.

## Lake Whillans

Lake Whillans.

[Map, red dot near the Whillans Ice Stream. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 800 m.]

Antarctica has hundreds of lakes hidden under its ice, and until twenty thirteen, nobody had cleanly sampled a single one.

That January, a team set up a hot-water drill over a lake called Whillans and melted a hole through about eight hundred meters of ice. The drill heated its water to about ninety degrees Celsius, and it was built to keep the team's own germs from going down the hole with them.

[A drill rig on the ice. Steam rises from the borehole.]

The lake itself is not deep. It is only about one point eight meters deep, about the height of a tall man, but it spreads out over about fifty-two square kilometers.

But that's not the disturbing part. Every sample came back full. There were about one hundred and thirty thousand cells in every milliliter of water, which is about as crowded as an ordinary lake or the ocean. A teaspoon holds five milliliters, so that's around six hundred and fifty thousand living cells in a single spoonful of water that hasn't seen the sun.

[A teaspoon of lake water under a magnifying glass, swarming with tiny dots. Label: "650,000".]

The team counted nearly four thousand different kinds of microbes in the water. None of them use sunlight. Many get their energy from chemicals like iron, nitrogen and sulfur, released as the moving ice grinds the rock beneath it.

The first lake ever properly sampled under the ice was not empty. Nothing down here is empty.

[Doug sits on a folding chair at the lake's edge with a sandwich and a fishing rod. The line is in the water.]

Doug brought a sandwich and a fishing rod, because as far as Doug is concerned, a lake is a lake.

Doug is fine.

[DOUG DEATHS stays at 133. Nothing bites. The microbes ignore him.]

The microbes eat rock and want nothing from him, and nothing at the bottom of a lake has ever ignored Doug before.

## The Sponge Boulder

The Sponge Boulder.

[Map, red dot on the Filchner-Ronne Ice Shelf. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 872 m.]

In twenty twenty-one, scientists from the British Antarctic Survey published something they had found completely by accident.

They had drilled through almost nine hundred meters of the Filchner-Ronne Ice Shelf to collect mud from the seabed. Instead of mud, their equipment hit a rock, sitting on the seabed more than twelve hundred meters down.

[A sampling tube bumps against a boulder on the dark seabed.]

When they checked the camera footage, the boulder was covered in animals, including sponges and other creatures that spend their whole lives fixed to one spot.

[The boulder lit by a camera light: sponges and stalked animals stuck to its surface.]

The spot was about two hundred and sixty kilometers from the open sea, in total darkness, in water around minus two point two degrees Celsius. That's colder than the inside of a refrigerator, with no light, ever.

The unsettling part is how these animals eat. Sponges cannot move, so they filter food out of the water drifting past, and in the open ocean that food starts as algae growing in sunlight near the surface.

Here, the researchers estimated that the nearest sunlit water upstream is somewhere between six hundred and twenty-five and fifteen hundred kilometers away. The lead scientist asked the obvious question himself. What are they eating?

[A long dotted arrow from a tiny sun far off the edge of the frame to the boulder. Label: "625 TO 1,500 KM".]

Nobody knows yet, and nobody knows how they got there or how long they have been there. They hit a rock by accident, and it was alive.

[Doug sits next to the boulder, perfectly still, mouth open, waiting for food to drift by like the sponges.]

Doug decided to live like the sponges, so he sat very still next to the boulder with his mouth open and waited for food to drift by.

[Time passes. Frost creeps over Doug. A Doug-shaped block of ice, the cap on top. Sticker "FILCHNER-RONNE". DOUG DEATHS: 134.]

The sponges have had a lot more practice.

## Mercer Subglacial Lake

Thick ice.

Mercer Subglacial Lake.

[Map, red dot on the Whillans Ice Plain. The ice bands turn glacier blue. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 1,087 m.]

In late December twenty eighteen, a team melted a hole through one thousand and eighty-seven meters of ice to reach a lake that nobody had ever sampled. The hole was only about forty centimeters wide, a little narrower than a basketball hoop, and they kept it open for about ten days.

[The rope disappears into a long narrow borehole.]

They brought up water and mud from the lake floor and looked at it under a microscope, expecting microbes.

Here's the problem. In the mud, they found the carcasses of tiny crustaceans, each smaller than a poppy seed, and the body of a tardigrade, an eight-legged animal often called a water bear.

[A poppy seed beside a tiny cartoon crustacean shell, which is smaller. A cartoon tardigrade silhouette next to them.]

The lead scientist was so surprised that he assumed the samples had been contaminated, so the team cleaned everything and sampled again. More shells came up.

None of these animals were alive, and they did not belong there. They appear to be land animals, the kind that live in ponds and streams. One idea is that they lived in the Transantarctic Mountains during a warmer spell. When the cold came back, they were washed down by water under the ice, or dragged in by an advancing glacier. One researcher estimated that they died thousands of years ago, not millions.

[A mountain range far off. A dotted line from a small pond down into the buried lake.]

The animals in this lake are not alive, and nobody knows exactly how they got there.

[Doug on the lake floor with a clipboard, taking notes on a shell. The rope above him goes slack and floats down in loops.]

Doug went down to take notes, and halfway through his second page, the rope stopped being attached to anything.

[Doug on the lake floor, X eyes. The clipboard drifts up. Sticker "MERCER LAKE". DOUG DEATHS: 135.]

Doug has died. Again.

He is now the largest thing in the lake that nobody can explain.

## Volcanoes Under the Ice

Volcanoes Under the Ice.

[Map, red dot on the West Antarctic Rift. The background deepens to cobalt. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 2,000 m.]

In twenty seventeen, a team at the University of Edinburgh went through radar maps of the ground under West Antarctica, looking for one very specific shape, a cone. The idea came from an undergraduate student.

[A radar map. Cone shapes highlighted one by one, dozens of them.]

They found ninety-one volcanoes nobody had known about. Added to the ones already known, that made one hundred and thirty-eight volcanoes under the West Antarctic ice sheet, roughly two kilometers below the surface. They are strung along a rift that runs for about three thousand five hundred kilometers.

Twenty-nine of them are more than a kilometer tall. The tallest is about three thousand eight hundred and fifty meters high, about the height of Mount Fuji, and it is completely buried.

[A Mount Fuji silhouette beside a buried cone of the same height, both under a thick lid of ice.]

The team described the area as potentially one of the largest volcanic regions on Earth, and one news report noted that only East Africa's rift has volcanoes packed this densely. You can't see any of it from the surface.

Here's the twist. Nobody knows how many of these volcanoes are still active. Even a quiet one can warm the ice from below and melt it into water, and the researchers suggested that if the ice above them thins, volcanic activity could increase. Nobody can check that yet.

[A volcano under the ice glows faintly. Meltwater collects in a pocket above it.]

One of the largest volcanic regions on Earth might be the one nobody can see.

[Doug finds a warm patch of ground on the bed, sighs happily, and lies down on it.]

Doug found a warm patch on the ground under two kilometers of ice and lay down on it, because it was the first warm thing he had felt in hours.

[The warm patch rumbles. A cartoon wave of meltwater sweeps Doug away. The cap spins on the wave. Sticker "WEST ANTARCTIC RIFT". DOUG DEATHS: 136.]

The warm patch was warm for a reason.

## Lake Ellsworth

Lake Ellsworth.

[Map, red dot in the heart of West Antarctica. Deep navy ice. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 3,200 m.]

Lake Ellsworth is about eleven kilometers long, sealed under three point two kilometers of ice, which is more than seven Empire State Buildings stacked on top of each other.

[Seven Empire State Buildings stacked inside the ice column, with room left over.]

It is about one hundred and fifty meters deep, and it may have been cut off from the outside world for up to a million years.

In December twenty twelve, a British team set up camp above it and started melting a hole straight down. The plan came with a deadline, because a hole melted with hot water starts freezing shut as soon as the hot water stops. Once they broke into the lake, they would have about twenty-four hours to take their samples before the hole closed.

[A clock beside the borehole, counting down from 24.]

They never got that far. First, the boiler that heats the drilling water failed. Then, about three hundred meters down, the team could not link the main hole to a second hole they needed to recycle the drilling water. They kept trying for more than a day, and by then the fuel was too low to finish, so the attempt was called off.

[Fuel drums, all empty. The drill crew loads the sledge.]

The disturbing part is the timing. The lake had waited perhaps a million years for someone to arrive, and the hole to it began closing the moment the crew turned the heat off.

[Doug hangs halfway down the borehole on the rope, waving up at the empty sky. The drill crew drives away.]

Doug was about halfway down when the crew packed up. Nobody told him, because nobody had told Doug about the plan to begin with.

[The borehole walls close in. A Doug-shaped column of ice, the cap on top, sealed into the ice sheet. Sticker "LAKE ELLSWORTH". DOUG DEATHS: 137.]

Doug has died. Again.

The team's leader said a return would take three to four years, maybe five. The lake is still down there, untouched, and the hole to it is gone.

## Lake Vostok

The bottom.

Lake Vostok.

[Map, red dot deep in East Antarctica. The background goes nearly black. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 3,766 m.]

Lake Vostok is the largest of the hundreds of lakes hidden under Antarctica's ice. It is about two hundred and thirty kilometers long and fifty kilometers wide, roughly the size of Lake Ontario, and about eight hundred meters deep in its southern basin.

[Lake Ontario outline laid over Lake Vostok. Nearly identical.]

It sits under almost three thousand eight hundred meters of ice, kept at about minus three degrees Celsius by heat coming up from the Earth. How long it has been sealed off depends on who you ask. Estimates run from about fifteen million years to about twenty-five million.

A Russian team at Vostok Station drilled toward it, and for about thirty years they kept the hole open with kerosene and antifreeze so it would not freeze shut. Other scientists worried that whatever came down that hole might end up in a lake nobody had ever touched.

[A deep borehole, a dark streak of fluid in it. Labels: "KEROSENE", "ANTIFREEZE".]

In February twenty twelve, at three thousand seven hundred and sixty-six meters, the drill finally broke through.

Here's the part nobody planned for. When the drill touched the lake, the water did not wait to be sampled. It rushed up the borehole on its own, pushing the drilling fluid ahead of it, and then it froze inside the hole.

[Lake water surges up the borehole in a cartoon column, then turns to ice mid-rush.]

The Russian team said that was good news, because water moving up meant less chance of anything moving down. Even so, one American expert warned that the frozen samples could be contaminated, because the upper part of the hole had never been drilled cleanly.

[Doug at the very bottom of the borehole, knocking on the ice floor. Speech bubble: "hello?"]

Doug knocked on the bottom of the hole to see if anybody was home.

[The lake surges up. Doug rises with it and freezes mid-wave in a pillar of ice, the cap on top. Sticker "VOSTOK". DOUG DEATHS: 138.]

Somebody was.

For millions of years, nothing touched this lake, and the first thing it did when we arrived was come up the hole.

## The Deepest Point on Land

The Deepest Point on Land.

[Map, red dot on the Denman Glacier, East Antarctica. Black ice with one faint red tone. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 4,000 m.]

In twenty nineteen, a team led by the University of California, Irvine published a new map of the ground hidden under Antarctica's ice.

Under the Denman Glacier in East Antarctica, it showed a canyon filled with ice whose floor sits more than three thousand five hundred meters below sea level. The ice in this part of the glacier is more than four kilometers thick, the thickest stack of ice above Doug on this whole trip.

[A side view: sea level as a dashed line. The canyon floor far below it, packed solid with ice up past the dashed line.]

That makes the canyon floor the deepest point on land anywhere on Earth. Only the trenches at the bottom of the ocean go deeper. It is about eight times lower than the shore of the Dead Sea, which is the lowest land you can stand on in the open air.

[The Dead Sea shore drawn as a small dip. The Denman canyon drawn as a drop eight times deeper.]

Nobody has measured the bottom directly. Radar could not see it, because echoes from the steep canyon walls kept drowning out the signal from the floor. So the team worked it out from how much ice flows through the canyon, and how deep the canyon must be to carry it all. That makes it an estimate, and the best one anyone has.

That's the real problem with this place. The glacier is moving, and between nineteen ninety-six and twenty eighteen, the point where it lifts off the ground and floats retreated more than five kilometers. On one side of the glacier, the ground slopes deeper as you go inland, the kind of slope that makes a retreat easier to keep going.

[An arrow along the bed pointing inland and downhill. Label: "WRONG WAY".]

The lowest ground on any continent is down here, under the ice, and part of it slopes the wrong way.

[Doug at the canyon floor, planting a small flag: "DOUG WAS HERE".]

Doug planted a flag that said "Doug was here", which stayed true for about four seconds.

[The ice slides. Doug and the flag are carried out of frame. The cap stays behind on the canyon floor. Sticker "DENMAN". DOUG DEATHS: 139.]

Doug has died. Again.

He isn't at the end of the world. He's at the bottom of it, and the bottom is moving.

[Back at the surface. The borehole is a tiny dark dot in a white plain. The rope winches up with only the red cap on the end. The full suitcase stands beside it with nine stickers. ICE ABOVE DOUG: 4,000 m.]

More than four kilometers of ice, the lowest ground on any continent, and the only thing that came back up was the cap.
