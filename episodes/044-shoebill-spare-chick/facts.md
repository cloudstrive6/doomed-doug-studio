## FLAGGED (screener should look first)
1. **"Insurance policy."** AEWA 2015, p. 9-10: "It is thus likely that the second or weaker chick is essentially an insurance policy in case the stronger chick fails." The script attributes it as "The international conservation plan for the species describes the second chick as essentially an insurance policy". It does not say "scientists call it". Discover Wildlife also says the second chick is "believed to be an evolutionary 'back-up' in case the first dies" (Fetched).
2. **Parents "let it happen", they don't kill.** AEWA 2015, p. 9: "Recent footage in the Bangweulu Wetlands illustrated well an adult bird ignoring the pleadings of a weaker chick, which was subsequently fatally pecked by the stronger one (BBC 2013)." The script says "an adult ignored the begging of the weaker chick" and "The parents do not kill it. They let it happen." I could not reach the BBC 2013 footage itself. Discover Wildlife (Fetched) describes the same footage: the weaker sibling "is then abandoned by the parents and left to die".
3. **Mullers & Amar 2015 full text was not reachable** (paywall). Only the abstract was read (BioOne, Fetched): water collected "for cooling the chicks", and "Parents fledged 0.89 chicks per nest (n = 11 nests) when nests were actively protected from human disturbance and theft." Hatching asynchrony, priority feeding and aggression come from AEWA 2015 (citing Renson 2008) and Grzimek, not from Mullers & Amar.
4. **Brief items cut, not hedged:** the Mabamba "bow" (no reliable source found); crocodiles taking shoebill chicks (not found; AEWA names pythons, which the script uses instead); the prey size of eaten monitors (no source records it, and the script says so); the 018 callback (optional, not used).
5. **"As tall as a ten-year-old."** The shoebill's height is well sourced (110-140 cm; AEWA "some 140 cm"). The child's height is from growth-chart calculator sites only (Search, Secondary): a median of about 138 cm at age 10 (boys). Another calculator gives about 136 cm. Either way it falls inside the shoebill's 110-140 cm range, so the comparison holds. If the screener wants an official table, the CDC 2000 stature-for-age data (https://www.cdc.gov/growthcharts/) is the place to check.
6. **RESOLVED (d2, screener confirmed).** Water cooling "as many as five times on a hot day". Grzimek, via Encyclopedia.com: parents bring water to cool the eggs in hot weather and "may do this as many as five times on hot days". I read this through a fetch summary that quoted the phrase, and the passage is about the chicks. If in doubt, cut to "again and again on hot days" (Mullers & Amar 2015 abstract supports repeated trips to collect water).
