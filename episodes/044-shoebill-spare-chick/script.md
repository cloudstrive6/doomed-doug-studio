The Statue.

[The clock's hand keeps going. The shoebill does not move. A dragonfly lands on the bill and leaves.]

## The Bill

## The First Egg

The First Egg.

[Two eggs in the nest. A crude calendar beside them. The first egg is labelled "DAY 1", the second "UP TO DAY 5".]

Shoebills usually lay two eggs, and the second one can arrive up to five days after the first.

That gap decides almost everything.

The first egg hatches first, so the first chick is already older and bigger by the time its sibling breaks out of the shell.

[The nest: one large, grey, scruffy chick and, in the corner, one uncracked egg. A parent stands over the chick, which is begging, bill up. Diagram arrow from the parent's throat to the chick's bill, labelled "FOOD".]

When a parent brings food or water back to the nest, the older chick gets priority, and it is also aggressive toward the younger one.

The food travels home inside the parent, which swallows it at the water and then passes it whole, straight into the chick's bill.

It is a race where one runner gets up to a five-day head start, plus lunch at every lap.

The worst part is that the gap is set before either chick has done anything at all.

[The calendar. A label: "ABOUT 95 DAYS TO FLEDGING".]

A shoebill chick spends about ninety-five days in and around the nest before it fledges, and its parents go on feeding it for a month or more after that.

That is a long time to be second.

## The Spare

The Spare.

[The nest from above. One half has the big grey shape and a pile of fish. The other half is small and empty. THE STATUE stands beside the nest.]

A pair of shoebills may hatch two chicks, but they almost always manage to raise only one of them.

The international conservation plan for the species describes the second chick as essentially an insurance policy, in case the stronger chick fails.

[A spare tyre in the boot of a car, with a small grey tag: "IN CASE".]

The second chick is a spare tyre. You carry it everywhere, and you hope you never need it.

Siblicide, one sibling killing another, is common in shoebills, and it can happen in the first few days after hatching, or weeks later, once the older chick is strong enough.

Then there are the parents.

[The parent at the edge of the nest, looking away across the swamp. Nothing else on screen moves.]
