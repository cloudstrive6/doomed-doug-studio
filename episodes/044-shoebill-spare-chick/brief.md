### Fish.
**1. The Lungfish** (one of the three strongest: the cold-open item)
- Facts to research: the shoebill (*Balaeniceps rex*) stands up to about 1.2-1.5 m tall (sourced range). Its main prey in many studies is the
  marbled lungfish (*Protopterus aethiopicus*), along with catfish and tilapia (cite a diet study, e.g. Guillet 1979 in the Sudd, or a Uganda/Zambia
  field study). Lungfish breathe air, so they have to come to the surface, and the shoebill waits for that. Marbled lungfish can survive dry seasons
  sealed in a mud cocoon (cite a source; say "months" or "years" only as the source does). The "collapse" strike: the bird throws its whole body
  forward onto the prey.
- **Twist (by 0:45):** its favourite food is a fish that can survive without water, and it still loses, because sooner or later it has to breathe.
- Scale analogy: the shoebill is as tall as a ten-year-old, and it has spent the afternoon not moving, like a coat stand that is hungry.
- Doug beat: Doug in a lungfish costume, cap on top, "holds his breath to be safe". He has to come up eventually. Death 337, cut to black on the
  collapse. "Doug did not agree to this." here, once.

**2. The Statue** (survival)
- Facts to research: how long shoebills stand motionless while hunting (sourced figure or "for long periods"). They hunt from floating vegetation
  and the edges of papyrus swamps; how often strikes succeed (only if a study gives a rate). The head-bobbing "bow" that visitors at Uganda's
  Mabamba swamp describe: **use only if a reliable source describes it, framed as what guides and visitors report, never as a fact about behaviour.
  Cut it otherwise.**
- Scale analogy: it can stand still longer than a waiter pretending he hasn't seen you.
- Doug beat: Doug, out of costume, challenges it to a staring contest. It doesn't blink and doesn't care: he is not food. **Survival: "Doug is fine."**
  The narrator notes this is the only time today the bird looks at Doug as Doug.

**3. The Bill**
- Facts to research: the bill size (length and width from a sourced measurement; it is among the largest bird bills). Sharp edges and a hooked tip.
  It often takes a mouthful of weeds with the prey and shakes them out before swallowing. It may decapitate the fish first: one plain clause, no
  visual. Bill clattering as a greeting at the nest (sourced). **Taxonomy twist:** it was long called a stork, but genetic studies place it closer
  to pelicans (cite, e.g., Hackett et al. 2008 or a later phylogeny; wording as published). The Arabic name *abu markub*, "father of the shoe".
- Scale analogy: the bill is about the size of a man's shoe, which is the whole joke, except this shoe has a hook on the end.
- Doug beat: catfish costume. Doug clatters a pair of wooden spoons "to say hello in its language". It is not a greeting it returns. Death 338.
  The sticky note "NOT A STORK." goes on the field guide page.

### Reptiles.
**4. The Monitor**
- Facts to research: Nile monitors and water snakes as recorded prey (cite a diet study). How the shoebill handles prey that fights back.
- **Twist:** a bird that waits like a heron is willing to take on a lizard over a meter long (check the prey size the source records).
- Scale analogy: picking up a Nile monitor is like picking up a garden hose that has teeth and an opinion.
- Doug beat: monitor-lizard costume. Doug "hisses to look bigger". The bird has seen bigger. Death 339.

**5. The Floating Mat**
- Facts to research: the Sudd (South Sudan), one of the largest wetlands in the world (sourced area), and the papyrus and floating vegetation the
  shoebill stands on. The shoebill weighs only about 4-7 kg (sourced), light enough to walk on mats that would not hold a person. The world
  population: roughly 5,000 adults or fewer (IUCN; give the current range).
- Scale analogy: the floating mat holds a shoebill the way a lilo holds a cat. It does not hold Doug the way a lilo does not hold a piano.
- Doug beat: out of costume, Doug follows it onto the mat "to see where it hunts". The mat lets the bird walk away and keeps Doug. Death 340, played
  flat. This is the only death that isn't a strike, and it's honest: the swamp kills him, the bird doesn't.

**6. The Baby Crocodile**
- Facts to research: shoebills have been recorded eating young Nile crocodiles (cite a diet study or a documented observation; say "young" or
  "hatchlings" exactly as the source does). Whether crocodiles also take shoebill chicks or eggs (cite, or cut). One 027 callback line.
- **Twist:** the bird that waits for lungfish also eats baby crocodiles, and the crocodiles would return the favour.
- Scale analogy: a hatchling croc is about the length of a ruler, and the shoebill swallows it the way you'd swallow a long spaghetti strand you
  regret.
- Doug beat: crocodile-hatchling costume. Doug "waits for his mum". So does the shoebill. Death 341. One line of 027 callback: "The last
  crocodile Doug met was bigger than his boat. This one is smaller than his shoe."

### Family.
**7. The Water Bucket** (survival)
- Facts to research: in the heat, parents bring water in their bills and pour it over the eggs or chicks to cool them (cite a nesting study, e.g.
  Buxton et al. 1978 or a later Zambia/Bangweulu study). Nest site, nest size, incubation period, both parents incubate.
- Scale analogy: the parent fills its bill like a watering can and walks it home, many times a day.
- Doug beat: Doug sits on the nest "to help with the heat". The parent pours a full bill of swamp water over him. **Survival.** The narrator is
  quietly disappointed (no "Doug is fine." here, it's used at item 2).

**8. The First Egg**
- Facts to research: the clutch is usually 1-3 eggs, laid days apart, so the first chick hatches first and is bigger (sourced). The older chick is
  fed more and is aggressive to the younger one (cite, e.g., Mullers & Amar 2015, Bangweulu Wetlands, Zambia). How long the chicks are in the nest
  before fledging.
- **Twist:** the eggs are laid days apart on purpose, so one chick always starts ahead.
- Scale analogy: it's a race where one runner gets a three-day head start and a free lunch.
- Doug beat: a grey, scruffy shoebill-chick costume (never cute; see risks). Doug is the late egg. He hatches, the older chick is already twice his
  size, and he "waits his turn" for food. Death 342, played flat, as a cut to the calendar page turning.

**9. The Spare** (the payoff promise, the boss)
- Facts to research: in most nests only one chick fledges (sourced rate). The second chick is described as an "insurance" or "backup" chick, raised
  only in case the first dies (cite Mullers & Amar 2015 or the source that uses the term; say "scientists call it" only if they do). The parents
  do not intervene when the older chick attacks the younger (cite). Wording must be exact: the parents **let** it die; they don't kill it.
- **Honesty line:** the shoebill does not hunt people (verify the wording against a source; if no source says it, say "there's no record of one
  ever killing a person" only if that is supported, otherwise cut). And the bird is in trouble itself: IUCN Vulnerable, a few thousand left,
  threatened by wetland loss and by people taking chicks for the illegal pet trade (cite IUCN / BirdLife).
- Scale analogy: the second chick is a spare tyre. You carry it everywhere and hope you never need it.
- Doug beat: Doug, still in the chick costume, is the spare. The older chick is fine, so he is not needed. The parents don't look up. Death 343,
  played flat, cut to black. No pecking on screen, no injured chick: the older chick is shown only as a big grey shape taking all the fish.
- Closing: the empty half of the nest with the cap in it. The Statue finally moves for the last time: it takes one step, looks at the cap, and
  leaves it there. Kicker: "The shoebill did not need the cap either. It already has a spare." Outro of 30 words or fewer: death counter plus one CTA.

## 7. Risks
- **Gore:** every strike is a cut to black or a cartoon swallow with X eyes on the costume. Decapitation of fish is one plain clause with no visual.
  Siblicide is never shown as violence: no pecking, no blood, no injured or dead chick on screen. The second chick is only ever Doug in a costume.
- **Kid appeal (HIGH: the shoebill is a meme bird and popular with kids, and the boss involves a baby bird):** no cute chick (the costume is grey,
  scruffy and deadpan, with Doug's own face), no big glossy eyes, no "angry bird" cartoon tone, no meme captions like "the bird that stares into
  your soul". Jokes stay dry and adult-coded. The siblicide is handled as plain biology with the narrator's flat tone, never as a sad-baby moment.
- **Safety advice (never approve):** nothing that tells viewers how to approach a shoebill, and no "they're harmless, go and bow to one" message.
- **Animal welfare:** the illegal pet trade is mentioned as a threat only, with no detail on how chicks are taken. No captive-bird footage style.
- **Myths to avoid:** "it's a stork" (debunk with the source), "it attacks people" (it doesn't), "it's a living dinosaur" (all birds are dinosaurs;
  use it only as a dry one-liner if at all), and the bow as a proven behaviour.
- **Facts that need strong sources:**
  - Height, weight and bill size (field measurements or a handbook such as HBW / Birds of the World).
  - Diet composition: lungfish, catfish, tilapia, monitors, water snakes, young crocodiles (Guillet 1979 or equivalent).
  - Lungfish cocoon survival (any reliable fish-biology source).
  - Standing-still durations and strike success, only if measured.
  - Water-carrying to cool eggs and chicks.
  - Clutch size, hatching asynchrony, siblicide and the "insurance chick" framing, fledging success (Mullers & Amar 2015 or equivalent).
  - Phylogeny: closer to pelicans than storks.
  - IUCN status and population range, the main threats (wetland loss, chick capture).
  - The Sudd's area.
  - The honesty line about people.
- **Pronunciation (editor):** *Balaeniceps rex* "bah-LEE-nih-seps REX", *Protopterus* "pro-TOP-ter-us", Sudd "sood", Mabamba "mah-BAHM-bah",
  Bangweulu "bang-WAY-oo-loo", *abu markub* "AH-boo mar-KOOB".
