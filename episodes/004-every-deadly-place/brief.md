## 2. Escalation axis: severity, slowest killer to fastest
The declared axis for the script header is **"Severity: how fast and how surely each place kills an unprotected
person, slowest to fastest."** It has three spoken section headers that work as zone shifts, and the backgrounds get hotter
and darker across the episode:
- **"Hours."** before item 1 (pale sand and daylight palette)
- **"Minutes."** before item 5 (the zone shift at about 38%; colder and darker)
- **"Seconds."** before item 9 (the darkest palette, ending in lava red on black)

**Sourcing rule for the axis:** a survival time is stated **only where a source gives it** (for example the time of useful
consciousness at altitude, the Naica crystal-cave limits, CO2 exposure limits, the duration of lightning). Everywhere
else the band comes from the mechanism, and the script never invents a number like "Doug would last 4 hours". The bands
are rough orders of magnitude, and the narration may say so once ("roughly").

**No safety advice.** The script states what kills and never tells the viewer how to survive it: no "if you ever get
stuck in quicksand, do X" and no hydration tips. Myth-busts are stated as science ("quicksand can't pull a person under"),
never as instructions.

Each item follows style bible 3.3: Name, Frame, Mechanism, Twist, Kicker, Doug beat. Every hypothesis or estimate gets
one honest framing word and nothing more.

### Hours.
**1. Death Valley** (the hook item)
- Research: the 134 °F / 56.7 °C record from July 1913 (WMO-recognised; note in one clause that it is disputed by some
  researchers, or use the 130 °F / 54.4 °C reading from 2020/2021, but pick one with a source); the record ground surface
  temperature (201 °F / 93.9 °C, Furnace Creek, 1972, NPS); summer nights that barely cool (sourced record warm low);
  how the body loses the race against heat with no water (one clinical line from a health or NPS source, no symptom list).
- Scale analogy: the ground is hotter than a cup of fresh coffee is meant to be served, or a hot ground "like a stovetop"
  (only the temperature needs a source).
- Doug beat: Doug arrives in flip-flops with one small bottle of water and a cheerful "nice". Death 28 (by the end:
  flat, X eyes, the cap shading his face).
- Twist (must land by 0:45, about 145 words): the ground is hotter than the air by about 70 °F, so the floor is the real
  killer. The writer may use a better sourced twist.

**2. Snake Island**
- Research: Ilha da Queimada Grande, Brazil; the endemic golden lancehead (*Bothrops insularis*), critically
  endangered; visits restricted by the Brazilian government (ICMBio / navy); population and density estimates (cite
  one sourced study, e.g. Martins et al. 2008 or a later survey, and **avoid the viral "one snake per square metre"
  claim** unless a source supports it, which it probably won't); the venom is considered very potent (sourced) and the
  snake hunts migratory birds, which is why the venom is fast (stated as a sourced hypothesis).
- Scale analogy: the island's area against something everyday (a sourced figure in football pitches or city blocks).
- Doug beat: Doug tries to befriend a golden lancehead ("hi friend?"). Death 29. **No bite wounds and no symptoms**: show
  the snake, the cut, the X eyes.

**3. Morecambe Bay**
- Research: England's largest intertidal sand flats; the quicksand and fast incoming tide (RNLI, Queen's/King's Guide
  to the Sands, sourced); Bonn et al. 2005, *Nature*: quicksand **cannot pull a person under**, because a human floats
  in it and only sinks to about the waist. Pulling a trapped foot out takes a large force (cite the paper or the Nature
  news piece for the "lifting a car" style comparison). **Do not mention the 2004 cockle pickers tragedy**: it is a
  labour-exploitation story with real victims and is off-lane.
- Scale analogy: the force needed to pull a foot free, compared with lifting a small car (only if the source supports it).
- Twist: the sand doesn't kill you; it just holds you in place for the tide.
- Doug beat: Doug is stuck to the waist and smug, because he watched a video that said quicksand can't sink him. He is
  right. Then the tide arrives. Death 30. The cap floats alone on the water.

**4. Lake Natron** (Doug survives)
- Research: Tanzania; highly alkaline soda lake (sourced pH, about 10.5 or higher) and hot water (sourced); the main
  breeding ground of lesser flamingos (sourced share); the famous "animals turned to stone" photos by Nick Brandt
  (2013) show birds that died and were later preserved by the salts and **posed by the photographer**. That is the
  myth-bust twist (source: Brandt's own interviews, e.g. NBC/Smithsonian).
- Scale analogy: the water is about as alkaline as ammonia or similar (only if a source gives the comparison).
- Doug beat: **costume gag** (the second use of the gag, after 002 and the 003 callback). Doug is zipped into a lesser
  flamingo costume "to blend in", cap on top, standing on one leg. The flamingos thrive here, and so does Doug. He
  survives with dry, cracked flip-flops. The narrator is quietly disappointed. No death (the counter stays at 30).

### Minutes.
**5. Antarctic Plateau** (zone shift, at about 38%)
- Research: the coldest measured air temperature, −89.2 °C at Vostok Station (1983, WMO); satellite-derived surface
  temperatures near −98 °C on the East Antarctic ridge (Scambos et al. 2018, *GRL*); high altitude plus dry air;
  frostbite onset time at extreme wind chill (sourced from a weather service chart, e.g. NWS). **Surface only**: nothing
  under the ice (that is backlog #29).
- Scale analogy: colder than a home freezer by about as much as a freezer is colder than a warm room (do the arithmetic
  from sourced figures).
- Doug beat: Doug brings sunscreen, because the last place was sunny. Death 31, frozen in the act of applying it.

**6. Everest Death Zone**
- Research: above about 8,000 m, the air holds roughly a third of the oxygen available at sea level (sourced); the
  "death zone" term; time of useful consciousness at that altitude for an unacclimatised person (FAA AC 61-107 or
  aviation physiology tables, about minutes); climbers survive there only after weeks of acclimatisation and/or with
  bottled oxygen (sourced). **Do not mention bodies on the mountain** or named climbers who died.
- Scale analogy: library asset `mount_everest.json`; the summit is about the cruising height of a passenger jet (sourced).
- Doug beat: Doug is dropped on the summit (no acclimatisation, which is the point of the axis), plants a tiny flag that
  says "DOUG", and has time to admire the view. Death 32.

**7. Cave of Crystals**
- Research: Naica, Mexico; gypsum crystals up to about 11-12 m long (sourced, e.g. García-Ruiz et al. 2007, *Geology*);
  air around 58 °C at close to 100% humidity; without cooling suits researchers could stay only about 10 minutes
  (sourced, e.g. National Geographic / Naica project); the cave flooded again when pumping stopped (sourced, 2015-2017).
  Mention why the humidity is the killer (sweat can't evaporate), as one physics line with no symptoms.
- Scale analogy: one crystal as long as a city bus or a telephone pole (sourced length).
- Twist: the most beautiful room on Earth gives you about ten minutes.
- Doug beat: Doug sets a 10-minute kitchen timer and sits on a crystal for a photo. The timer is optimistic. Death 33.

**8. Dallol**
- Research: Danakil Depression, Ethiopia; one of the hottest places on Earth by annual average (sourced); acid hot
  springs with **pH below zero**; Belilla et al. 2019, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*: some Dallol pools contain no
  active life at all (frame the debate honestly in one clause if needed); toxic gas pockets near the springs (sourced);
  the neon yellow and green colours (sulfur and iron salts).
- Scale analogy: the pools are more acidic than battery acid (only if sourced; otherwise "stronger than stomach acid"
  style, sourced).
- Twist: scientists went looking for life in the pools and found none. That is a rare result on Earth.
- Doug beat: Doug arrives with a magnifying glass, looking for life. He finds none, and briefly that includes him. Death 34.
  **Nothing dissolves on screen**: show the gas (green cloud), not the acid on Doug.

### Seconds.
**9. Lake Nyos** (the darkest beat; **zero jokes in this item**)
- Research: Cameroon; the limnic eruption of 21 August 1986; a cloud of CO2 killed about 1,700 people and thousands of
  livestock in a single night (USGS / Kling et al. 1987, *Science*; use one sourced figure); CO2 is heavier than air and
  flowed down valleys; degassing pipes installed since 2001 (sourced). One sentence: Lake Kivu holds far more gas (sourced)
  and sits next to millions of people.
- Scale analogy: the gas cloud flowing downhill like water poured onto a table, or its sourced depth/height against
  a house.
- Doug beat: **no joke**. Doug stands on the shore at night and lies down. The counter ticks to 35 in silence. The
  narration stays clinical and respectful toward the real victims. This is the one sombre beat; the deadpan resumes at
  the next name.

**10. Lake Maracaibo** (Doug survives)
- Research: Venezuela; Catatumbo lightning; the place with the most lightning on Earth (NASA LIS, Albrecht et al. 2016,
  *BAMS*, flashes per km² per year); storms on most nights of the year (sourced count); a lightning stroke lasts a
  fraction of a second (NOAA/NWS); **about 90% of people struck by lightning survive** (NWS). That is the twist.
- Scale analogy: the flash rate, as so many flashes per minute during a storm (sourced), or lightning hotter than the
  surface of the Sun (NWS gives about 5x).
- Doug beat: Doug is struck holding a fishing rod. He survives: smoking cap, hair sticking out from under it,
  "nope". The narrator is quietly disappointed. No death (counter stays 35). This is the clean comic release after Nyos.

**11. Nyiragongo** (the boss, the longest item)
- Research: DR Congo; long-lived lava lake, at times the largest on Earth (Smithsonian GVP); very fluid, low-silica
  lava, with the 1977 flank flows among the fastest recorded (about 60 km/h, sourced, USGS/GVP); the 2002 and 2021
  eruptions toward Goma (sourced, one sentence each, death counts only if sourced, no details); the "mazuku" CO2
  pockets in low ground nearby that kill animals (sourced), as a callback to Nyos.
- Scale analogy: lava faster than a car on a city road or a racehorse (sourced speed); the lake width against a
  stadium (sourced).
- Twist: the lava is not even the only thing trying to kill you; the low ground around the volcano can suffocate
  you too. Or: the lava runs downhill faster than most people can drive through town.
- Doug beat: Doug has seen marshmallows go badly before (003 callback) and brings a fire extinguisher **and** a
  marshmallow. Death 36. The final image is the cap resting on the cooled black crust at the crater rim, slightly smoking.
  The cap survives.
- Kicker: one deadpan line about the cap (or the lake being still there), then the outro.

**Death plan:** the counter is on screen from the first shot at **27** and ends at **36** (9 deaths; survivals at items 4 and 10).
By band: Hours 3, Minutes 4, Seconds 2.

## 8. Risks
- **Gore:** medium-low. Heat, acid, lava, venom and cold are shown on the environment, the costume and the cap,
  **never on Doug's body**: no burns, blisters, melting, dissolving, frostbite or bite wounds. Deaths are cartoon only
  (X eyes, flat pose, ghost Doug, the lone cap). Do not mention or show the dead flamingos at Natron or the Yellowstone/hot
  spring injury cases.
- **Real tragedies:** Lake Nyos (1986), Nyiragongo (2002, 2021). Use counts only, from a source, with no jokes within the
  item that mentions them, and no named victims. No Everest bodies. No Morecambe 2004.
- **Safety and medical advice:** none. There are no survival tips, no "what to do if", and no hydration or first-aid lines. The
  quicksand and lightning myth-busts are stated as research findings, not instructions.
- **Kid-appeal:** flamingo costume, snakes and lightning attract kids. Keep the voice deadpan and adult-coded (the
  10-minute kitchen timer, the smug quicksand expert, sunscreen in Antarctica). There are no mascot voices and no nursery words.
- **Political and travel sensitivities:** DR Congo, Ethiopia (Afar) and Venezuela have current conflict or crisis news.
  These are **geology only**: no politics, conflict or travel-safety commentary.
- **Viral myths to avoid or debunk with a source:** "one snake per square metre" on Snake Island; "Lake Natron turns
  animals to stone instantly"; "quicksand swallows people"; "the Death Valley record is undisputed".
- **Facts that need strong sources (cut the claim if it can't be sourced):** both Death Valley temperature records and the
  dispute; golden lancehead status, population estimate and access ban; Bonn et al. 2005 and the force comparison;
  Natron pH, the flamingo share and the Brandt photo process; the Vostok record and Scambos 2018; the Everest oxygen fraction
  and time of useful consciousness; Naica crystal size, conditions and limits; the Dallol pH and no-life finding;
  the Nyos death toll and mechanism; the Maracaibo flash density and the 90% survival figure; the Nyiragongo lava speed, the lake size
  and mazuku. Prefer primary papers, USGS, NPS, NOAA/NWS, WMO, NASA, the Smithsonian GVP and university press
  releases; avoid listicles.
- **Repetition and overlaps:** nothing from the deep sea (001), no parasites (002), no deep-time events (003). This
  episode absorbs backlog #7 ("fall into every deadly place") and touches #12 ("every way nature can kill"). The
  analyst should re-rank or merge those. Antarctica stays on the surface (#29 is under the ice).
- **Description:** fill at most 4,800 characters, with at least one institutional or primary source per item.
