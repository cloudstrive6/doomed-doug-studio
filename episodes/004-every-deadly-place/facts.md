# Facts: 004 Every Deadly Place

Every factual claim in script.md is listed here with its source. Doug, his deaths, the suitcase, the flamingo costume,
the narrator's asides and the jokes ("dinner can fly away", "wet concrete that has decided to keep you", "like a
professional", the kitchen timer, "briefly that includes him", the fire extinguisher and marshmallow) are fiction or
framing. Everyday comparisons that rest on common knowledge are marked "common knowledge" and checked by arithmetic.

Checked 2026-10-01. "Verified" = I read the quoted text on the page or in the abstract (Europe PMC API for paywalled
papers). "Search snippet" = the page returned 403 to WebFetch and the figure was confirmed through search-result text
quoting that page; the screener should re-open these if possible.

The severity bands ("Hours.", "Minutes.", "Seconds.") are framing, flagged once as "roughly" in the opener. No invented
survival times: the only stated times are the Naica visit limits, the FAA time of useful consciousness, the NWS frostbite
chart and the length of a lightning stroke, each sourced below.

## Opener
- "Eleven places on Earth can kill anyone who stays. We ranked them, roughly, by how fast." Framing of the episode's
  severity axis (brief section 2). Draft 2 adds "can" (screener F3): each place is capable of killing an unprotected

## Death Valley
Source: NPS, Death Valley "Weather and Climate": https://www.nps.gov/deva/learn/nature/weather-and-climate.htm (verified)
- "The hottest air temperature ever recorded in Death Valley (Furnace Creek) was 134°F (57°C) on July 10, 1913."
  Script: July tenth, 1913, 134 F, about 57 C.
- "The highest ground temperature recorded was 201° F at Furnace Creek on July 15, 1972." and "The maximum air
  temperature for that day was 128° F." Script: 201 F ground, 128 F air, 73 degrees difference (201 − 128 = 73).
- "overnight lows may only dip into the 85°F to 95°F (30°C to 35°C) range." Script: "may only drop to between
  eighty-five and ninety-five degrees".
- Average July high 116 F (NPS climate table). Script: "a normal July afternoon here averages about one hundred and sixteen."
- Water boils at 212 F at sea level (common knowledge); 212 − 201 = 11.
- Official world record: WMO recognizes 134 F at Greenland Ranch, Death Valley, 10 July 1913. WMO archive of weather and
  climate extremes: https://wmo.asu.edu/ (record entry "World: Highest Temperature"); reported in
  https://pvtimes.com/news/death-valley-heat-record-recorded-in-1913-being-disputed/ (search snippet).
- Dispute: Spencer, Christy and Reid, Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society, 2025: the 10 July 1913 high was
  "likely 12-16 degrees cooler than the reported 134-degree reading"; the record stays official unless the National
  Climate Extremes Committee and WMO rule otherwise. https://weather.com/news/weather/news/2025-11-19-death-valley-world-record-1913-study
  (verified); AMS summary https://headlines.ametsoc.org/2025/11/14/global-record-in-question-could-death-valley-lose-its-claim-to-worlds-hottest-place/
  (403, search snippet). Script: "A study in two thousand twenty-five argued that the reading was a mistake and that the
  real high was closer to one hundred and twenty, but for now the record still stands." (Draft 2, screener F2: no
  official review has been announced, so the script no longer claims one.)
- Sweating cools the body; evaporation is the cooling process. NWS Amarillo, "What is the heat index?":
  https://www.weather.gov/ama/heatindex (search snippet: "When the body gets too hot, it begins to perspire or sweat to
  cool itself off... Evaporation is a cooling process."). Script: "Your body cools itself by sweating, and sweat is made of water."
- "The real heater in Death Valley is the floor, which soaks up sunlight all day and gives it back from below." Framing
  that follows from the NPS ground-versus-air figures (ground 73 F hotter than the air).

## Everest Death Zone
- Summit 8,848.86 m (2020 joint Nepal-China survey); NatGeo https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/mount-everest-height-officially-changes
  Script: eight thousand eight hundred and forty-nine meters.
- Empire State Building roof height 381 m (1,250 ft), common reference figure (https://www.esbnyc.com/about/facts-figures).
  8,849 / 381 ≈ 23.2. Script: about twenty-three stacked.
- "Death zone" above about 8,000 m: climbers' term. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8287323/ ("Death Zone
  Weather Extremes Mountaineers Have Experienced in Successful Ascents", Frontiers in Physiology 2021, title verified).
- Summit air pressure about one third of sea level: West 1999, "Barometric pressures on Mt. Everest: new data and
  physiological significance", J Appl Physiol, PMID 10066724 (abstract verified via Europe PMC): "the summit pressure is
  251-253 Torr"; sea level standard is 760 Torr, 253/760 = 0.33. Oxygen fraction of air is the same at all heights (about
  21%), so inspired oxygen scales with pressure. Script: "about a third of what it is at sea level, so every breath
  brings in roughly a third of the oxygen"; "the same mix of gases as at the beach, there's just much less of it."
- Grocott et al. 2009, "Arterial blood gases and oxygen content in climbers on Mount Everest", NEJM
  https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa0801581 (abstract verified via Europe PMC): "The level of environmental
  hypobaric hypoxia that affects climbers at the summit of Mount Everest (8848 m) is close to the limit of tolerance by
  humans." and "The hemoglobin concentration increased" during the ascent. Script: blood carried more and more of the
  oxygen-carrying protein on the way up; "close to the limit of what a human can tolerate."
- Supplemental oxygen use by climbers: University of Washington release on Huey et al. 2020:
  https://www.washington.edu/news/2020/08/26/mount-everest-summit-success-rates-double-death-rate-stays-the-same-over-last-30-years/
  (verified: "Some climbers are using elevated flow rates of supplemental oxygen"). Script: "many climbers also breathe bottled oxygen."
- Time of useful consciousness: FAA Advisory Circular 61-107B, table "Times of useful consciousness versus altitude":
  https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_61-107B_CHG_1.pdf (search snippet of the table):
  25,000 ft 3-5 min; 28,000 ft 2.5-3 min; 30,000 ft 1-2 min (gradual ascent). Everest's summit is 29,032 ft, between
  the 28,000 and 30,000 ft rows, so 1-3 min. Script: "somewhere between one and three minutes." (Draft 2, screener F1;
  draft 1 had skipped the 28,000 ft row.) The term's definition ("the time an individual can function effectively in
  an oxygen-poor environment before losing the ability to take proper corrective action") is from the same circular.
- No bodies on the mountain and no named climbers (brief rule).
