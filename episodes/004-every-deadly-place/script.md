## Death Valley

Death Valley.

This long, dry basin in California holds the official world record for the hottest air ever measured.

## Snake Island

Snake Island.

## Morecambe Bay

Morecambe Bay.

On the northwest coast of England, the tide goes out twice a day and leaves about three hundred and ten square kilometers of sand and mud behind. It looks like the biggest, flattest beach in the country, and on a sunny day it looks completely harmless.

[Wide shot: endless tan flats, a thin line of sea on the horizon.]

People have crossed these sands on foot for a very long time, and the bay even has an official King's Guide to the Sands, a local expert who leads walkers across. The local museum service lists the most common causes of accidents out here: quicksand, fog, and the speed of the incoming tide.

Quicksand is a mix of fine sand, clay and salt water, and if you stand still, it can feel almost solid. Start wriggling, and it suddenly behaves like a liquid, so you sink. Then the sand around your legs settles to the bottom and packs tight, like wet concrete that has decided to keep you.

[Cross-section: Doug's legs in the sand, little arrows showing the grains packing in.]

Here's the twist. Quicksand cannot actually swallow you.

In two thousand five, physicists at the University of Amsterdam tested it in the lab. The average human body is about half as dense as quicksand, so a person sinks to about the waist and then floats, and nobody vanishes with one hand waving.

Getting out is another story. According to the same team, pulling one leg free takes about the same force as lifting a small car.

## Lake Natron

Lake Natron.

In northern Tanzania, near the border with Kenya, there's a shallow lake with a reputation online for turning any animal that touches it into stone.

[A flat, crusted lake under a hazy sky. Label: "pH 10.5".]

The water is loaded with dissolved sodium carbonate, which makes it strongly alkaline. On the scale chemists use, it measures between nine and ten and a half, not far from household ammonia at about eleven. In places, the water can also reach sixty degrees Celsius, while a hot tub is never supposed to go above forty.

For most animals, this is a place to avoid. For one bird, it's home.

[Thousands of pink flamingos. Label: "75%".]

About three quarters of the world's lesser flamingos are born here. The adults have tough, leathery skin on their legs, so the water doesn't bother them much, and they build their nests on islands of salt crust. The caustic water around those islands works like a moat that predators can't cross.

## Antarctic Plateau

Antarctic Plateau.

In the middle of East Antarctica, the ice sheet rises into a high, flat plateau more than three and a half kilometers above sea level. This is where Earth keeps its coldest air.

[Endless white plain under a dark sky. A tiny station hut. Label: "Vostok".]

On July twenty-first, nineteen eighty-three, Vostok Station recorded minus eighty-nine point two degrees Celsius, which is still the official record for the coldest air ever measured on the ground.

[Three thermometers side by side: kitchen, home freezer, Vostok.]

For scale, a home freezer is set to about minus eighteen, so your kitchen is about forty degrees warmer than your freezer. Vostok was about seventy degrees colder than it.

However, Vostok was not even the coldest spot.

[Satellite view. Small dark hollows along a ridge glow deep purple. Label: "-98°C".]

Here's the worst part. Satellites mapped the snow surface along a high ridge between two ice domes, Dome Argus and Dome Fuji, and in winter, small hollows there reached about minus ninety-eight degrees Celsius. The recipe is clear skies and extremely dry air, which let the snow's heat escape straight into space. The cold, heavy air then slides into the hollows, gets trapped and keeps cooling.

The National Weather Service has a chart for how fast bare skin freezes, and it only goes down to about minus forty-three Celsius. At the coldest corner of that chart, the answer is five minutes. Even the air in these hollows, at around minus ninety-four, is more than fifty degrees colder than the bottom of the chart.

## Everest Death Zone

Everest Death Zone.

The summit of Mount Everest is eight thousand eight hundred and forty-nine meters above sea level, about the height of twenty-three Empire State Buildings stacked on top of each other.

[Mount Everest, a dotted line marked "8,000 m" across the upper slopes. Label: "Death Zone".]

Above about eight thousand meters, climbers call it the death zone. The air up here has the same mix of gases as at the beach, there's just much less of it. At the summit, the air pressure is about a third of what it is at sea level, so every breath brings in roughly a third of the oxygen.

People do stand here, but they get here slowly, over a long climb that gives the body time to adjust. Researchers who measured climbers on Everest found that their blood carried more and more of the oxygen-carrying protein on the way up, and many climbers also breathe bottled oxygen. Even then, scientists describe the summit as close to the limit of what a human can tolerate.

Here's the problem. Pilots have a number for this, called the time of useful consciousness, which is how long a person has to act sensibly once the oxygen runs short. After that, nobody gets to decide anything.

## Cave of Crystals

Cave of Crystals.

[A huge cavern crossed by pale, glowing beams. Doug tiny in the corner.]

[Thermometer and a humidity meter side by side, both near the top.]

The air in the crystal chambers sits around fifty degrees Celsius or more, and the humidity is around ninety percent or higher. That second number is the real killer.

Your body sheds heat by evaporating sweat off your skin, but when the air is already that full of water, sweat barely evaporates, so the cooling system simply stops working. The air is also hotter than you are, so it heats you up instead of cooling you down.

## Dallol

Dallol.

In the Danakil Depression of northern Ethiopia, more than a hundred meters below sea level, there's a field of pools colored neon yellow, green and orange.

[Bright yellow and green pools, salt chimneys, a white crust. Then a thermometer. Label: "34.5°C average".]

Dallol holds the world record for the highest average yearly temperature ever measured, about thirty-four and a half degrees Celsius, recorded in the nineteen sixties. That is the average, including every night.

Underneath, hot water rises through thick layers of salt over a body of magma, and it comes up as boiling brine, saturated with salt and loaded with iron. The iron, along with sulfur and other minerals, paints the pools those impossible colors.

On the acidity scale, lemon juice is about two, and each step down is ten times stronger. These pools measure around zero, or even below it, so they are more than a hundred times more acidic than lemon juice.

[Green gas haze drifting low over the pools. Yellow warning triangle.]

The springs also give off acidic gases, including carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide and hydrogen sulfide.

Here's the strange part. Scientists came here looking for life, and in the worst pools, they didn't find any.

Life on Earth turns up almost anywhere there's liquid water. But in two thousand nineteen, a French and Spanish team reported no microbial life in the hottest, saltiest, most acidic pools, or in the magnesium-rich brine lakes next to them. In the desert and the salty canyons around the site, life was doing fine. Another team has reported tiny microbes in the pools, so the argument is not completely settled.

[Doug crouches over a pool with a giant magnifying glass. Hopeful smile.]

Doug has brought a magnifying glass to help the search for life. He doesn't find any, and briefly that includes him.

## Lake Nyos

Lake Nyos.

Lake Nyos is a deep crater lake in the hills of northwest Cameroon, and on August twenty-first, nineteen eighty-six, it released a huge cloud of gas.

[A calm lake in green hills, night sky. No labels yet.]

Deep below the surface, the water had been collecting carbon dioxide from magma under the lake, and the weight of the water above kept it dissolved, like a sealed bottle of soda.

That day, the balance broke. It all came out at once.

[A pale cloud spilling over the lip of the crater and running downhill.]

Carbon dioxide is about one and a half times as dense as air, so instead of drifting away, it hugs the ground and flows downhill like water poured across a table.

At least one thousand seven hundred people died, along with thousands of livestock. The scientists who investigated found that the gas was carbon dioxide from deep in the lake, not a volcanic eruption.

[A raft in the middle of the lake with a thin pipe and a small fountain.]

The most unsettling part is that the gas kept building up again after the disaster. Since two thousand one, pipes in the lake have been lifting deep water to the surface so the gas escapes slowly, a little at a time.

Lake Kivu, between Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, holds hundreds of times as much gas, even by the most cautious estimates, and around two million people live in the region.

## Lake Maracaibo

Lake Maracaibo.

Lake Maracaibo in northwestern Venezuela gets more lightning than anywhere else on Earth, and it isn't close.

[A wide lake at night under towering storm clouds. Flash after flash.]

A sensor aboard a NASA satellite counted lightning flashes from orbit for sixteen years, and in two thousand sixteen, scientists used that record to rank every lightning hotspot on the planet. Lake Maracaibo came out on top, with more than two hundred and thirty-three flashes per square kilometer per year. That's more than one flash a year over every soccer field. On average, it gets thunderstorms on two hundred and ninety-seven nights a year.

The recipe is local. On most nights, winds off the mountains, the lake and the sea meet over the warm water, push humid air up, and build storms over the same spot. The flashes are so regular that the storms have their own name, the Catatumbo lightning.

[Label: "50,000°F". A small sun next to a lightning bolt, the bolt five times bigger.]

Lightning heats the air around it to about fifty thousand degrees Fahrenheit, which is about five times hotter than the surface of the Sun. The main stroke is over in a few thousandths of a second.

## Nyiragongo

Nyiragongo.

Nyiragongo is a volcano in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, about twelve kilometers north of the city of Goma. For long stretches of time, its summit crater has held something rare: a lake of liquid lava.

[A steep cone. Then the view down into the crater: a glowing red lake with a black, cracking skin. Label: "230 m".]

The summit crater is about one point two kilometers wide. The lake inside it has stretched about two hundred and thirty meters across, roughly two soccer fields end to end. London's Natural History Museum calls it the world's largest continuously active lava lake.

Most lava flows move slowly enough that people can walk away from them. Nyiragongo's lava is different. It is very low in silica, and less silica means runnier lava, so this lava flows almost like water. It is often called the fastest-flowing lava on Earth.

[Side view of the volcano. The lava lake drains through cracks on the side.]

In January nineteen seventy-seven, the lava lake drained in less than an hour through cracks on the side of the mountain. Those flows moved at up to sixty kilometers an hour, faster than cars are allowed to drive through most towns.

[A lava lake bubbling again. Label: "Still here".]

And each time the lake has drained, it has eventually filled back up.

Here's the part most people never hear about: the lava isn't the only thing that kills around this volcano.

[Low hollow in the forest, a few dead birds and insects as small grey shapes. Invisible gas shown as a faint green layer hugging the ground.]

In the low ground around Nyiragongo and Goma, carbon dioxide seeps up through cracks in the rock, and locals call these spots mazuku, which means evil wind. It's the same gas as at Lake Nyos, with no lake needed. Because it is heavier than air, it collects in hollows, usually in the lowest meter of air, and stays there. Dead animals are the usual warning sign, because insects, birds, goats and dogs walk in and don't walk out.

So the volcano has two ways to get you: a glowing lake on the summit that can outrun traffic, and an invisible puddle at the bottom of the hill.
