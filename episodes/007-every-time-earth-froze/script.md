## Year Without a Summer

Year Without a Summer.

June in Vermont should be green, warm and slightly boring, but in eighteen sixteen it was snowing.

[A New England farm in June, green fields, snow falling on them. Label: "JUNE 1816".]

On the sixth of June, a storm dropped snow across New England, and two days later the snow in Cabot, Vermont, was eighteen inches deep. That's about the height of a kitchen chair seat.

Here's the twist. Nothing in Vermont caused it.

The cause was a volcano on the far side of the planet, and it had erupted more than a year earlier.

[Globe spins to Indonesia. A volcano explodes. Dial label: "APRIL 1815".]

In April eighteen fifteen, Mount Tambora, on the island of Sumbawa in what is now Indonesia, blew apart in the largest eruption in recorded history. It threw out something like a hundred cubic kilometers of rock and ash, which is enough to fill a cube nearly five kilometers tall on every side.

## Frost Fairs

Frost Fairs.

The River Thames in London is wide, tidal and far too busy to freeze, yet between the early sixteen hundreds and the early eighteen hundreds it froze over about twenty-four times.

[London, grey sky. The Thames frozen white between bridges. FROZEN globe: a slightly bigger white cap.]

This was the Little Ice Age, a long cold stretch when temperatures dropped by about half a degree Celsius. That sounds tiny. It was enough.

When the ice got thick enough, Londoners did the obvious thing and built a street on it, and these were called frost fairs. The great freeze of sixteen eighty-three lasted about two months, with booths, bowling and printing presses set up right on the river.

The presses were the hit. For a small fee, a printer would set your name in type, add the date, and print you a card saying it was made on the Thames.

## Doggerland

Doggerland.

Today, the North Sea sits between Britain and the Netherlands, but during the last ice age there was no sea there at all, just hills, rivers, marshes and people.

[Map: Britain joined to Europe by a wide green land. Label: "DOGGERLAND". FROZEN globe cap grows.]

So much water was locked up in ice sheets that sea level was about a hundred and twenty meters lower than today, which is roughly the height of a forty-story building. The floor of the North Sea was dry land, and archaeologists call it Doggerland.

Then the ice started melting, sea level climbed, and Doggerland shrank. Over thousands of years, it broke into low islands, then a single island, then little more than a sandbank.

[Animation: the green land shrinks to islands, then one island.]

About eight thousand one hundred and fifty years ago, off the coast of Norway, the seabed gave way. The Storegga Slide was an underwater landslide roughly the size of Scotland, and it moved about three thousand two hundred cubic kilometers of sediment and sent a tsunami across the North Sea.

[A slab of seabed slides downhill. A wave rolls out across the map.]

On the Scottish coast, the wave left sand up to five meters above the sea, and the real height may have been several meters more. People lived along these coasts, and the wave reached them too.

But the wave isn't what finished Doggerland. A study in twenty twenty found that what was left of it drowned slowly, as the sea kept creeping up. The ice melting is what killed Doggerland.

The end of an ice age can be just as dangerous as the ice age.

## The Younger Dryas

The Younger Dryas.

About fourteen and a half thousand years ago, the ice age was finally ending, the planet was warming, the ice sheets were melting, and things were looking up.

[Dial: "12,900 YEARS AGO". Green shoots on a thawing plain.]

Then the Northern Hemisphere fell straight back into near-glacial cold.

This cold snap is called the Younger Dryas, and it lasted roughly twelve hundred years. It's named after a small white tundra flower, Dryas octopetala, that does well in the cold and became common across Europe while the cold lasted.

[A small white flower with eight petals pops up in the snow. Label: "DRYAS".]

The leading explanation starts with meltwater. As the great North American ice sheet melted, much of its fresh water is thought to have been rerouted toward the North Atlantic, away from the Mississippi. Fresh water is lighter than salt water, so it can sit on top of the ocean and slow down the currents that carry heat north.

[A big ocean conveyor arrow across the Atlantic slows down and turns blue.]

Europe essentially lost its central heating, and down in Antarctica, temperatures actually went up while the north froze.

The ending was even stranger. Greenland's ice cores record a jump of about ten degrees Celsius in roughly a decade, which is about the gap between the inside of a fridge and a chilly kitchen. Layered lake mud in Europe shows the same sudden flip.

Someone could have watched the whole switch before turning twenty.

## The Missoula Floods

The Missoula Floods.

About eighteen thousand to fifteen thousand years ago, a tongue of ice slid south out of Canada and blocked a river valley in northern Idaho, and the river had nowhere to go.

[Map of the American northwest. A white ice lobe plugs a valley. Water pools behind it.]

It backed up into Montana and became Glacial Lake Missoula. Behind the ice dam, the water rose about six hundred and ten meters deep, which is deeper than Toronto's CN Tower is tall. At its fullest, the lake held about as much water as Lake Erie and Lake Ontario combined.

Ice dams are not built to last. When this one failed, the whole lake emptied in about two days.

[The dam bursts. A wall of water races west across Washington State.]

A wall of water raced across Washington State at around a hundred kilometers an hour, carrying about ten times the flow of all the world's rivers today. It stripped the soil away and carved a maze of dry canyons and bare rock called the Channeled Scablands.

Here's the problem. It didn't happen once.

The ice kept sliding back, the dam kept rebuilding, and the lake kept refilling, so over thousands of years the floods happened dozens of times.

The first geologist to say a giant flood made this landscape was J Harlen Bretz, in nineteen twenty-three, and other geologists dismissed him for decades. In nineteen seventy-nine, he finally received the highest award of the Geological Society of America.

## The Last Glacial Maximum

The Last Glacial Maximum.

## Antarctica Freezes

Antarctica Freezes.

Antarctica has not always been a frozen desert. About fifty-three million years ago, palm trees grew along its coast, and summer days reached around twenty-five degrees Celsius.

[Dial: "53 MILLION YEARS AGO". Antarctica, green, palm trees on the beach, beech trees on the hills.]

Inland, the hills were covered in beech trees and conifers. Then the carbon dioxide in the air slowly fell. One study found it dropped by about forty percent in the three million years before the ice arrived.

Around thirty-four million years ago, at the boundary between two epochs called the Eocene and the Oligocene, Antarctica crossed a line. The prime suspect is carbon dioxide dropping below a threshold, with a wobble in Earth's orbit giving the continent summers too cold to melt the winter snow.

[Dial: "34 MILLION YEARS AGO". Snow falls on the beech trees and doesn't melt.]

Opening seaways around Antarctica may have helped too, though how much is still argued about.

The snow stopped melting, and year after year it piled up and pressed itself into an ice sheet.

The air had taken millions of years to cool, but the ice itself moved in fast. Seafloor mud from the Pacific records it arriving in two quick jumps, all within a few hundred thousand years.

Today, that ice sheet is about two kilometers thick on average, which is around five Empire State Buildings stacked up. At its thickest, it reaches almost five kilometers.

[Cross-section of today's ice sheet with five little Empire State Buildings stacked beside it.]

What nobody tells you is that this freeze never really ended. The ice has grown and shrunk since then, but the continent has stayed glaciated the whole time, so the cold that started thirty-four million years ago is still here.

## The Karoo Ice Age

The Karoo Ice Age.

About three hundred million years ago, most of Earth's land was crowded together, and a giant southern continent called Gondwana sat over the South Pole.

[Dial: "300 MILLION YEARS AGO". A huge continent with a white ice cap over its southern half. FROZEN globe: half white.]

Ice sheets spread across land that is now South America, southern Africa, India, Antarctica and Australia, and the rocks they left behind in South Africa's Karoo region gave this ice age its name. It was the longest-lived ice age of the last half a billion years, and it came and went for tens of millions of years.

Meanwhile, nearer the equator, it was a jungle. Huge swamp forests grew in the tropics, and the air may have held far more oxygen than it does today.

Insects got big. Meganeura, a giant relative of the dragonfly, had a wingspan of about seventy centimeters, which is almost as wide as a front door.

[A dragonfly the size of a door hovers over a fern swamp. A normal front door drawn next to it for scale.]

Here's the catch. Those forests were helping to freeze the planet.

Every tree pulls carbon dioxide out of the air to build its wood. When these swamp trees died, many of them didn't rot away, and they got buried, squashed and slowly turned into coal, with the carbon still locked inside.

[Cross-section: trees fall, get buried, turn into a black coal layer. The carbon dioxide arrows point down into the ground.]

Most of the coal that later powered the Industrial Revolution formed during this time. One climate study found that all that buried carbon brought Earth close to freezing over completely, about three hundred million years ago. Mountain building and rock weathering helped pull carbon dioxide down as well.

## The Marinoan Snowball

The Marinoan Snowball.

Around six hundred and forty million years ago, long before the first big animals, Earth did something that sounds impossible, and froze from the poles almost to the equator.

[Dial: "640 MILLION YEARS AGO". The FROZEN globe is completely white.]

The clue is in the rocks. Glacial deposits from this time turn up in places that sat close to the equator back then, within about ten degrees of it, which is like glaciers reaching the sea in Costa Rica.

In nineteen ninety-two, the geologist Joseph Kirschvink gave the idea its name: Snowball Earth.

[Earth as a white ball with a few dark rock patches. Label: "SNOWBALL EARTH?"]

Once enough of the planet is covered in ice, the ice reflects sunlight back into space, which makes it colder, which makes more ice. Scientists still argue about whether the oceans froze over completely, or whether a slushy band of open water survived near the equator.

The escape route was volcanoes.

With the planet frozen, rain and the weathering of rock almost stopped, so nothing was pulling carbon dioxide out of the air, and the volcanoes kept breathing it out anyway.

[Volcanoes poke through the ice, puffing grey clouds. A gauge labelled "CARBON DIOXIDE" climbs and climbs.]

It built up for millions of years, to an estimated three hundred and fifty times the modern level, until the ice finally gave way and the planet swung into an extreme greenhouse. The carbon dioxide washed into the sea and turned into thick layers of limestone, called cap carbonates, that sit right on top of the glacier rubble all over the world.

The thaw was worse than the freeze.

[Doug in a giant puffy parka, hood up, cap on top of the hood, standing proudly on the ice. Then the ice melts away and the sun turns orange. Sweat drops fly.]

## The Sturtian Snowball

The Sturtian Snowball.

Fifty-seven million years of ice sounds like plenty of time to get used to it.

[Dial: "717 MILLION YEARS AGO". White globe, colder blue shadows. Frost thick on the time machine.]

Most dates put it at about seven hundred and seventeen to six hundred and sixty million years ago, which is where that fifty-seven million comes from. The dinosaurs died out sixty-six million years ago, so this freeze lasted almost as long as all the time since then.

[Timeline: a long white bar labelled "STURTIAN" next to a slightly longer bar labelled "SINCE THE DINOSAURS".]

Researchers at Harvard think it started when a huge spread of volcanic rock in what's now northern Canada weathered in the rain, pulling carbon dioxide out of the air. They also suggest it may not have been one long freeze, but several, with hot spells in between.

On a Snowball Earth, average global temperatures may have been around minus fifty degrees Celsius, which is about thirty degrees colder than a home freezer.

But that's not the strange part. Life made it through.

## The First Snowball

The First Snowball.

Go back about two point four billion years, more than half of Earth's history, and the planet doesn't look like home. There are no plants, no animals and almost no oxygen.

[Dial: "2,400,000,000 YEARS AGO". An orange-brown hazy sky over a bare rocky coast. Big scene globe in the corner of the shot: dark, almost no white. The corner FROZEN meter stays fully white (it never goes backwards).]

What the planet did have was methane, a much stronger greenhouse gas than carbon dioxide, keeping it warm. It also had cyanobacteria, tiny microbes that had learned to use sunlight to split water, releasing oxygen as waste.

[Green-blue scum in the shallows, tiny bubbles labelled "O2" rising out of it.]

Oxygen started building up in the air, in what scientists call the Great Oxidation Event.

Here's the disturbing part. Oxygen reacts with methane.

As the oxygen rose, it destroyed the methane, turning it into carbon dioxide and water, which trap far less heat. The planet's blanket got thinner.

[The orange haze clears. Frost creeps in from the edges of the frame.]

Earth cooled into a series of glaciations that came and went for around three hundred million years. Their rocks are exposed today in Ontario, near Lake Huron, which is why they're called the Huronian glaciations.

Some geologists think at least one of them covered the whole planet, possibly the first Snowball Earth ever. Others see milder spells in between. A two thousand and five study by researchers at Caltech went further. It argued that the oxygen makers later tipped Earth into a full snowball, recorded in rocks in South Africa, and that it could have happened in as little as a million years.

The worst part is who did it. No volcano. No comet. Life froze the planet.

The first living things that learned to make oxygen were a disaster for everything that couldn't handle it.
