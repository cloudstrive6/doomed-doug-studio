## 2. Escalation axis: age, youngest to oldest (and colder the further back)

**Recurring visual (for the art director to approve):** a small **"FROZEN" globe icon** in a corner, next to the death

### Cold snaps.
**1. Year Without a Summer** (1816; the hook item)
- Research: Mount Tambora (Sumbawa, Indonesia) erupted in April 1815, the largest eruption in recorded history (VEI 7);
  sulfate aerosols cooled the Northern Hemisphere; snow fell in New England and Quebec in June 1816, with frosts in July and August;
  crops failed and famine followed in Europe; Mary Shelley began *Frankenstein* that cold, wet summer at Lake Geneva.
  Sources: Smithsonian GVP, USGS, NOAA, Oppenheimer 2003 (*Progress in Physical Geography*).
- Scale analogy: the volume of rock Tambora threw out, compared with something everyday (use the sourced figure only).
- Twist (aim for **25-35 s**, the 006 note; the hard limit is 45 s / 145 words): the June snow was caused by a volcano on the
  other side of the planet that erupted **a year earlier**.
- Doug beat: Doug steps out of the time machine into Vermont in June with a deck chair and sunscreen. Snow. Death 57 (frozen in the deck chair, X eyes, cap on top).
  **No jokes about the famine or its victims**; Doug dies of cold, nobody else is the punchline.

**2. Frost Fairs** (Doug survives)
- Research: during the Little Ice Age the Thames in London froze over several times (1683-84 Great Frost; last frost fair
  February 1814); stalls, printing presses printing souvenir name cards on the ice, and in 1814 an elephant led across
  the ice below Blackfriars Bridge; the old London Bridge's narrow arches slowed the river, which helped it freeze; the 1814 fair
  ended within days when the thaw came. Sources: Museum of London, Historic England, Royal Museums Greenwich, Met Office.
- Scale analogy: the thickness of the ice or the length of the fair street, against something everyday (sourced).
- Twist: the river froze partly because of a **bridge**. When the old bridge was replaced (1831), the frost fairs never came back.
- Doug beat: Doug has his name printed on a souvenir card on the ice and gets off just before the thaw. He survives.
  "Doug is fine." The card ("DOUG, PRINTED ON THE THAMES") is the visual kicker. Counter stays at 57.

### Ice age.
**3. Doggerland** (about 8,200 years ago)
- Research: during the last ice age, sea level was about 120 m lower and a land bridge (Doggerland) joined Britain to Europe;
  the rising sea drowned it; the Storegga Slide off Norway (about 8,150 years ago) was one of the largest known underwater
  landslides, and its tsunami hit Shetland, Scotland and Norway. Sources: University of Bradford "Europe's Lost Frontiers",
  Walker et al. 2020 (*Antiquity*), BGS.
- Scale analogy: the Storegga Slide's area or volume against a country or everyday object (sourced; check whether "about the
  size of Iceland" holds before using it).
- Twist: the ice melting is what killed Doggerland. The end of the freeze is as dangerous as the freeze.
- Doug beat: Doug walks from England to the Netherlands ("shortcut"). Death 58 (cartoon wave; the cap floats). Mesolithic people
  are never the joke.

**4. The Younger Dryas** (about 12,900 years ago)
- Research: the world was warming out of the ice age when the Northern Hemisphere snapped back to near-glacial cold for
  about 1,200 years; Greenland ice cores show the end came abruptly, with a large warming in Greenland within decades
  (Alley 2000, *Quaternary Science Reviews*; NOAA paleoclimate); it is named after *Dryas octopetala*, a tundra flower.
  The leading cause is a slowdown of the Atlantic overturning circulation; the impact hypothesis is disputed, so **don't present it as fact**.
- Scale analogy: the speed of the change against a human lifetime (sourced).
- Twist: it ended faster than it started, possibly within one human lifetime.
- Doug beat: Doug befriends the Dryas flower ("hi friend?"). Death 59, frozen mid-sniff. The flower survives (it's a tundra flower).

**5. The Missoula Floods** (about 18,000-15,000 years ago)
- Research: an ice dam blocked Glacial Lake Missoula in Montana; it failed again and again (dozens of times), emptying
  the lake across Washington State in days and carving the Channeled Scablands; peak flow roughly ten times all the world's
  rivers combined (NPS / USGS wording; use theirs); geologist J Harlen Bretz proposed the flood in the 1920s and was
  rejected for decades. Sources: NPS Ice Age Floods National Geologic Trail, USGS, Montana Natural History Center.
- Scale analogy: the lake's volume against a Great Lake (sourced), or the dam's height against a skyscraper.
- Twist: this didn't happen once. It happened **dozens of times**, and the first scientist to say so was laughed at for decades.
- Doug beat: Doug stands on top of the ice dam with a fishing rod. Death 60. A fast-forward gag shows the dam rebuilt and Doug back on top, but it counts as one death
  (the narrator refuses to count the repeats; one dry line).

**6. The Last Glacial Maximum** (about 26,000-20,000 years ago)
- Research: ice sheets covered most of Canada and the northern US (the Laurentide Ice Sheet, up to about 3 km thick at its
  centre), plus Scandinavia and northern Britain; sea level was about 120 m lower; the sites of Chicago and New York were under or at the ice
  edge (sourced). Sources: NOAA, USGS, Dalton et al. 2020 (*Quaternary Science Reviews*), NSIDC.
- Scale analogy: the ice thickness against stacked Eiffel Towers or the Empire State Building (sourced thickness only).
- Twist: the ice was so heavy that it pushed the land down, and some places (Hudson Bay, Scandinavia) are **still rising** today.
- Doug beat: Doug plants a flag on the ice "above Chicago". Death 61 (crevasse; the cap stays on the rim).

### Deep time.
**7. Antarctica Freezes** (about 34 million years ago; zone shift)
- Research: before this, Antarctica had forests (fossil evidence; Southern Beech, *Nothofagus*); at the Eocene-Oligocene
  transition, around 34 million years ago, the permanent ice sheet formed quickly in geologic terms as CO2 fell, with
  ocean gateways opening also a factor (Drake Passage; contested weighting); global cooling and extinctions followed.
  Sources: DeConto & Pollard 2003 (*Nature*), British Antarctic Survey, Smithsonian.
- Scale analogy: the volume or height of today's Antarctic ice sheet against something everyday (sourced).
- Twist: the ice that is on Antarctica today started here, so **it has never melted since**.
- Doug beat: Doug picnics in an Antarctic forest. Death 62. Callback allowed once: "He has been to Antarctica before. It went the same way." (004)

**8. The Karoo Ice Age** (about 360-260 million years ago)
- Research: the Late Paleozoic Ice Age, the longest of the last 500 million years; ice sheets across southern Gondwana; at the same time,
  coal swamps and giant insects (*Meganeura*, wingspan about 70 cm) lived nearer the equator, with high oxygen
  levels; a leading explanation is that land plants pulled CO2 out of the air and much of it was buried as coal (state it as
  "one major factor", sourced). Sources: Montañez & Poulsen 2013 (*Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences*), Smithsonian, Natural History Museum.
  **Don't use** the "fungi couldn't digest wood yet" claim (disputed, Nelsen et al. 2016).
- Scale analogy: Meganeura's wingspan against a household object (sourced).
- Twist: **forests** helped freeze the planet.
- Doug beat: Doug befriends a Meganeura. Death 63 (cold, not the dragonfly). One callback line to 003's Gondwana is allowed.

### Snowball.
**9. The Marinoan Snowball** (about 650-635 million years ago)
- Research: glacial deposits at tropical latitudes show ice reached near the equator (Snowball Earth hypothesis; Kirschvink
  1992 coined the term; Hoffman et al. 1998, *Science*); it ended when volcanic CO2 built up over millions of years and
  triggered a fast melt into a greenhouse climate; "cap carbonates" sit directly on top of the glacial rocks. Sources: Hoffman et al.
  1998 and 2017 (*Science Advances*), Harvard/Caltech press materials. State the temperatures only as sourced.
- Scale analogy: the CO2 buildup or the duration against something relatable (sourced).
- Twist: **the thaw was worse than the freeze**. Volcanoes saved the planet by cooking it.
- Doug beat: Doug survives the ice in a parka and gets heatstroke in the thaw. Death 64 (parka on, X eyes).

**10. The Sturtian Snowball** (about 717-660 million years ago; Doug survives)
- Research: the longest Snowball, about 57 million years (Rooney et al. 2015, *Geology*, or MacLennan et al.); sea ice may
  have been very thick (state only the sourced range); life survived in refuges such as meltwater ponds, thin ice, and
  hydrothermal vents (sourced; hedge if the source hedges).
- Scale analogy: **57 million years is almost as long as the time since the dinosaurs died (66 million)**.
- Twist: life still made it through, in tiny pockets.
- Doug beat: Doug shelters in a meltwater pond with green algae he befriends ("hi friend?"). He survives. The narrator is quietly
  disappointed. Counter stays at 64. **Plant the algae here**; it pays off in item 11.

**11. The First Snowball** (the Huronian, about 2.4-2.2 billion years ago; the boss, the longest item)
- Research: the Great Oxidation Event; oxygen from cyanobacteria reacted with atmospheric methane, a strong greenhouse gas,
  and Earth cooled into the Huronian glaciations, **possibly global** (hedge exactly as the source does); evidence in glacial rocks of the
  Gowganda Formation, Ontario; the glaciations spread over roughly 300 million years (sourced). Sources: Kopp et al.
  2005 (*PNAS*), NASA Astrobiology, Lyons et al. 2014 (*Nature*).
- Scale analogy: 2.4 billion years against something everyday (for example, half of Earth's age, if sourced).
- Twist: **life froze the planet.** The first things that learned to make oxygen nearly killed everything else.
- Doug beat: Doug arrives hopeful, with the algae friend's ancestors floating nearby. The narrator says the line once:
  "Doug's friends have been doing this for a while." Death 65. The final image is the decision for the
  writer and director (see 4).

## 8. Risks
- **Gore:** low. All deaths are cartoon (frozen solid, X eyes, ghost Doug, the lone cap). No frostbite descriptions,
  no injury detail in the crevasse, flood or heatstroke beats.
- **Real victims:** the 1816 famine, the Storegga tsunami (Mesolithic people) and the frost fair drownings were real. **Zero jokes
  about them**, and they get one plain factual line each at most. Doug is the only one who dies on screen.
- **Kid appeal:** snow, ice and a frozen planet invite a winter-cartoon tone. **Banned:** snowmen, snowball fights,
  cute penguins, sledging, any *Frozen* (Disney) or "let it go" reference, and nursery wording. The jokes stay adult-coded and deadpan
  (the souvenir card, the narrator refusing to count the repeats, "Doug's friends have been doing this for a while").
- **Climate politics:** this is deep-time science, not a climate-change commentary. No present-day policy lines and no
  forecasts. CO2 is described as a mechanism in the past only. Item 6's "land still rising" is fine (it is geology).
- **Contested science, which must be hedged or cut:** the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis (mention only as disputed, or skip it); whether
  the Huronian was fully global ("possibly"); Snowball vs. "Slushball" for the Marinoan (one hedge line); the drivers of the Karoo
  glaciation; the gateway vs. CO2 weighting for Antarctica; Sturtian ice thickness and its refuges.
- **Facts that need strong sources (cut the claim if it can't be sourced):** Tambora's VEI, volume and death toll range, and the June 1816 snow dates;
  the frost fair years, the 1814 elephant, the printed cards and the bridge mechanism; Doggerland's sea-level figure, the Storegga date, area
  and tsunami reach; the Younger Dryas duration and how abruptly it ended; the Missoula flood count, flow comparison and Bretz timeline; the
  Laurentide thickness and the rebound still happening today; the Antarctic forest fossils and the 34 Ma date; the Karoo dates, Meganeura's wingspan and
  the CO2/coal mechanism; the Marinoan and Sturtian dates and durations, low-latitude ice and cap carbonates; the Huronian dates and the GOE-methane
  mechanism. Prefer primary papers and USGS, NOAA, NPS, NASA, BAS, Smithsonian and NHM. Avoid listicles as the sole source.
- **Repetition:** there is no Ordovician glaciation (003). There are no ice-age mummies (005). Antarctica appears once, as a callback, and not as the 004 plateau.
  Item 9's thaw-greenhouse beat must not echo 003's "Hot Tub Ocean" wording.
- **Description:** fill at most 4,800 characters, with at least one primary or institutional source per item.
