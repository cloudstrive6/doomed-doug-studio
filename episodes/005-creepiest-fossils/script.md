## Sparta the Cave Lion Cub

Sparta the Cave Lion Cub.

Strictly speaking, the frozen animals in this half are mummies, and only the stone ones are fossils. Both kinds still look alive.

[Drawing of a small furry cub curled up in frozen mud. Small real museum photo in the corner, labelled "REAL".]

In twenty seventeen and twenty eighteen, mammoth tusk hunters on the banks of the Semyuelyakh River in Siberia found two cave lion cubs in the frozen ground. The female, Sparta, still has her fur and even her whiskers. Researchers called her probably the best-preserved Ice Age animal ever found.

The male, Boris, is a little more damaged. The two cubs were lying just fifteen meters apart, both were only one or two months old, and everyone assumed they were siblings.

Here's the twist. Radiocarbon dating says Sparta is about twenty-eight thousand years old, and Boris is more than forty-three thousand. The cubs that looked like littermates were born roughly fifteen thousand years apart.

Not counting her tail, Sparta is about half a meter long, and after thawing she weighed less than a one-liter bottle of water, a fraction of a grown house cat.

## Blue Babe

Blue Babe.

This one looks less like a fossil and more like a crime scene, with a piece of the suspect's tooth still stuck in the victim.

[Drawing of a big bison carcass lying on its side, tinted bright blue. Label: "1979".]

In nineteen seventy-nine, gold miners near Fairbanks, Alaska, were thawing frozen ground with hydraulic equipment when they uncovered a steppe bison. The University of Alaska Museum of the North dates it to about thirty-six thousand years ago.

It came out blue. During its long burial, the body reacted with minerals in the silt, and a blue mineral called vivianite formed on its skin. Hence the name, after Paul Bunyan's giant blue ox.

[Bison next to a small car. Label: "ABOUT 900 KG".]

Steppe bison were about the size of today's bison, and a big bull today can weigh around nine hundred kilograms, roughly the weight of a small car.

The cause of death is written on the hide: claw marks down its back end, tooth punctures in the skin, and a piece of a lion's tooth. An American lion, a cousin of Sparta's mother, got there first.

[Red arrows on the hide: "CLAWS", "TEETH".]

But that's not the disturbing part. In nineteen eighty-four, the paleontologist Dale Guthrie hosted a dinner, and a small piece of Blue Babe's neck went into the stew. His review was that it was well aged, a little tough, and had a strong Pleistocene aroma, and nobody at the table got sick.

## Lyuba the Baby Mammoth

Lyuba the Baby Mammoth.

Most mammoth finds are a tusk, a tooth or a few bones, but this one has skin, organs, and a face.

[Drawing of a small, dark-brown baby mammoth lying on a riverbank. Small real photo, labelled "REAL".]

In two thousand seven, a reindeer herder named Yuri Khudi found a baby woolly mammoth on the Yamal Peninsula in northern Siberia. She was named Lyuba, after his wife, and she is often called the best-preserved mammoth ever found.

Lyuba died about forty-two thousand years ago, when she was roughly a month old. She weighed about forty-five kilograms, which made her roughly the size of a large dog. Her organs were intact, and her stomach still held her mother's milk. In twenty ten, she went on display at the Field Museum in Chicago, her first visit to the United States.

[Medical-scan style drawing of the calf's head. A dark plug in the trunk. Label: "MUD".]

A medical scan found a solid plug of fine mud blocking her trunk, and researchers concluded that she inhaled it and suffocated near a river.

Then the mud did something strange. After she died, bacteria in the wet ground produced lactic acid, and scientists think that acid essentially pickled her soft tissues. It is the same chemistry that turns cabbage into sauerkraut, and it means the thing that killed her is the reason she still has a face.

[Time machine dial: "42,000 YEARS AGO". Doug finds the calf stuck at a muddy riverbank and rolls up his sleeves. Speech bubble: "I got you".]

## The Batagaika Foal

The Batagaika Foal.

In northern Siberia, there is a hole in the ground that will not stop growing, and it has earned the nickname "the gateway to the underworld".

[Drawing of a huge horseshoe-shaped scar in a forest, seen from above. Football fields laid end to end across it. Label: "ABOUT 1 KM".]

The Batagaika crater is not an impact crater, just frozen ground that thawed and slumped. It is now close to a kilometer across, about nine football fields laid end to end, and its cliffs reach about eighty-six meters high. Its edge moves outward by ten meters or more every year, and as the walls thaw, they keep giving up things that froze a very long time ago.

[A small foal lying on a muddy slope. Small real photo, labelled "REAL".]

In August twenty eighteen, one of those things was a foal. It belonged to the Lena horse, an extinct horse that once lived in this part of Siberia, and it was about forty-two thousand years old. Researchers said it was only a few weeks old when it died, probably stuck in mud, and its skin, hair and organs were intact.

The unsettling part came the next year. In twenty nineteen, the team at North-Eastern Federal University in Yakutsk reported that they had drawn liquid blood from the foal's heart, and urine from its bladder. They described it as the oldest liquid blood ever found.

They also tried to clone it, and working with a South Korean lab, they made more than twenty attempts to grow living cells from the foal.

None of them worked.

[Time machine parked at the crater rim, present day. Doug walks to the edge for the view. Speech bubble: "wow".]

Before going back for the foal, Doug parks at the rim to admire the view, on a rim that has been moving outward for decades.

[The edge crumbles under Doug. The red cap rolls down the slope after him. DOUG DEATHS: 39]

Today it moves with Doug.

Batagaika took forty-two thousand years to give back one foal, so Doug's cap should turn up any millennium now.

## Zhùr the Wolf Pup

Zhùr the Wolf Pup.

Ice-age wolves are supposed to hunt big animals on the frozen grasslands, but this one grew up on something else.

[Drawing of a small, dark, curled-up wolf pup in frozen mud. Small real photo, labelled "REAL". Muted colours, no jokes on screen.]

In July two thousand sixteen, near Dawson City in Canada's Yukon, a miner was thawing frozen mud with a jet of water. A small shape with fur came out of the wall.

It was a grey wolf pup, about fifty-seven thousand years old, and she is the most complete wolf mummy ever found. Her fur, skin and teeth are all still there.

The Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, the First Nation whose land she was found on, named her Zhùr, which means wolf in their Hän language.

[Pup next to a loaf of bread. Label: "670 G".]

Zhùr was about seven weeks old, and she measures about forty-two centimeters from her nose to the base of her tail. She weighs about six hundred and seventy grams, roughly as much as a loaf of bread. Researchers think she was inside her den when it collapsed, and that she died instantly.

Then scientists checked her diet. Chemical signals in her body show she ate a lot from the water, especially salmon, probably caught in the nearby Klondike River. An ice-age wolf on the mammoth steppe, raised partly on fish.

## The Messel Turtles

The Messel Turtles.

In twenty twelve, paleontologists described nine suspected pairs of fossil turtles from one German lake, and every pair seems to have died in the middle of mating.

[Drawing of a quarry in Germany. Small real photo of the pit, labelled "REAL".]

Near Frankfurt, in Germany, an old oil shale quarry called the Messel Pit is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. About forty-seven million years ago, it was a lake surrounded by forest, and its rock now holds animals preserved in astonishing detail.

[Two small turtles, one slightly smaller, side by side in grey rock. A dinner plate next to them for scale.]

Over about thirty years, researchers have recovered nine pairs of a turtle called Allaeochelys from the pit. Each pair is a male and a female, preserved together, apparently while mating, and the males are about a fifth smaller than the females. Each turtle is only about twenty centimeters long, smaller than a dinner plate.

The worst part is that, as of that study, no other vertebrate fossils like this were known anywhere in the world. This one lake produced nine.

[Cross-section of the lake. Top layer light blue, labelled "FINE". Bottom layer murky green, labelled "POISON". Two turtles drifting down.]

The explanation is in the water, because the surface of the lake was safe, but the deep water was poisonous. Researchers think the turtles began mating near the surface and sank together while they did. About ten meters down, their skin absorbed poisons, probably carbon dioxide from volcanic gas, and they died before they could separate. The lead researcher called it a very reasonable guess, which in paleontology is high praise.

[Time machine dial: "47,000,000 YEARS AGO". Doug wades into the lake, sees two turtles, covers his eyes. Speech bubble: "sorry".]

Doug wades into the lake, interrupts a very private moment, apologizes, and walks deeper to give the couple some space.

[Doug sinks out of frame. The red cap floats on the calm surface. DOUG DEATHS: 40]

The lake does not care.

The turtles sank as a pair, and the lake kept them that way for forty-seven million years.

## The Fighting Dinosaurs

The Fighting Dinosaurs.

Of all the dinosaur fossils ever dug up, this is the most dramatic proof that two of them actually fought, and the fight never got an ending.

[Drawing of a desert at dawn. Two skeletons locked together in a block of sandstone. Label: "1971".]

On the third of August, nineteen seventy-one, a Polish and Mongolian expedition in the Gobi Desert found a Velociraptor and a Protoceratops locked together in the rock. The block is now a national treasure of Mongolia.

Fossils almost never record behavior. Bones tell you what an animal was, not what it was doing, so this block is a rare snapshot of two dinosaurs in the middle of an afternoon.

[Doug standing next to a turkey-sized raptor. Then next to a movie-sized one, crossed out.]

Forget the movie version. A real Velociraptor was about the size of a turkey. Each foot carried a curved claw that could measure more than six and a half centimeters around its outer edge, about the length of a house key. Protoceratops was a plant-eater with a beak and a bony frill around its neck.

In the fossil, the raptor's foot claw is at the other animal's throat, and the Protoceratops has the raptor's arm in its mouth.

Nobody knows who started it. The raptor may have been hunting, or the Protoceratops may have been defending itself.

Here's the problem. Neither of them won, because both were buried alive in the middle of the fight, probably by a collapsing sand dune or a sandstorm. That was somewhere between seventy-one and seventy-five million years ago, and they have held the same pose ever since.

[A wall of sand looming over both animals.]

[Time machine dial: "75,000,000 YEARS AGO". Doug steps between the two dinosaurs with both arms raised. Speech bubble: "guys".]

Doug steps in between them with both arms raised to calm everyone down, and the dune settles the argument for all three of them.

[Sand pours down. A smooth fresh dune. The red cap sits on top. DOUG DEATHS: 41]

It is the most famous fight in paleontology, and the referee was a sand dune.

## Borealopelta

Borealopelta.

Stand in front of it at the Royal Tyrrell Museum in Alberta, and you are not looking at a skeleton. You are looking at a dinosaur's face.

[Drawing of a huge machine in an oil sands mine, its bucket stopped against a dark lump. Label: "2011".]

On March twenty-first, twenty eleven, a heavy-equipment operator named Shawn Funk was digging in an oil sands mine in northern Alberta, Canada, when he hit something unusual. It was a nodosaur, an armored plant-eating dinosaur, about a hundred and ten million years old.

Its skin and armor plates are preserved in three dimensions, still in place, so the front half looks like a sculpture. A museum technician, Mark Mitchell, spent more than seven thousand hours freeing it from the rock, and the species is named after him.

[Nodosaur next to a small car. Label: "1,300 KG".]

Alive, it was about five and a half meters long and weighed about thirteen hundred kilograms, about as much as a small car. Even its stomach survived, and its last meal was mostly ferns, plus bits of charcoal, so it had been feeding where a fire had burned not long before.

What makes it creepy is the color, because traces of reddish-brown pigment cover its back but not its belly. Researchers think that two-tone pattern was camouflage, which means something out there hunted a thirteen-hundred-kilogram armored tank, and the tank had to hide.

[Map: a river emptying into an ancient sea. A belly-up carcass drifting out.]

Researchers think the carcass washed down a river, drifted out to sea belly up, kept afloat by bloat, and finally sank back first onto the seafloor.

[Time machine dial: "110,000,000 YEARS AGO". Doug sits on the floating carcass like a raft, thumbs up.]

Doug finds a floating dinosaur and rides it out to sea like a raft, thumbs up, for several days. Then the raft sinks.

[Doug underneath, flat on the seafloor. The red cap drifts above. DOUG DEATHS: 42]

The best-preserved armored dinosaur ever found got that way by dying in the wrong place, floating, and sinking upside down.

## The Death March

The Death March.

This slab of German limestone holds the longest death track ever found, and the animal that made it is lying at the very end.

[Drawing of a shallow tropical lagoon under a stormy sky. A small horseshoe crab tumbling in a wave.]

About a hundred and fifty million years ago, in what is now Bavaria, in southern Germany, a young horseshoe crab about thirteen centimeters long ended up in a lagoon. Researchers think a storm washed it in.

The lagoon was a trap. Its water was extremely salty, and near the bottom there was almost no oxygen, which is bad news for anything that breathes.

[A long trail of tiny footprints across a pale limestone slab. A city bus drawn next to it for scale.]

The crab started walking, and its tracks run for more than nine point seven meters, nearly the length of a city bus. At the very end of the trail, the crab itself is preserved, right where it stopped. There is no sign that a predator was involved. The lagoon did all the work.

[Zoom along the trail. The footprints get messier.]

Look closer, and it gets personal. You can read the walk, step by step. Footprints, tail drag marks and changes in walking style show an animal in trouble, and toward the end, the prints grow deeper and more erratic. Then they stop, and the low oxygen that ended the walk is also what kept every step of it.

[Time machine dial: "150,000,000 YEARS AGO". Doug in a snorkel, following the trail across the lagoon floor. Speech bubble: "where's it going?"]

Doug follows the trail across the lagoon floor, very curious about where it leads.

[Two trails, side by side. One ends at the crab. The other ends at the red cap. DOUG DEATHS: 43]

Now there are two trails.

It is still the longest death track ever found, but it is no longer the only one in the lagoon.

## The Ichthyosaur's Skin

The Ichthyosaur's Skin.

From a distance, it looks like a dolphin, but it is actually a reptile, and it is not closely related to dolphins at all.

[Drawing of a sleek, dolphin-shaped reptile with a long snout. Small real photo of the dark fossil slab, labelled "REAL".]

About a hundred and eighty million years ago, an ichthyosaur called Stenopterygius died in the sea that covered what is now Holzmaden, in southern Germany. The preserved part is eighty-five centimeters long, about half the animal, so alive it was about as long as an adult person is tall.

Plenty of ichthyosaurs are known from their bones, but skin is another matter. This one still has it.

[Close-up drawing: smooth skin, dark on top, pale underneath.]

In twenty eighteen, a team led by Johan Lindgren reported that the skin was smooth, with no scales, and that they could see separate layers of cells in it. Some of those cells still held pigment, showing a dark back and a pale belly, a camouflage pattern seen in many sea predators today.

[A thick yellow layer under the skin. Label: "BLUBBER?"]

Here's the creepy part. Under the skin is a layer of material chemically consistent with blubber, the fat that keeps whales warm. Blubber belongs to warm-blooded animals, so it is the first direct chemical evidence that ichthyosaurs were warm-blooded.

Stomachs of this kind of ichthyosaur often hold tiny hooks from the arms of belemnites, squid-like animals it ate.

[Time machine dial: "180,000,000 YEARS AGO". Doug underwater in the squid costume, red cap on top, waving. Speech bubble: "hello fellow squid".]

Which is exactly why Doug is dressed as one, because he wants to get close, and the costume is very convincing.

[A dark shape passes. The costume floats empty and flat, X eyes painted on it. The red cap drifts up. DOUG DEATHS: 44]

The ichthyosaur kept its skin for a hundred and eighty million years, and the costume did not last a minute.

## The Gogo Fish Heart

The Gogo Fish Heart.

Bones fossilize all the time, but soft organs almost never do, and a heart in three dimensions should be impossible.

[Drawing of red-brown hills in Western Australia. Then a cross-section showing the same place as a huge ancient reef.]

In the Kimberley region of Western Australia, there is rock that was once a giant reef, sometimes called Australia's first great barrier reef. About three hundred and eighty million years ago, during the Devonian, fish that died here sank into deep water with little oxygen, and limestone nodules formed around them.

Many were arthrodires, extinct fish with heads and chests covered in bony armor plates. As a group, arthrodires ranged from small bottom feeders to six-meter top predators, longer than a family car.

[A round stone nodule, scanned. Glowing shapes inside: "STOMACH", "LIVER", "HEART".]

In twenty twenty-two, a team led by Professor Kate Trinajstic of Curtin University scanned these nodules with neutron beams and X-rays. Inside, they found a stomach, an intestine, and a liver.

And a heart.

The unnerving part is how complete it is, because the heart is S-shaped, with two chambers, the smaller one on top. It is about two hundred and fifty million years older than the oldest vertebrate heart known before it. It sits far forward, under the gills, much like a shark's heart today, which helps scientists understand how bodies changed as animals evolved jaws.

[Time machine dial: "380,000,000 YEARS AGO". Doug in a snorkel on a bright reef, waving at a huge armored fish. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug has been to a Devonian reef before, and it went badly then, too. This time he swims up to the biggest armored fish he can find, to say hello.

[Cut away. Off-frame crunch. The red cap floats up through the water. DOUG DEATHS: 45]

The Gogo reef is famous for keeping everything that sinks into it, which is not the reputation you want in a swimming spot.

## Stanleycaris

Stanleycaris.

Everything on this list so far kept something it should have lost, like fur, skin, blood, a stomach or a heart, and the oldest one kept its brain.

[Background: near-black Cambrian sea. Drawing of steep mountains with a thin dark layer of rock high up. Label: "YOHO NATIONAL PARK".]

High in the Canadian Rockies, in Yoho National Park in British Columbia, there is a layer of rock called the Burgess Shale. About five hundred and six million years ago, it was the floor of a sea.

[A one-year calendar. Almost the whole year is grey. A tiny sliver at the end of December 31st is labelled "ICE AGE ANIMALS". A mark in late November is labelled "STANLEYCARIS".]

Squeeze the whole history of Earth into one calendar year. Sparta and the other ice-age animals died in the last seven minutes before midnight on New Year's Eve. Stanleycaris died in late November.

For decades, its fossils sat in the collection of the Royal Ontario Museum in Toronto, mostly unstudied, until a student volunteer, Joseph Moysiuk, came across them. Years later, for his doctorate, he studied all two hundred and sixty-eight.

[Drawing of Stanleycaris next to a pencil. Label: "20 CM".]

Stanleycaris was a radiodont, an early relative of insects and spiders. It was no more than twenty centimeters long, about the length of a new pencil. It had a round mouth lined with teeth and two front claws covered in spines, which was plenty for a Cambrian sea.

It also had three eyes. Two sat on stalks, and the third was a large eye in the middle of the front of its head, something nobody had seen in a radiodont before.

[Close-up: the head, with a glowing outline of a two-part brain wired to the eyes and claws. Label: "BRAIN".]

Here's what makes it the boss. In eighty-four of those fossils, the brain and nerves are still there, after five hundred and six million years. You can see that the brain had two segments, one wired to the eyes and one to the front claws. The team said the detail looked like an animal that had died yesterday.

The reason is how they died. Burgess Shale animals were buried by sudden underwater mudflows that covered the seafloor all at once. The fine mud, and the lack of oxygen inside it, kept the soft parts from rotting away.

Doug steps out onto the Cambrian seafloor, meets a creature with three eyes, and stares back with two.

Nobody blinks.

[A brown wall of mud rolls in from the left and covers everything. DOUG DEATHS: 46]

Then the mudflow arrives.

[Back in the museum hall at night. The time machine's door creaks open by itself. Nobody steps out. The red cap is lying on the floor inside.]

Back in the museum, the time machine door opens by itself, and nobody steps out.

The cap crossed half a billion years without a scratch, and it is already waiting by the door for the next trip.
