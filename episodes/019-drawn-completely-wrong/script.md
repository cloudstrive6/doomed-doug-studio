## Brontosaurus

Brontosaurus.

[Caption bar: BRONTOSAURUS. The OLD DRAWING card fills the frame: a long-necked giant with a short, boxy head. Year: 1932.]

This is the long-necked giant everyone pictures, and for most of a century, nobody pictured it correctly.

In 1879, Othniel Marsh named Brontosaurus from a huge skeleton dug up in the American West, an animal nearly as long as a tennis court from nose to tail.

[A tennis court with the sauropod laid along it, nose to tail.]

The skeleton was missing one thing, which was the head.

So Marsh borrowed one. His restoration used a boxy skull found at a different site, from a different animal entirely.

The problem is, the borrowed head stuck.

For most of the twentieth century, one of the most famous dinosaurs in the world was wearing somebody else's head.

[Red X stamps the card.]

Then it lost its name as well, because in 1903, Elmer Riggs decided Brontosaurus was the same animal as Apatosaurus, which Marsh had named two years earlier, and the older name won. At the Carnegie Museum in Pittsburgh, the Apatosaurus skeleton stood headless for years. In 1932, it was given a blunt Camarasaurus skull, following the expert opinion of the day.

It wore that skull for forty-seven years. In 1978, David Berman and John McIntosh argued that a long, low skull found with the skeleton was the right one, and in 1979, the museum swapped the heads.

In 2015, a team led by Emanuel Tschopp argued that Brontosaurus was different enough to be its own animal again, and although not everyone agrees, the name is back.

## Iguanodon

Iguanodon.

[Caption bar: IGUANODON. OLD DRAWING card, year 1825: a heavy, lizard-like animal on four legs with a single cone-shaped horn on its nose.]

It was one of the first dinosaurs ever named, and its first drawings came with a horn on its nose.

In 1825, Gideon Mantell named it from a handful of fossil teeth that looked like an iguana's teeth, only far bigger, so he pictured a giant iguana. His wife, Mary Ann, had already found a pointed, cone-shaped bone. With no skeleton to show where it went, Mantell put it where horns usually go, which was on the nose.

[The cone-shaped bone hovers, then snaps onto the snout.]

The idea grew, and in the 1850s, a life-size Iguanodon was built for the Crystal Palace park in London, as a heavy four-legged reptile with the horn on its snout. On New Year's Eve, 1853, before the model was finished, its builder threw a dinner party inside the mould. Most accounts say twenty-one guests attended, and the anatomist Richard Owen sat in the head.

[A long dinner table inside a half-built Iguanodon. Top hats. One stick man in the head end.]

Here's the catch. In 1878, coal miners in Bernissart, Belgium, hit bones three hundred and twenty-two meters underground. Over the next three years, around thirty skeletons came out, many of them nearly complete, and none of them had a horn on its nose.

The horn was a thumb.

[Red X stamps the card. The cone moves from the nose to the hand.]

Each hand carried one, on the thumb. One fossil spike is about fourteen centimeters long, a little shorter than a dollar bill, and in life it was covered in horn, so it was bigger. What it was for is still argued, and it may have been for defense, or for stripping leaves and breaking open seeds.

[Doug steps out of the time machine wearing a fencing mask, ready for a nose horn.]

[Doug gives a thumbs up back. DOUG DEATHS stays at 155.]

Both of the park's Victorian Iguanodons are still there today, and so is the horn on each of their noses.

## Elasmosaurus

Elasmosaurus.

[Caption bar: ELASMOSAURUS. OLD DRAWING card, year 1869: a sea reptile with a short neck, a long tail, and a small head on the end of the tail.]

This is a sea reptile with an enormous neck, and the man who named it was sure that neck was a tail.

In 1868, Edward Drinker Cope presented a skeleton of a marine reptile from western Kansas, with a long row of backbones at one end and a short row at the other. He decided that the long row was the tail, so he put the skull on the end of the short one and published the drawing.

[The card's head bobs on its stubby neck while the long tail trails behind.]

In 1870, his senior colleague Joseph Leidy told a meeting of the Academy of Natural Sciences in Philadelphia that Cope had the animal backwards. Cope tried to recall every copy of the paper he had already sent out. In the usual telling, his rival Othniel Marsh kept his copies, and the embarrassment helped turn the two men into lifelong enemies in the fossil feud known as the Bone Wars.

The awkward part is that Cope was not slightly wrong, because the head was on the wrong end.

[Red X stamps the card. The head jumps from the tail to the neck.]

And that neck was the longest part of the animal. A 2013 restudy counted seventy-two vertebrae in the neck alone, and an old estimate put the neck at about seven meters. That is more than twice the height of a basketball hoop, and all of it ends in teeth.

[A basketball hoop, then a second hoop stacked on top, then the neck drawn beside both.]

[Doug steps out of the time machine into shallow sea, holding the 1869 card. He studies it.]

Doug studies the card. Long tail, short neck, head at the short end. So he swims toward the long end, which is obviously the safe one.

[Doug paddles toward the long end. Far behind him, the real head rises on its neck.]

The long end has a mouth, and it also has seven meters of reach.

## Stegosaurus

Stegosaurus.

[Caption bar: STEGOSAURUS. OLD DRAWING card, year 1877: a giant turtle-like animal with flat triangular plates overlapping across its back like a roof.]

Its name means roof lizard, and for a while, the scientist who named it meant that quite literally.

When Othniel Marsh described it in 1877, he had a pile of bones and a set of flat, triangular plates, and he decided the plates lay flat across the back, overlapping like a shell. For a few years he also thought the tail spikes were weapons on its wrists.

[The plates slide flat across the back. Spikes appear on the wrists, then a red "?" beside them.]

Then a skeleton turned up still held in mud, with the plates where they belonged, and they stood upright, alternating along either side of the spine. Fossils found later point to two staggered rows, though not every drawing agrees on the exact pattern.

[The plates flip upright one by one.]

Here's the bigger problem, which is that the roof was never the dangerous part of this animal.

The tail carried four spikes, each sixty to ninety centimeters long. In 1982, the cartoonist Gary Larson drew a Far Side panel in which a caveman names them after the late Thag Simmons. Paleontologists started calling the spikes the thagomizer, and the name stuck.

[A cartoon lecture hall. A blank chalkboard labeled THAGOMIZER.]

In 2005, a team including Kenneth Carpenter described a tail bone from Allosaurus, the big predator of the time. A piece about four centimeters across is missing from it, a little smaller than a golf ball, and a Stegosaurus spike fits the hole. The edges had started to heal, so that Allosaurus lived to regret it.

The roof was a warning sign. The danger was at the other end.

[Red X stamps the card.]

[Doug steps out of the time machine and climbs onto the Stegosaurus, card in hand, looking for a flat place to sit.]

Doug's card says the roof is flat, so he climbs up to sit on it. It is not flat.

[Doug slides down between the plates toward the tail. Cut away. Dust cloud. DOUG DEATHS: 157.]

The spikes are still officially named after a cartoon caveman. Doug was not considered for the name.

## Megalosaurus

Megalosaurus.

[Caption bar: MEGALOSAURUS. OLD DRAWING card, year 1854: a bulky, scaly animal on four straight legs, like an elephant with a lizard's head.]

It was the first dinosaur ever given a name, which also made it the first dinosaur anyone got to draw wrong.

In 1824, William Buckland named it from a handful of bones found around Oxford, in England, including part of a lower jaw with teeth and part of a leg bone. The word dinosaur did not exist yet, and would not be coined until 1842, so Buckland described a giant lizard. He pictured it walking on four legs, like an elephant.

[The jaw and the leg bone float on the card, and a huge four-legged lizard is sketched around them.]

Victorian estimates then grew, and some people thought it might have been around twenty meters long. In 1854, the Crystal Palace park, the same one with the nose-horned Iguanodon, gave it a statue that stands on four straight legs, bulky and slow.

The later fossils told a different story.

As more meat-eating dinosaurs were dug up with complete skeletons, it became clear that animals like this walked on two legs. The Natural History Museum in London now puts Megalosaurus at around six meters long, a little longer than two ping-pong tables end to end, and less than a third of the biggest Victorian guess.

The giant lizard was a two-legged hunter, and the hunter was much smaller than the drawing.

[Two ping-pong tables end to end, with the real Megalosaurus standing beside them. Red X stamps the card. The four-legged giant shrinks and stands up on two legs.]

[Doug steps out of the time machine carrying a very tall ladder, so he can reach the head of a twenty-meter lizard.]

Doug has brought a very tall ladder, so he can reach the head of a twenty-meter lizard. The real Megalosaurus is far shorter. It looks at the ladder, looks at Doug at the top of it, and walks away.

[DOUG DEATHS stays at 157.]

Doug is fine. Being too strange to eat turns out to be a survival strategy.

Two hundred years after it was named, nobody has found a complete Megalosaurus skeleton, so every drawing of it is still partly a guess.

## Deinocheirus

Deinocheirus.

[Caption bar: DEINOCHEIRUS. OLD DRAWING card, year 1965: two enormous clawed arms, and a dark, question-mark silhouette where the body should be.]

For almost fifty years, this animal was just a pair of arms, and they were enough to scare everyone.

In 1965, a Polish and Mongolian expedition in the Gobi Desert found two arms, each about two point four meters long. That is longer than an average American man is tall, by more than half a meter. Each hand ended in claws around twenty centimeters long.

[One arm stood on end beside a stick man of average height. The arm is much taller.]

There was no body and no head, just the arms and a lot of imagination, so for decades it was drawn as a giant clawed hunter.

In 2014, a team led by Yuong-Nam Lee described two new skeletons in the journal Nature. Poachers had got to some of the bones first, and the skull and the feet had been smuggled out of Mongolia into a private collection in Europe. A French fossil dealer recognized them and bought them to give back to Mongolia.

The strangest part was everything else.

The animal attached to those arms was about eleven meters long and weighed about six tonnes. It had a tall ridge of bony spines on its back, like a sail, and a broad, toothless bill like a duck's. Inside the ribcage were more than one thousand four hundred gizzard stones, and fish remains.

[The silhouette fills in: hump, duck bill, big belly. Red X stamps the card.]

Fifty years of nightmares about the claws, and the rest of the animal turned out to be a duck with a hump.

[Doug steps out of the time machine behind the biggest shield he owns.]

Doug brings his biggest shield, and Deinocheirus walks right past him, dips its bill into the river, and eats a fish. Doug is ignored completely.

[Doug lowers the shield. DOUG DEATHS still stays at 157.]

People drew those claws for half a century, and the fish was caught without them.

## Hallucigenia

Hallucigenia.

[Caption bar: HALLUCIGENIA. OLD DRAWING card, year 1977: a small worm walking on seven pairs of stilt-like spines, with tentacles waving on its back and a big blob at one end.]

It was named for how dreamlike it looked, and for decades nobody could tell which way up it went.

It lived more than half a billion years ago on the sea floor, in rock that is now the Burgess Shale in the Canadian Rockies. The same rock gave us Anomalocaris, which Doug has already met. Hallucigenia was tiny, between one and three centimeters long, so the smallest would fit across a quarter.

[A quarter, with a tiny Hallucigenia lying across it.]

In 1977, Simon Conway Morris described it walking on stiff spines, like stilts, with a row of soft tentacles on its back. At one end sat a big blob, which looked like a head.

In 1991, Lars Ramsköld and Hou Xianguang compared it with similar fossils from China and turned it over. The tentacles were legs, and the stilts were defensive spines on its back. The next year, Ramsköld turned it around as well, because the blob was probably a stain, fluid that leaked out of the body as it decayed.

[The card flips upside down, then spins end to end.]

But that's not where it ends.

In 2015, Martin Smith and Jean-Bernard Caron described the real head at the other end. It had a pair of simple eyes and a mouth ringed with spines, with needle-like teeth lining its throat.

Upside down, and back to front. It was wrong in every direction it is possible to be wrong.

[Red X stamps the card.]

These animals are preserved in such detail because underwater mudslides buried them where they lived, faster than they could rot.

Doug lies on his back to see it the 1977 way. Hallucigenia does nothing to him at all. The mudslide that made it a fossil does.

## Dimetrodon

Dimetrodon.

[Caption bar: DIMETRODON. OLD DRAWING card, year 1878: a sail-backed animal on a gift-shop shelf labeled DINOSAURS.]

It shows up in plenty of toy dinosaur sets, with a big sail on its back and a mouth full of teeth. It is not a dinosaur.

Edward Drinker Cope named it in 1878 from fossils found in Texas. It lived roughly two hundred and ninety-eight to two hundred and seventy-two million years ago, in the Permian period, and it was long gone before the first dinosaurs appeared. The Field Museum in Chicago once asked the public to send in photos of toys and games that called it a dinosaur.

Measured in time, Dimetrodon is further from Tyrannosaurus than Tyrannosaurus is from you, by about three times.

[A timeline. Dimetrodon at the far left, Tyrannosaurus in the middle, a tiny Doug at the right end. The left gap is three times longer than the right one.]

It was also the top predator on land in its time. In 2014, Kirstin Brink and Robert Reisz found that the larger, later species had serrated teeth, like steak knives, about forty million years before meat-eating dinosaurs evolved the same trick.

The uncomfortable part is where it sits on the family tree.

Dimetrodon was a synapsid. Its skull has a single opening behind each eye socket, the same basic plan as yours, and it sits on the branch of animals that would eventually lead to mammals. That makes it more closely related to you than to any dinosaur.

It's not a dinosaur. It's family.

## Therizinosaurus

Therizinosaurus.

[Caption bar: THERIZINOSAURUS. OLD DRAWING card, year 1954: a giant turtle-like reptile paddling through water, three huge flat claws on its flippers.]

This animal was known from its claws long before anyone knew what was holding them.

In 1954, Evgeny Maleev described three huge claws from Mongolia, along with some rib pieces and a hand bone. No land animal he knew of had claws like that, so he decided they belonged to a giant turtle-like reptile, about four and a half meters long, that used them to cut water plants. He even named the species for it. Cheloniformis means turtle-shaped.

In 1970, a Soviet paleontologist, Anatoly Rozhdestvensky, looked again. The rib came from a long-necked sauropod, and the hand bone and the claws came from a theropod, the same broad group as Tyrannosaurus. Later finds of its relatives showed long necks, beaks and sturdy arms.

[The turtle shell on the card cracks off. Red X stamps the card.]

So the old drawing was wrong three times over. The turtle was a dinosaur. The dinosaur ate plants. The claws stayed exactly the same size.

Each claw is about fifty centimeters long, longer than one and a half school rulers. The Natural History Museum in London says they are thought to be the longest claws of any land animal ever. The whole animal was about ten meters long, although even now it is known only from some arm, leg and rib bones, so much of its body is borrowed from its relatives.

[One and a half rulers end to end beside a claw. The claw is longer.]

Nobody is sure what the claws were for. Pulling branches closer is one idea, and simply looking threatening is another.

None of that helps Doug.

Doug has brought a bunch of water plants as a gift for a turtle, and he is standing between Therizinosaurus and the tree it wanted.

[Cut away. Leaves fall. DOUG DEATHS: 160.]

## Homo diluvii testis

Homo diluvii testis.

[Caption bar: HOMO DILUVII TESTIS. OLD DRAWING card, year 1726: a stone slab with a round skull and a backbone, labeled in old lettering as a drowned man.]

This one was not drawn as the wrong animal. It was drawn as a person.

In 1726, the Swiss naturalist Johann Jakob Scheuchzer announced a fossil from a limestone quarry near Öhningen, in southern Germany. To him, the bones in the stone looked like part of a human skeleton. He believed it was the skeleton of a man who had drowned in Noah's flood, so he named it Homo diluvii testis, man, witness of the flood. With only part of a skeleton showing in the stone, it was a natural reading for a scholar of his time.

[The slab in a glass case. A brass plate: HOMO DILUVII TESTIS.]

The fossil was later bought by Teylers Museum in Haarlem, in the Netherlands, and in 1811, the French anatomist Georges Cuvier examined it there. Using a sharp needle, he carefully freed more bone from the stone and exposed the shoulder bones and both front legs.

What nobody expected was the shape of those legs. They belonged to a salamander, and a giant one.

For eighty-five years, a fossil salamander was described as a man.

[Red X stamps the card. The outline of a giant salamander appears inside the slab.]

Today it is called Andrias scheuchzeri, after the man who first described it, and it is so close to the living Japanese giant salamander that some scientists have treated them as one species. That salamander can reach about one and a half meters, about as long as a ping-pong table is wide.

[A ping-pong table, seen from the end, with a giant salamander lying across it.]

[Doug steps out of the time machine and lies down next to the slab to compare. Speech bubble: "twins?"]

Doug lies down next to the slab to compare the two of them. A giant salamander crawls past, looks at him for a moment, and keeps going.

[DOUG DEATHS stays at 160.]

Doug is fine.

The fossil is still at Teylers Museum, and it has now been a salamander for far longer than it was a man.

## Spinosaurus

Spinosaurus.

[Caption bar: SPINOSAURUS. Not one card this time. A stack of four OLD DRAWING cards, years 1930s, 2014, 2020 and 2022, each showing a different animal.]

It is one of the longest predatory dinosaurs ever found, and in a little over a century, science has drawn it at least four completely different ways.

In 1915, the German paleontologist Ernst Stromer described it from bones found in Egypt. Stromer was a critic of the Nazi regime, and during the Second World War he asked the director of the Munich museum to move the fossils to safety. The director refused. In April 1944, those bones were destroyed when Allied bombs hit Munich, and only his drawings, photographs and descriptions survived. His reconstruction showed a big upright hunter, on land, with a tall spined sail on its back.

[Card one: an upright, land-walking predator with a sail.]

In 2014, a team led by Nizar Ibrahim used new fossils from Morocco, plus Stromer's surviving records, and argued in the journal Science that it was semiaquatic, with surprisingly short hind legs.

[Card two: a low, crocodile-jawed animal with stubby back legs at a riverbank.]

In 2020, the same team found a tail with tall bony struts, shaped like a paddle, and argued in Nature that it swam after its prey. Their estimate puts it at around fifteen meters long, about three giraffes laid nose to tail.

[Card three: the animal swimming, paddle tail behind it. Three giraffes in a line beneath it for scale.]

Here's what nobody has settled.

In 2022, another study in Nature found its bones were dense, like those of animals that dive for food, and argued that it hunted underwater. The same year, Paul Sereno's team argued in the journal eLife that it was far too buoyant to dive. In their version, it walked the shallows on two legs, ambushing fish at the water's edge.

[Card four: the animal standing upright in knee-deep water, head down, like a giant heron.]

Some researchers even doubt that all the Moroccan bones belong to the same species as Stromer's, and scientists still argue about all of it.

There is no final drawing, so Doug can't know which Spinosaurus is coming. On land. In the river. Under it.

Doug has brought all four cards, and he holds them up one at a time, checking the river after each one.

[Doug holds up card one, then two, then three, then four, looking left, right, at the water, and down. Behind him, a shape that matches none of the cards rises out of the river.]

The real one is the version nobody has drawn yet.

[Cut away. Splash. DOUG DEATHS: 161.]
