## 2. Escalation axis and items
**Axis: severity of the error, measured as how much of the first drawing was wrong.** One wrong part, then a wrong body, then a wrong
kind of animal, and the boss is the animal science is **still** redrawing. Spoken section headers (not used before):
- **"Wrong part."** (opener, items 1-3)
- **"Wrong body."** (before item 4)
- **"Wrong animal."** (before item 7, about 60%)
- **"Still wrong."** (before item 10 only, the boss)

**Episode device (new, episode-only): the OLD DRAWING card.** Each item opens on the historical reconstruction redrawn in our style on a
yellowed "museum card" with a year ("1883", "1877"...). Doug studies the card, prepares for that animal, and the real one arrives. At the
twist, a red X stamps the card. No separate meter (the counter is enough). The time machine is the vehicle (series bible, `prehistoric`):
Doug steps out holding the card each time.

**Rules:** every number and date needs a source. Where scientists still disagree, say so ("scientists still argue about..."). If a fact
fails, cut it, don't hedge it. The old drawings are **redrawn by us**, never copied from historical art or modern paleoart; real
historical images only with verified public-domain status (most 19th-century plates qualify; the writer notes candidates, the director
checks the licence). Deaths: 7, numbered 155 to 161. Death 160 is just a number. No bit.

### Wrong part.

**1. Brontosaurus** (hook item; death 155)
- Facts:
  - Marsh named Brontosaurus in 1879; the skeletons mounted in museums carried a short, boxy skull of the Camarasaurus type because no
    head had been found with the body (verify which skull and on which mounts, Carnegie/Yale/AMNH).
  - Riggs (1903) sank Brontosaurus into Apatosaurus; the correct long, low skull was identified by Berman & McIntosh (1978), and museums
    swapped heads around 1979 (verify the dates).
  - Tschopp, Mateus & Benson 2015 (*PeerJ*) revived Brontosaurus as a separate genus (verify).
- Analogy idea: body mass (about 15-17 t; verify) against a sourced everyday object (a number of cars, or a loaded fire truck).
- Twist (by 0:45): **for most of the 20th century, the most famous dinosaur in the world was wearing somebody else's head.** Then it lost
  its name, and then it got it back.
- Doug beat: Doug walks up to the boxy-headed end holding the 1883-style card ("hi friend?"). The real head is somewhere else, about
  twenty meters away, and so is the foot. Dust cloud. Death 155. "Doug did not agree to this." comes right before, when the narrator
  hands him the card.

**2. Iguanodon** (Doug survives; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - Mantell described Iguanodon in 1825 from teeth, and his sketch put a single conical spike on its nose (verify).
  - At Crystal Palace (1854) it was built as a giant four-legged reptile; a dinner was held inside the mould on New Year's Eve 1853
    (verify the date and the number of guests).
  - In 1878 miners at Bernissart, Belgium, found dozens of skeletons in a coal mine (verify count and depth). Dollo's work showed the
    spike was a **thumb**.
  - What the thumb was for is still argued (defence, food handling). **Do not claim it was a weapon.**
- Analogy idea: thumb spike length against a sourced everyday object (a TV remote, a banana). Verify the size.
- Twist: **the horn was a thumb.**
- Doug beat: Doug wears a fencing mask to protect his face from the nose horn. The Iguanodon gives him a thumbs up (callback to Doug's
  own pose; art director confirms). "Doug is fine." Counter stays at 155.

**3. Elasmosaurus** (death 156)
- Facts:
  - In 1868-69 Cope reconstructed Elasmosaurus with the head on the end of the tail; Leidy pointed out the mistake (verify the story and
    how Cope tried to recall the publication).
  - It is often given as a trigger of the "Bone Wars" rivalry with Marsh (attribute; verify).
  - The neck had about 70 vertebrae and was about 7 m long (verify both).
- Analogy idea: neck length against a sourced object (a giraffe is about 2 m of neck; a city bus about 12 m). Verify.
- Twist: **the head was on the wrong end.** Doug picked the end he thought was safe.
- Doug beat: Doug swims toward the "tail" end with the card. The tail end has no mouth. The other end does, and it is seven meters
  behind him. Splash. Death 156. The cap floats.

### Wrong body.

**4. Stegosaurus** (death 157)
- Facts:
  - Marsh (1877) first thought the plates lay flat over the back like roof tiles, hence "roof lizard" (verify).
  - The plates stood upright in two rows, most likely alternating (verify; say "most likely" if contested).
  - "Thagomizer", the name for the tail spikes, comes from a 1982 *Far Side* cartoon by Gary Larson and was adopted by paleontologists
    (verify). One dry line.
  - An Allosaurus tail vertebra with a puncture that matches a Stegosaurus spike (Carpenter et al. 2005; verify).
- Analogy idea: spike length against a sourced object (a baseball bat). Verify.
- Twist: **the roof was a warning sign, and the danger was at the other end.**
- Doug beat: Doug climbs on top to "sit on the roof". It isn't flat. He slides off the back toward the tail. Cut away to dust. Death 157.
  No contact shown.

**5. Deinocheirus** (Doug survives; narrator quietly disappointed)
- Facts:
  - In 1965 a Polish-Mongolian expedition found only a pair of arms about 2.4 m long with large claws (verify), and for almost 50 years
    the animal was imagined as a giant clawed predator.
  - Lee et al. 2014 (*Nature*): new skeletons showed a humpbacked, duck-billed animal about 11 m long that ate plants and fish, with
    fish remains and gastroliths in the gut area (verify all).
  - The missing skull and feet had been poached and were returned from a private collection in Europe (verify the story and dates).
- Analogy idea: arm length against a sourced object (taller than a doorway? verify).
- Twist: **fifty years of nightmares about the claws, and the rest of the animal turned out to be a duck with a hump.**
- Doug beat: Doug brings a big shield for the claws. The animal ignores him and eats a fish. Survival. The narrator says one dry line
  about drawing those claws for 50 years for nothing. (Don't reuse "had the death drawn already" or "sharpened the pencils".)

**6. Hallucigenia** (death 158)
- Facts:
  - Conway Morris (1977) described it from the Burgess Shale walking on its spines, with its legs taken for tentacles on its back
    (verify).
  - Ramsköld & Hou (1991) flipped it over; Smith & Caron 2015 (*Nature*) found the head was at the other end, with eyes and a ring of
    teeth (verify).
  - About 508 million years old; a few centimetres long (verify).
  - Burgess Shale animals were preserved when mud flows buried them (verify the mechanism wording).
  - One callback clause allowed: the same rock gave us Anomalocaris (009).
- Analogy idea: length against a sourced small object (a paper clip). Verify.
- Twist: **upside down, and back to front. It was wrong in every direction it is possible to be wrong.**
- Doug beat: Doug lies on his back to see it "the 1977 way". It's fine. The mud flow that preserved it is not. Death 158 (buried in
  mud, cartoon, cap on top of the mud). This is the death that made the fossil, so it's the reason we know the animal at all.

### Wrong animal.

**7. Dimetrodon** (death 159)
- Facts:
  - Named by Cope in 1878; it is routinely sold in dinosaur toy sets, but it lived tens of millions of years before the first dinosaurs
    (verify the gap).
  - It is a synapsid, on the line that leads to mammals, so it is more closely related to us than to any dinosaur (verify the wording).
  - It was a top predator of its time, with serrated teeth (Brink & Reisz 2014, *Nature Communications*; verify).
- Analogy idea: the time gap between Dimetrodon and T. rex against a sourced comparison (Dimetrodon is further from T. rex in time than
  T. rex is from us; verify the numbers before using).
- Twist: **it's not a dinosaur. It's family.** Doug's distant cousin.
- Doug beat: Doug brings a family photo ("cousin?"). Death 159. The cap is left on the sail like a hat on a coat rack.

**8. Therizinosaurus** (death 160; no bit)
- Facts:
  - Maleev (1954) described the claws as belonging to a giant turtle-like reptile that may have used them to cut seaweed (verify).
  - It is a theropod, in the same broad group as T. rex, that ate plants (verify).
  - The claws are among the longest of any known animal (attribute; verify the bony-core length, about 50 cm, and whether a sheath made
    them longer).
- Analogy idea: claw length against a sourced object (a baseball bat or a forearm). Don't use the ballpoint pen (011) or anything 018 used.
- Twist: **the turtle was a dinosaur, and the dinosaur was a vegetarian, and none of that helps Doug.** Short fragment burst.
- Doug beat: Doug brings seaweed as a peace offering for a turtle. He stands between it and a tree it wanted. Death 160, said flat.

**9. Homo diluvii testis** (Doug survives; the one human in the episode is not Doug)
- Facts:
  - In 1726 Johann Scheuchzer described a fossil from Öhningen as a human who had witnessed Noah's flood (verify date and place).
  - In 1811 Cuvier chipped away the rock in front of witnesses and exposed the forelimbs of a giant salamander (verify the year).
  - It is now *Andrias scheuchzeri*, related to the living Chinese giant salamander, which reaches about 1.8 m (verify).
- Analogy idea: salamander length against Doug's height or a sourced everyday object (a sofa).
- Twist: **for 85 years, a museum displayed a salamander as a man.**
- Doug beat: Doug lies down next to the slab to compare ("same?" was used in 018, so the writer writes a new bubble). A giant salamander
  walks past, ignores him. "Doug is fine." This is the respite before the boss. Played straight; no religion joke (state Scheuchzer's
  claim as history, no mockery).

### Still wrong.

**10. Spinosaurus** (the boss; death 161)
- Facts:
  - Stromer described it in 1915 from Egypt; the original bones were destroyed when Munich was bombed in April 1944 (verify).
  - It has been redrawn repeatedly: an upright land predator, then a crocodile-faced fish-eater, then Ibrahim et al. 2014 (*Science*,
    short hind legs, semi-aquatic), then Ibrahim et al. 2020 (*Nature*, a paddle-like tail) (verify each).
  - Whether it swam after prey or waded like a heron is still argued: Fabbri et al. 2022 (*Nature*, dense bones) vs Sereno et al. 2022
    (*eLife*, wader) (verify; present both, no winner).
  - Length estimates are among the largest of any predatory dinosaur, about 14 m (verify; give the range and say they are estimates).
- Analogy idea: length against a sourced object (a city bus is about 12 m). Use only if it's not repeated from 009's Tylosaurus line;
  otherwise a tennis-court width or similar.
- Twist (the episode's thesis): **one of the most redrawn predators in paleontology (attribute; don't claim "the most" unless sourced) still doesn't have a final drawing, so Doug can't know which
  Spinosaurus is coming.** Fragment burst: "On land. In the river. Under it."
- Doug beat: Doug arrives with a stack of four cards, one per version. He holds them up one by one. The real one is the one he didn't
  bring. Death 161.
- **Closing image:** the stack of cards on a riverbank in Cretaceous Morocco, with the cap on top. A fifth blank card slides into the
  frame. The narrator hasn't drawn it yet. Kicker: one dry line that the narrator will draw Doug's version "when scientists agree".

Deaths: 7 (155-161). Survivals: Iguanodon, Deinocheirus, Homo diluvii testis. 10 items. **Option for the writer:** if the draft runs
under 2,900 words, add **"Megalosaurus"** between items 4 and 5 (Wrong body.: Buckland 1824, the first named dinosaur, built as a
four-legged "giant lizard" at Crystal Palace; verify) as a survival. Don't add a death; the boss stays 161.

## 7. Risks
- **Kid-appeal risk (HIGH):** dinosaurs are the most kid-searched topic in our lane.
  - "Dinosaur" stays out of the title and the thumbnail labels. Narrator voice stays dry and adult; the humour is about bad science and
    Doug's choices, not cute creatures.
  - Animals drawn at the detailed cartoon tier, never big-eyed, no babies, no roaring sound effects, no "dino" nicknames.
  - Adult-coded jokes: the Bone Wars rivalry, the dinner inside the Iguanodon, the *Far Side* name, the poached skull.
  - Metadata never targets kids.
- **Gore (MEDIUM):** Stegosaurus spike, Therizinosaurus claws and the Spinosaurus teeth. **No claw or spike touches Doug on screen.**
  Deaths cut to dust, splash or mud. No injury talk. The Allosaurus puncture is described as a hole in a bone, nothing more.
- **Mockery risk (LOW-MEDIUM):** the old scientists worked with fragments. Frame errors as reasonable guesses, not stupidity. No jokes at
  Scheuchzer's religion. The Cope/Marsh rivalry is fair game as history.
- **Accuracy risk (HIGH):** this episode is about corrections, so we can't make new errors.
  - Where a question is open (Spinosaurus swimming, Iguanodon thumb use, Stegosaurus plate pattern), say it is open.
  - Don't present a single modern reconstruction as final.
  - No "Brontosaurus never existed" (outdated since 2015).
- **Copyright:** we redraw the old reconstructions in our own style. Historical plates only with verified public-domain status. No
  modern paleoart, no museum photos without a licence.
- **Assets:** library has `sauropod.json`, `plesiosaur.json`, `dinosaur_skeleton.json`, `feathered_dinosaur.json`, `time_machine.json`,
  `museum_building.json`, `library_book.json`. Likely requests:
