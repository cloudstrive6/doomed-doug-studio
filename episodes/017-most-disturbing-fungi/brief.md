### Insects.

**1. Massospora** (Massospora cicadina, the periodical-cicada fungus; the hook item; Doug in a cicada costume; death 140)
- Facts:
  - It infects periodical cicadas. The back part of the cicada's abdomen is replaced by a chalky plug of spores, and the segments fall away.
  - The cicada keeps flying, calling and trying to mate, scattering spores as it goes (Cooley et al. 2018, *Fungal Ecology*; verify).
  - Infected males also make the wing-flick that females use, so other males try to mate with them too (Cooley et al. 2018; verify).
  - The fungus contains cathinone, an amphetamine-like compound; a relative contains psilocybin (Boyce et al. 2019, *Fungal Ecology*). Say it
    once, flat. No drug jokes, no "trip" humour.
  - It can't infect people (state once: "which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a cicada").
- Analogy idea: the spore plug against a sourced everyday object (a pencil eraser) if a size source exists.
- Twist (by 0:45): **its back half falls off, and it keeps going. It's not dying. It's delivering.**
- Doug beat: Doug the cicada keeps waving to the others with half a costume; the costume's back end pops off like a cork, Doug doesn't notice.
  A bird. Death 140, cap on a branch. Keep the tone dry, nothing sexual drawn or spoken beyond "tries to mate".

**2. Death-Pose Fly Fungus** (Entomophthora muscae; Doug in a house-fly costume; death 141)
- Facts:
  - Infected flies climb to a high spot near sunset, stick their mouthparts to the surface, raise their wings and die in that pose
    (Elya et al. 2018, *eLife*; verify timing).
  - Hours later the fungus bursts through the body in bands and fires spores (verify the "shoots" mechanism and distance).
  - Male flies are drawn to dead infected females, more than to living ones; the fungus releases chemicals that lure them in (Naundrup et al.
    2022, *The ISME Journal*; verify).
  - Callback (allowed, one line): 002's zombie ant fungus also made its host climb. The difference: this one sets an alarm for sunset.
- Analogy idea: spores fired a sourced distance compared with body length (a person throwing something across a sourced space).
- Twist: **it doesn't only kill the fly. It turns the body into a trap for the next one.**
- Doug beat: Doug the fly climbs a window at sunset, wings up, very proud of the pose. Death 141 (cartoon fuzz bands on the costume, cap stuck to
  the glass). No close-up of the burst; draw it as stripes and a puff.

**3. The Cave Spider Fungus** (Gibellula attenboroughii; Doug in a spider costume; death 142)
- Facts:
  - Found by a BBC film crew in a cave in Northern Ireland in 2021, formally described later and named after David Attenborough
    (Evans et al. 2025; verify the journal, year and cave).
  - It infects cave orb-weaver spiders (Metellina merianae, Meta menardi; verify). Infected spiders leave their webs and die exposed on the cave
    ceiling, where air currents can carry spores (attribute the hypothesis).
  - Scientists suspect it changes the spider's behaviour, but it's not proven (attribute; don't overclaim).
- Analogy idea: the spider's normal web versus a sourced household object (a dinner plate) if a source gives the size; otherwise skip.
- Twist: **a spider that never leaves its web walks out into the open to die, and nobody had noticed until a film crew did.**
- Doug beat: Doug the spider abandons his web and strolls onto the ceiling, waving. Death 142 (white fuzz, cap hanging from one thread).
  Spider costume is drawn at creature tier, not cute.

### Whole species.

**4. Chytrid** (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, Bd; Doug in a frog costume; death 143)
- Facts:
  - A fungus of amphibian skin. Frogs drink and breathe partly through their skin, and Bd disrupts that, which can stop the heart through
    electrolyte loss (Voyles et al. 2009, *Science*; verify the wording).
  - Linked to declines of at least 501 amphibian species, including 90 presumed extinctions: the greatest recorded loss of biodiversity
    attributable to a disease (Scheele et al. 2019, *Science*; attribute; mention that some scientists dispute the count, Lambert et al. 2020).
  - It spread around the world with the trade in amphibians (O'Hanlon et al. 2018, *Science*; Korean peninsula origin; verify).
  - Can't infect people (one line, costume rule).
- Analogy idea: 90 species gone against a sourced everyday set (more species than a sourced zoo's whole frog house), only if sourced.
- Twist: **it isn't killing frogs. It's killing the idea of frogs, one species at a time.** (Writer may rephrase; the point is species,
  not individuals.)
- Doug beat: Doug the frog sits in a pond reading a pamphlet about clean water; the water is clean. Death 143. The cap floats on a lily pad.
  A sober card follows: "90 species, presumed extinct" (no Doug, no joke).

**5. White-Nose Syndrome** (Pseudogymnoascus destructans; Doug in a bat costume; death 144)
- Facts:
  - First documented in a cave near Albany, New York, in a 2006 photo (USGS / USFWS; verify).
  - It grows on hibernating bats' muzzles and wings in the cold. Bats wake far more often in winter and burn through the fat they needed to
    reach spring (Reeder et al. 2012, *PLoS ONE*; verify).
  - It had killed more than 5.7-6.7 million bats in North America by 2012 (USFWS estimate; verify the exact range and wording).
  - The northern long-eared bat was reclassified as endangered in 2023 (USFWS; verify date).
  - Can't infect people (one line).
- Analogy idea: a bat waking in winter is like turning the heating on full blast, with a sourced fraction of the fuel tank (keep it as an
  energy analogy, not a symptom).
- Twist: **it doesn't kill the bat. It keeps waking it up until it starves in its sleep.** (Say "runs out of energy" on screen.)
- Doug beat: Doug the bat hangs upside down, sets an alarm, is woken, hangs back, woken again (three quick beats). Death 144: flat bat
  costume with a white cartoon nose, cap on the cave floor.

**6. Valley Fever** (Coccidioides; Doug survives; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - It lives in the soil of dry areas in the US Southwest and parts of Latin America; people breathe in spores from dust (CDC).
  - About 60% of people infected have no symptoms (CDC; verify).
  - After the 1994 Northridge earthquake, landslides raised dust clouds and a Ventura County outbreak followed: 203 cases, 3 deaths
    (Schneider et al. 1997, *JAMA*; verify).
- Analogy idea: the dust cloud against a sourced landmark (only if the paper gives a height or size).
- Twist: **an earthquake shook a fungus out of the ground and into a county's lungs.** (Keep "lungs" as the only body word.)
- Doug beat: Doug stands in the dust cloud with a hard hat, coughs once, and is fine. **"Doug is fine."** Counter stays 144. The narrator
  notes, flatly, that most people never know they had it, and Doug is most people. The three real deaths go on a sober card, no Doug.
  **No medical advice:** no symptoms list, no "see a doctor if".

**7. Ergot** (Claviceps purpurea; St. Anthony's Fire; death 145)
- Facts:
  - It grows on rye and replaces the grain with a dark spur that looks like a grain (verify).
  - Medieval outbreaks of ergotism were called St. Anthony's Fire for the burning feeling; whole towns ate contaminated bread (verify an
    authoritative history source; give one dated outbreak only if sourced).
  - It has been proposed as a cause of the Salem witch accusations (Caporael 1976, *Science*). Other researchers rejected it (Spanos and
    Gottlieb 1976, *Science*). **Say both.**
  - Optional, one clause: ergot compounds led to modern migraine and childbirth medicines (verify; no drug-culture reference, no LSD).
- Analogy idea: the ergot spur next to a sourced object (a grain of rice / a paperclip) for size.
- Twist: **for centuries the poison was the bread, and nobody could see the difference.**
- Doug beat: Doug buys a loaf at a medieval market and eats it. Death 145 (cartoon: Doug glows faintly orange, X eyes, ghost floats up). No
  gangrene, no limbs, no symptom detail, ever.

**8. The Tomb Fungus** (Aspergillus flavus and the tomb of Casimir IV; Doug survives)
- Facts:
  - In 1973 researchers opened the tomb of King Casimir IV Jagiellon in Kraków's Wawel Cathedral; several of them died within a few years
    (verify the number and the time span; newspapers called it a curse).
  - Aspergillus flavus was found in the tomb and has been blamed for lung infections in some of them (verify; attribute "blamed").
  - In 2025 a team at the University of Pennsylvania reported compounds from A. flavus that killed leukaemia cells in the lab
    (Nie et al. 2025, *Nature Chemical Biology*; verify; say "in the lab", not a cure).
- Analogy idea: none forced; use the 500-year seal against a sourced age (older than a sourced landmark).
- Twist: **the "curse" fungus is now being tested as a cancer drug lead.**
- Doug beat: Doug opens the tomb wearing a paper crown and says "hi king?". Nothing happens. **"Doug is fine."** Counter stays 145. The
  narrator is quietly disappointed ("the curse was mostly a newspaper"). Do not reuse 011/013's "death drawn already" line.

**9. Cryptococcus gattii** (the Vancouver Island outbreak; death 146)
- Facts:
  - In 1999 people and animals on Vancouver Island began falling ill with a fungus thought to live mostly in the tropics and subtropics
    (BC CDC; Kidd et al. 2004, *PNAS*; verify).
  - Unlike most dangerous fungi, it infected people with healthy immune systems (attribute; CDC).
  - It spread to mainland BC, Washington and Oregon (CDC; verify), and it was found living in trees and soil there.
  - Case count and deaths: use the BC CDC / CDC numbers only (verify).
- Analogy idea: the distance from its usual range to Vancouver Island as a sourced flight or drive.
- Twist: **it was a tropical fungus, and it moved somewhere cold, and it didn't need anyone to be sick first.**
- Doug beat: Doug hugs a tree on a nature walk ("hi friend?"). Death 146 (cartoon; a puff of spores, flat Doug, cap hung on a branch).

**10. Candida auris** (the boss; death 147)
- Facts:
  - First described in 2009 from a patient's ear in Japan (Satoh et al. 2009; verify).
  - It appeared on three continents at about the same time as genetically separate strains (Lockhart et al. 2017, *Clinical Infectious
    Diseases*; verify).
  - Many strains resist more than one class of antifungal drug; it persists on hospital surfaces and spreads between patients (CDC; verify
    wording). The CDC called it an urgent threat (2019 report) and WHO put it in its critical priority group (2022 fungal priority list).
  - Most fungi can't grow at our body temperature, about 37 °C; that heat is one reason mammals rarely get fungal infections (Robert and
    Casadevall 2009, *Journal of Infectious Diseases*; attribute).
  - One hypothesis: C. auris adapted to warmer temperatures as the climate warmed, which let it cross that barrier (Casadevall et al. 2019,
    *mBio*). **It is a hypothesis; say so.**
- Analogy idea: 37 °C against a sourced everyday temperature (a warm bath), "the fence around every mammal".
- Twist (the thesis line): **our body heat has been a wall against fungi for millions of years, and this might be the first one learning to
  climb it.**
- Doug beat: Doug turns his thermostat up to 37 °C, stands in the warm room, smug. A cartoon spore puff walks in and sits beside him.
  Death 147 (fuzzy white Doug silhouette, cap on top). **No patients, no hospital beds, no ill people drawn.** The hospital appears only as an
  outside building with a sober card for the real numbers.
- **Closing image:** a slow push-in on the cap resting on a windowsill in the warm room; a single white fuzz spot appears on the brim. One dry
  kicker about the cap (e.g. it has outlived 147 Dougs and is now, apparently, food). The outro gives the counter and one comment prompt.

Deaths: 8 (140-147). Survivals: Valley Fever (6) and The Tomb Fungus (8). 10 items. **Option for the writer:** if under 2,900 words, add one

hit and survives), or extend items 1, 4 and 10. Do not add a death; the boss stays 147.
