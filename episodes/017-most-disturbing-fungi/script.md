## Massospora

Massospora.

[Map dot on the eastern United States. A forest floor cross-section: cicada nymphs underground, a counter reading "17 YEARS".]

Periodical cicadas spend thirteen or seventeen years underground, then climb out all at once for about a month of singing and mating, and Massospora is waiting for them.

[Spores in the soil drift up to meet a nymph as it digs. A cicada, creature tier, next to a paperclip of the same length.]

Spores left in the soil by an earlier generation infect the young cicadas as they dig their way up. On an insect about the length of a paperclip, the fungus eats away the lower half of the abdomen from the inside.

[The back half of the abdomen turns chalky white. The last segments drop off.]

The joints between the last few segments break, and those segments simply drop off, leaving a chalky white plug made entirely of spores.

The disturbing part is that the cicada doesn't stop.

[The half-cicada flies on, a soft trail of white dust behind it.]

It keeps flying around and trying to mate, and every landing shakes spores onto the next cicada in line. It isn't dying. It's delivering.

Infected males even start flicking their wings the way females do when they're ready to mate, so healthy males come straight to them and leave covered in spores.

[Label: "CATHINONE".]

Chemists have also found cathinone inside infected cicadas, a stimulant related to amphetamine, and the researchers think it may help keep the insects going while they fall apart.

Later in the season, a second form of the fungus makes tough resting spores instead, which fall into the soil and wait for the next generation to come up.

Massospora is built for cicadas and can't infect people, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a cicada.

[Doug the cicada waves to the other cicadas. The back end of his costume pops off like a cork. He doesn't notice. A bird lands. Doug, X eyes, ghost floats up. The cap sits on a branch. DOUG DEATHS: 140.]

Doug kept waving at the other cicadas while the back of his costume popped off like a cork. He didn't notice. The bird did.

Seventeen years underground, one month in the sun, and the fungus gets most of the month.

## Death-Pose Fly Fungus

Death-Pose Fly Fungus.

[A forest-green window at dusk. A fly on the glass, wings raised. Doug in a house-fly costume, cap on top.]

Its scientific name is Entomophthora muscae, but the people who study it usually recognise it by something simpler: the exact pose its flies are found dead in.

[A calendar: days four to seven circled. A clock face with the sun setting.]

In a lab study, infected flies died four to seven days after they were exposed, and on the final day they made their last move in the five hours before sunset.

[The fly climbs a stalk. A small sticky dot appears under its mouth. The wings lift in small jerks.]

The fly climbs to a high spot and presses its mouthparts against the surface, where a sticky droplet glues it in place. Then, in small bursts, its wings lift up and away from its body, and it dies exactly like that. In the lab, about nine out of ten dying flies raised their wings this way.

Doug has met a fungus like this before, when an ant fungus made him climb a plant in his parasite days. This one adds an alarm clock.

[Night. Pale stripes appear across the fly's body. Tiny puffs fire outward. Next to it, a stick sprinter with the label "9.58 SECONDS".]

About five hours after sunset, the fungus breaks out through the body in pale bands and starts firing spores. Each one leaves at about ten meters per second, close to the average speed of Usain Bolt's world-record hundred meters.

The worst part is what the fungus does with the body next.

[A dead female fly on the glass. Male flies turn their heads toward her. Soft scent lines rise from her.]

On dead female house flies, the fungus gives off a blend of chemicals that draws in healthy males. They try to mate with the dead, infected female, and that close contact is exactly how they catch it.

The fly fungus doesn't infect people either, hence the fly costume.

[Doug the fly climbs the window at sunset, sets his mouthparts, raises his wings, very proud of the pose. Stripes and a puff. X eyes. Ghost Doug rises. The cap stays stuck to the glass. DOUG DEATHS: 141.]

Doug climbed the window at sunset, raised his wings, and was very proud of the pose. He held it permanently.

Doug has died. Again.

It doesn't only kill the fly, it turns the body into bait for the next one.

## The Cave Spider Fungus

The Cave Spider Fungus.

[Map, red dot on Northern Ireland. A brick storeroom with a sign: "GUNPOWDER STORE". A film camera on a tripod.]

In twenty twenty-one, a BBC crew was filming the nature show Winterwatch at Castle Espie, a wetland reserve in Northern Ireland, when a volunteer looked up.

[A volunteer with a torch points at the ceiling. A spider covered in soft white fuzz.]

On the ceiling of an old Victorian gunpowder store was a spider coated in white fungus. DNA tests later showed that nobody had ever described this fungus before. In January twenty twenty-five, scientists named it Gibellula attenboroughii, after David Attenborough.

[A cave spider, creature tier, next to a credit card. Its legs span roughly the card's short side.]

It infects orb-weaving cave spiders. The bigger of the two known hosts has a legspan of around five centimeters, about the height of a credit card, and both species normally keep to dim, damp places.

Here's the eerie part. The infected spiders leave their webs and hiding spots and walk out onto open ceilings and walls to die.

[A spider walks out of its web and across a bare cave ceiling. A faint arrow for air moving through the cave.]

Out in the open, where air moves and other spiders pass by, the spores have a better chance of spreading. The researchers suspect the fungus is steering the spider, the way some fungi steer ants, but nobody has proven that yet.

Since then, more infected spiders have turned up on cave roofs and walls across Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland. Old records also hint at similar spider die-offs in parts of England and Wales.

Its whole genus attacks only spiders, so Doug is a spider.

[Doug the spider leaves his web and strolls onto the ceiling, waving. White fuzz grows over the costume. X eyes. The cap hangs from a single thread. DOUG DEATHS: 142.]

Doug left his web and strolled across the ceiling to say hello to everyone. Everyone could see him. That was the idea.

Doug has died. Again.

A spider that lives in the dark walked into the open to die, for a fungus nobody had even named.

## Chytrid

Chytrid.

[Swamp-olive pond. A frog sits on a rock. Close-up of its skin as a soft cartoon pattern of dots.]

Every fungus so far has taken one animal at a time, but this one is counted in species.

Chytrid is a fungus that lives in the skin of frogs, toads and other amphibians, which is a bad place for a fungus to be, because frogs drink and breathe partly through their skin.

[A frog outline with arrows of water and salts moving through the skin. The arrows thin out.]

The fungus wrecks the way that skin moves salts in and out. In a study on green tree frogs, salt transport across the skin dropped by more than half, the salts in the blood fell, and in the end the heart simply stopped.

[A map of the world. One dot on the Korean peninsula, then arrows fanning out along shipping routes.]

Genetic studies trace the strain behind the outbreak to the Korean peninsula. It began spreading in the early twentieth century, around the same time the global trade in amphibians took off, and it rode that trade around the world.

[A deck of fifty-two cards. Three and a half cards turn grey.]

Here comes the part that's hard to say out loud. A major study in twenty nineteen linked chytrid to the decline of at least five hundred and one amphibian species. That's roughly six and a half percent of every known amphibian, or more than three cards out of every fifty-two card deck.

Ninety of those species are presumed extinct. The declines peaked in the nineteen eighties, and only about one in eight of the affected species shows any sign of recovery.

[Sober card on a dark olive background, no Doug: "90 SPECIES, PRESUMED EXTINCT".]

The study's authors called it the greatest recorded loss of biodiversity caused by a disease. Other scientists have challenged how that count was made, and the authors have defended it.

It isn't killing frogs. It's deleting kinds of frog.

Chytrid isn't known to affect humans, but Doug is a frog anyway.

[Doug the frog sits in a clean pond reading a pamphlet titled "CLEAN WATER". The water is clean. X eyes. Ghost Doug rises. The cap floats on a lily pad. DOUG DEATHS: 143.]

Doug sat in a perfectly clean pond and read a pamphlet about how clean it was. The water really was clean.

Doug has died. Again.

Nobody had to poison the pond, because the fungus came in riding on the frogs.

## White-Nose Syndrome

White-Nose Syndrome.

[Cave-grey. Bats hanging in a cave, a camera flash. Label: "FEBRUARY 2006".]

In February twenty oh six, a caver about forty miles west of Albany, New York, photographed hibernating bats with something white on their noses, and noticed several dead bats nearby.

[Close-up: a bat's muzzle and wings with soft white fuzz.]

The white was a fungus called Pseudogymnoascus destructans. It loves the cold, and it grows on the muzzles, ears and wings of bats while they hibernate and can't do anything about it.

[A bat hanging still. A small fat-reserve bar beside it.]

A hibernating bat lives on a fixed amount of stored fat, and that fat has to last the whole winter.

[A thermostat dial spun to MAX. A fuel gauge dropping fast.]

Even healthy bats wake up now and then over the winter, and every wake-up burns fat. Bats with this fungus wake up far more often, and the more they wake, the sooner the infected ones die. A government study found infected bats burned twice as much energy over the winter as healthy ones did. It's like heating your house all winter on half a tank of fuel.

The catch is that the fungus doesn't have to kill the bat directly. It just keeps waking it up until the bat runs out.

[A tally on a cave wall. The number climbs. A map of North America filling in.]

By twenty twelve, biologists estimated it had killed at least five point seven to six point seven million bats in North America. Three species have dropped by more than ninety percent, and by twenty twenty-three, the disease had reached forty states and eight Canadian provinces.

That same year, the northern long-eared bat was reclassified as endangered.

No human illness has ever been linked to it, which is what the bat costume is for.

[Doug the bat hangs upside down, sets an alarm clock, closes his eyes. The alarm rings. He hangs back. It rings. He hangs back. It rings. He drops, flat bat costume, white cartoon nose, X eyes. The cap lands on the cave floor. DOUG DEATHS: 144.]

Doug hung upside down and set his alarm for spring. It went off in January. Then again. Then again.

Doug has died. Again.

He didn't die of the cold, he died of getting up too often.

## Valley Fever

Valley Fever.

[Doug unzips the bat costume and steps out, himself again. Dry hills, dusty soil, a desert sun.]

The costumes come off now, because every fungus from here on can reach people, starting with one that lives in the dirt.

Valley fever is caused by a fungus called Coccidioides, which lives in the soil of dry areas in the southwestern United States, Mexico, and parts of Central and South America. People catch it by breathing in spores from dust.

[A human hair drawn as a thick log. A row of tiny dots lined up across its width.]

The spores are tiny, about two to five micrometers long, so more than a dozen of them, lined up end to end, would fit across a single human hair.

[Map dot on Northridge, California. The ground shakes. Hillsides slide. A dust cloud rolls into a valley town.]

Here's the twist. On January seventeenth, nineteen ninety-four, the Northridge earthquake hit Southern California. It set off landslides in the mountains near Simi Valley, and those landslides threw up huge clouds of dust that drifted down into the towns below, with the spores inside them.

[A counter ticks up to 203.]

Over the next eight weeks, Ventura County recorded two hundred and three cases, roughly ten times what doctors would normally expect, with the most new cases appearing about two weeks after the earthquake. More than half of them were in Simi Valley, and people caught in the dust clouds had about three times the odds of being diagnosed. It was the first outbreak of its kind ever reported after an earthquake.

[Sober card, cave-grey, no Doug: "3 PEOPLE DIED".]

Three people died.

[Doug stands in the dust cloud in a hard hat. He coughs once. He gives a thumbs up. DOUG DEATHS: 144.]

Doug stood in the middle of the dust cloud in a hard hat and coughed exactly once.

Doug is fine.

About sixty percent of people infected never have any symptoms at all, and never know they had it. Doug is most people.

An earthquake shook a fungus out of a mountain and into a county's lungs.

## Ergot

Ergot.

[A medieval village, a field of rye. Close-up of a rye head with one long dark spur sticking out where a grain should be.]

For most of history, nobody knew this was a fungus, so people just ground it up and ate it.

Ergot is a fungus that grows on rye, and it replaces the grain with a hard spur, dark purple to black, sitting right where the kernel should be. Some spurs are only a couple of millimeters long, while others grow to about ten times the size of a normal grain.

[A baker's sack of rye. A few dark spurs mixed in. Then a loaf of bread.]

At harvest, the spurs were ground into flour along with everything else, and in parts of Europe, poor families lived largely on rye bread.

[A stone hospital with a cross on the door. Label: "ORDER OF ST. ANTHONY, 1095".]

People called the illness holy fire, for the burning it caused, and later Saint Anthony's Fire, after an order of monks founded in ten ninety-five to care for its victims. That order went on to build more than three hundred and seventy hospitals.

[Sober card, swamp-olive, no Doug: "SOUTHERN FRANCE, 990 TO 1130: AN ESTIMATED 50,000+ DEATHS".]

Between the years nine ninety and eleven thirty, an estimated fifty thousand people or more died of it in southern France alone.

The unsettling part is that for centuries, the poison was the bread, and nobody could see the difference. Scientists didn't even suggest that the spur was a fungus until seventeen eleven.

[A courtroom in Salem, 1692. A question mark over a loaf.]

In nineteen seventy-six, a researcher proposed in the journal Science that ergot might explain the Salem witch accusations. That same year, two other researchers replied in the same journal that the evidence didn't support it, and many historians remain unconvinced.

[A plain modern pill bottle, no label text.]

Ergot did leave something useful behind. Chemicals from it became medicines, including one still used for migraine and one used to control bleeding after childbirth.

[Doug at a medieval market, buys a loaf, takes a big bite. He glows faintly orange. X eyes. Ghost Doug floats up. The cap sits on the bread stall. DOUG DEATHS: 145.]

Doug bought a fresh loaf at the market and ate half of it on the spot, announcing it was the best bread he had ever had.

Doug has died. Again.

Somewhere in that loaf was a black spur that looked exactly like a grain.

## The Tomb Fungus

The Tomb Fungus.

[Kraków, Wawel Cathedral at night. A stone tomb, sealed. Label: "1492".]

King Casimir the Fourth of Poland died in fourteen ninety-two, the same year Columbus first sailed across the Atlantic, and he was laid to rest in a tomb in Wawel Cathedral, in Kraków.

[1973. A group of stick researchers with lamps open the lid. Soft dust drifts out.]

The tomb stayed sealed for nearly five hundred years. In nineteen seventy-three, a team of about a dozen researchers opened it, and within a short time, ten of them had died.

[A newspaper headline: "CURSE".]

The newspapers called it the curse of the king. Later investigations found a mould in the tomb called Aspergillus flavus, and that mould has been blamed for the deaths.

[A bag of peanuts and an ear of corn, each with a small spot of yellow-green mould.]

Aspergillus flavus is not exotic at all. It's the same mould that can grow on stored peanuts and corn, and its spores can cause lung infections, mostly in people whose immune systems are already weak.

[Sober card, cave-grey, no Doug: "10 OF 12 RESEARCHERS DIED".]

But here's the strange part. The curse fungus is now a lead for a cancer drug.

[A University of Pennsylvania lab. A petri dish of yellow-green colonies, then a vial labelled "ASPERIGIMYCINS".]

In twenty twenty-five, scientists at the University of Pennsylvania pulled a new family of molecules out of Aspergillus flavus and named them asperigimycins. In lab dishes, two of them had strong effects against leukemia cells, and one modified version did about as well as two drugs already used to treat leukemia.

So far that's only in the lab, on cells, with animal testing next, and it's a long way from a cure.

[Doug in a paper crown pushes open the tomb. Speech bubble: "hi king?". Nothing happens. A small cloud of dust settles on his cap. DOUG DEATHS: 145.]

Doug opened the tomb wearing a paper crown and said, "hi king?" Nothing happened at all.

Doug is fine.

We had high hopes for this one, but the curse, it turns out, was mostly a newspaper headline.

## Cryptococcus gattii

Cryptococcus gattii.

[Forest green, darker now. Vancouver Island on a map, tall Douglas fir trees.]

Until the late nineteen nineties, this fungus was mostly known from tropical and subtropical regions, often around eucalyptus trees in places like Australia.

[A dog, a cat, a porpoise and a llama outlines, each with a small question mark.]

Then, starting in nineteen ninety-nine, people and animals on Vancouver Island, off the west coast of Canada, began falling ill with it. The animals included dogs, cats, ferrets, porpoises, llamas and even a horse, and some were found before the first human cases.

[Spore puffs on bark, in the soil, in the air, in the water.]

Scientists went looking and found it living on Douglas fir trees, in the soil around them, and even floating in the air and the water. It wasn't passing through. It had moved in, and scientists still can't say for sure whether it arrived recently or had been there unnoticed all along.

Here's the problem. Most dangerous fungi go after people whose immune systems are already weak, and this one also infects healthy people.

[A baseball stadium, every seat full. One seat glows.]

On Vancouver Island, the yearly rate came to about twenty-five cases per million people, which is roughly one person a year from every sold-out crowd at Wrigley Field. British Columbia has the largest reported group of people infected with it anywhere in the world.

[Sober card, dark forest green, no Doug: "BRITISH COLUMBIA, 1999 TO 2007: 218 CASES, 19 DEATHS".]

From nineteen ninety-nine to two thousand seven, British Columbia recorded two hundred and eighteen cases and nineteen deaths.

[Map: arrows from Vancouver Island to mainland British Columbia, Washington and Oregon.]

It didn't stay on the island, either. Cases have since turned up on the British Columbia mainland and in Washington and Oregon.

[Doug on a forest trail. He wraps his arms around a big fir. Speech bubble: "hi friend?". A puff of spores. Doug lies flat, X eyes. Ghost Doug rises. The cap hangs on a branch. DOUG DEATHS: 146.]

Doug went for a nature walk and hugged the biggest tree on the trail. "Hi friend?"

Doug has died. Again.

It was a tropical fungus, it moved somewhere cold, and it didn't wait for anyone to get sick first.

## Candida auris

Candida auris.

[Sterile white with a sickly yellow tint. A plain hospital building, seen only from outside.]

In two thousand nine, scientists in Japan described a new yeast taken from a patient's ear and named it after the Latin word for ear. At the time, it looked like a footnote.

[A world map. Dots light up on South Asia, South Africa and South America at the same moment.]

Then it showed up in South Asia, South Africa and South America at almost the same time. Genetic testing showed these weren't one strain spreading around the world, but separate groups that had appeared on their own, on three continents, all at once.

[A shelf of three medicine bottles. Crosses over one, then two, then all three.]

Most strains resist at least one main type of antifungal drug, and some resist all three. It survives on surfaces and objects for a long time, and it spreads easily between patients in healthcare facilities.

[Sober card, sterile white, no Doug: "U.S. CLINICAL CASES: 476 IN 2019, 1,471 IN 2021".]

In twenty nineteen, the Centers for Disease Control put it on its list of urgent threats, and in twenty twenty-two, the World Health Organization put it in its critical priority group. In the United States, clinical cases went from four hundred and seventy-six in twenty nineteen to one thousand four hundred and seventy-one in twenty twenty-one.

[A thermometer at 37 degrees. Around it, a wall. Fungi bump against the wall and fall back.]

To understand why this one matters, you need to know about the wall.

Our bodies sit at about thirty-seven degrees Celsius, and most fungi simply can't grow that warm. When researchers tested nearly five thousand fungal strains, every one-degree rise between thirty and forty degrees shut out about six percent more of them. That heat is one big reason mammals rarely get fungal infections.

[A hot tub with a thermometer reading 40. Label: "HOT TUB LIMIT".]

When it was first described, Candida auris grew well at forty degrees. That's hotter than our bodies, and about as hot as safety regulators say a hot tub should ever get.

And here's the most disturbing part. One hypothesis says it learned to handle that heat out in the world, adapting to warmer temperatures as the climate warmed, until our bodies were no longer too hot for it. It's only a hypothesis, and the researchers themselves say it will be hard to prove.

Our body heat has been a wall against fungi for millions of years. This might be the first one learning to climb it.

[Doug in his living room turns the thermostat to 37. He stands smug in the warm room. A small cartoon spore puff walks in and sits down beside him. Doug turns into a fuzzy white Doug silhouette, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 147.]

Doug turned his thermostat up to thirty-seven degrees, on the theory that nothing could stand it. Something sat down beside him.

Doug has died. Again.

[Slow push-in on the red cap resting on the windowsill of the warm room. One soft white fuzz spot appears on the brim.]

The cap has outlived one hundred and forty-seven Dougs, and as of today, it is also food.
