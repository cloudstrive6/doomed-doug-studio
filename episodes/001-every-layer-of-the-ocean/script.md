## Portuguese Man o' War

Portuguese Man o' War.

From a distance, it looks like a purple party balloon that somebody lost at the beach, and it's drifting straight toward Doug.

That balloon is a gas-filled float that can sit about fifteen centimeters above the waterline. It has no engine and no way to steer, so wind and currents push it wherever they like, sometimes in drifting fleets of more than a thousand.

Under the float hang the tentacles. On average they stretch about ten meters, and some reach thirty, which is longer than two city buses parked end to end, dangling under something the size of a sandwich bag. Every tentacle is packed with microscopic stinging capsules, and each capsule holds a coiled, barbed tube that fires venom into whatever brushes past.

Here's where it gets strange. The man o' war isn't one animal. It's a colony of genetically identical clones called zooids, and every type of zooid has exactly one job. Some keep it afloat, some catch food, some digest it, and some handle reproduction, but none of them could survive on its own. It looks like a jellyfish, but it's closer to a floating committee.

## Geography Cone Snail

Geography Cone Snail.

It's a snail that lives on shallow reefs in the tropical Indo-Pacific, it moves at snail speed, and its shell is covered in a brown and white pattern that collectors love.

The shell is seven to fifteen centimeters long, about the size of a television remote. Something that slow can't chase fish, so it uses chemistry instead. Its weapon is a hollow, modified tooth that works like a harpoon, loaded into a stretchy tube that the snail aims at a fish like a gun barrel. When it fires, the tooth goes in and the venom follows.

But that's not the disturbing part. Scientists discovered that this snail also releases insulin into the water, and it isn't human insulin but a fish version, matched to the snail's favorite prey. It's part of a toxic cloud that researchers nicknamed the nirvana cabal. Fish that swim through it have their blood sugar crash, and they go limp and dazed. Then the snail spreads a stretchy false mouth over them like a cape and pulls them inside.

Very few animals on Earth are known to use insulin as a weapon, and this is one of them.

## Bobbit Worm

Bobbit Worm.

The sand on a warm, shallow reef looks empty, apart from a few thin stalks poking out, and nothing about it seems worth worrying about.

Those stalks are antennae, and they belong to a worm buried in the sand, with the rest of its body underneath you. Bobbit worms can grow up to about three meters long, which is longer than a sofa, living under the floor. They spend their lives in a burrow with only the head showing, and they wait.

The antennae work like tripwires. When a fish brushes one, the worm snaps up out of the sand with a pair of scissor-like jaws and drags the fish down into the burrow. The strike is so fast and so powerful that prey is sometimes cut clean in two, and a second later the worm is gone, the fish is gone, and the sand looks empty again.

The unsettling part is that they don't always stay in the ocean, because they can hitch a ride inside live rock that ends up in aquariums. One public aquarium in Cornwall, England, kept finding fish injured or missing and coral sliced apart, and nobody could explain why. When staff finally took the whole display apart, they found a worm more than a meter long living inside it. They named it Barry.

## Humboldt Squid

Humboldt Squid.

Fishermen in Baja California call it diablo rojo, the red devil, so you'd expect a monster. You'd be about half right.

Humboldt squid can grow to about one and a half meters long and weigh up to fifty kilograms, which is heavier than the packed suitcase the airline makes you pay extra for. They spend the day deep in the twilight zone, and after dusk they rise toward the surface to feed, a nightly commute that scientists have tracked with electronic tags.

Their skin is loaded with color cells, and a whole squid can flash between red and white a few times every second, like a broken traffic light. Scientists think the flashing is a way of signaling to other squid.

Here's the problem with the red devil reputation. When a researcher was surrounded by hundreds of them and switched on his dive light, they didn't attack him, they bolted, and he said he was surprised at how timid they were. They aren't nearly as timid with each other. One researcher examined the stomachs of more than five hundred Humboldt squid and found signs of cannibalism in about a quarter of them. The bigger ones did it more often.

## Giant Squid

Giant Squid.

For centuries, the giant squid was known mostly from dead ones washing ashore and from sailors' stories, and nobody had ever filmed one alive down where it actually lives.

That changed in July twenty twelve, when a Japanese team led by zoologist Tsunemi Kubodera took a submersible down near the Ogasawara Islands, about a thousand kilometers south of Tokyo. They used lights that squid can't see, dangled a one-meter squid as bait, and made around a hundred dives. At about six hundred and thirty meters, a giant squid finally showed up, and they followed it down to roughly nine hundred meters. The animal was about three meters long.

What makes it unsettling is the eyes. A giant squid eye has been measured at about twenty-seven centimeters across, roughly the size of a basketball. The pupil alone is about nine centimeters wide, big enough to fit the entire eye of a swordfish inside it, and it's the largest eye in the animal kingdom.

Scientists think eyes that big aren't built for hunting but for spotting sperm whales, which stir up glowing plankton as they move through the dark. Sperm whales eat giant squid, and many whales carry round sucker scars from the fights.

## Barreleye Fish

Barreleye Fish.

Look at its face and you'll see two small dark spots above the mouth, staring back at you. Those aren't its eyes.

They're its nostrils, and the real eyes are inside its head. The barreleye lives about six hundred to eight hundred meters down in the North Pacific, and it's only about fifteen centimeters long. The top of its head is a transparent, fluid-filled shield, like a see-through phone case, and inside that shield sit two tube-shaped eyes with bright green lenses.

For decades after the species was first described in nineteen thirty-nine, scientists believed those eyes were fixed in place, pointing straight up and giving the fish permanent tunnel vision. Most specimens came up in nets, and the delicate shield didn't survive the trip, so nobody had seen the whole picture.

Here's the part that surprised everyone. When researchers at the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute finally watched living barreleyes with underwater robots, they found that the eyes rotate. The fish hangs motionless in the dark with its eyes pointed up, watching for the shadows of prey above it. When it spots something, it turns, and the eyes swing forward inside the shield to follow. Scientists think it may even pick off food caught in the tentacles of siphonophores, the same group of drifting colonies as the man o' war.

[Doug presses his face against the clear shield. Inside, the green eyes rotate to meet his.]

Doug leans in for a closer look, and the barreleye rotates its eyes to look back at him, through its own forehead.

## Vampire Squid

Vampire Squid.

Its scientific name, Vampyroteuthis infernalis, translates to the vampire squid from hell, so if anything in this video is going to finish Doug off with some style, it's this.

It lives about six hundred to nine hundred meters down, in a layer called the oxygen minimum zone, where there's so little oxygen that most predators can't stay for long. It grows to about thirty centimeters, roughly the size of a football, and it's dark reddish brown, with webbing between its arms like a cape. Despite the name, it's neither a vampire nor a squid, but the last surviving member of an ancient group of cephalopods.

But here's the catch. The vampire squid from hell doesn't hunt. It eats marine snow, a slow drizzle of dead plankton, mucus and other scraps drifting down from above, which it collects with two long, sticky filaments and packs into little balls before eating them.

When something threatens it, it doesn't bite. First it flips its webbed arms inside out to look like something else, and if that fails, it squirts out a cloud of glowing, sticky mucus and slips away while the predator is distracted.

## Anglerfish

Anglerfish.

Below one thousand meters, sunlight is gone for good, and the only light left is made by animals. Almost every one of them is a trap.

The female deep-sea anglerfish has a fishing rod growing out of her head, and at the tip is a glowing bulb called an esca. She doesn't make that glow herself, because it comes from bioluminescent bacteria living inside the bulb. She hangs still in the dark and lets the lure dangle in front of her mouth, glowing like a phone screen in a dark bedroom. Anything curious enough to swim up and check it out becomes dinner.

The most disturbing part is the males. In some deep-sea species, the male is only a fraction of the female's size and has no lure of his own. In an ocean this big and this dark, finding a female is so rare that when he does, he latches onto her body and never lets go. Over time, his tissue fuses with hers and their bloodstreams connect, his eyes stop working, and he gets all his nutrition from her. He becomes a permanent attachment whose only job is to supply sperm. Scientists are still studying how their immune systems allow this, when a body would normally reject a transplanted organ.

## Black Swallower

Black Swallower.

It's small and dark, and at about twenty-five centimeters long it's barely longer than a banana, which makes it the fish down here you'd probably ignore.

The black swallower lives deep in tropical and subtropical waters, and its whole body is built around one idea. Its jaws open wide, and its stomach stretches so far that the skin around it turns see-through. It can swallow prey more than twice its own length and ten times its own weight, which for you would be roughly like swallowing a canoe in one go.

What nobody expected was just how far it would push that. In two thousand seven, a dead black swallower about nineteen centimeters long was found floating off Grand Cayman. Inside it was a snake mackerel eighty-six centimeters long, more than four times the length of the fish that ate it.

The meal was simply too big to digest in time, so it began to break down inside the swallower, and the gas floated the whole fish up to the surface.

## Zombie Worms

Zombie Worms.

When a whale dies, its body can sink all the way to the sea floor, and down here, where food is scarce, that's a feast. Scientists call it a whale fall.

Some of the strangest guests are Osedax, the bone-eating zombie worms, first described in two thousand four from whale bones deep in Monterey Canyon, off California. They have no mouth, no stomach and no gut. Instead, the female grows root-like tissue down into the bone and releases acid to dissolve her way in, while bacteria living inside those roots help break down the fats and proteins locked in the bone. Picture a whale skeleton as long as a school bus, covered in tiny pink worms like a shag carpet.

The worst part is where the males live. You won't find them on the bone, because they're microscopic, and dozens of them live inside the female's tube. They never eat.

Zombie worms turn up far deeper than Monterey, too. The deepest ones on record were found on a whale skeleton four thousand two hundred and four meters down, off the coast of Brazil.

## Hadal Snailfish

Hadal Snailfish.

Below six thousand meters, the sea floor splits open into trenches, and this is the hadal zone, where most living things give up.

Fish, it turns out, have a depth limit. Pressure bends proteins out of shape, so deep-sea fish pack their cells with a protective molecule called trimethylamine oxide, and the deeper they live, the more of it they need. Past a certain point, all that extra molecule starts pulling water into their cells, and things stop working. Scientists calculated that fish should hit that wall somewhere around eight thousand two hundred to eight thousand four hundred meters.

The fish living right at the wall isn't a monster. In August twenty twenty-two, a baited camera in the Izu-Ogasawara Trench off Japan filmed a young snailfish at eight thousand three hundred and thirty-six meters, the deepest fish ever recorded. It's small, soft and pale, and it looks a bit like an oversized tadpole.

The disturbing part is how comfortable it looks. At that depth, the pressure is more than eight hundred times what it is at the surface, which is like parking a small car on your thumbnail. The snailfish copes by having no swim bladder at all, since a pocket of gas couldn't survive down here. Instead, it relies on a soft, flexible skeleton and a jelly-like body that keeps it floating without effort.

## Challenger Deep

Challenger Deep.

This is the deepest known point on Earth, at the southern end of the Mariana Trench in the western Pacific. The most precise measurement so far puts the bottom at about ten thousand nine hundred and thirty-five meters.

If you dropped Mount Everest in here, its peak would still be about two kilometers underwater, and the pressure at the bottom is more than a thousand times what you feel at the surface.

The first people to make it down were Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh, in January nineteen sixty, aboard a bathyscaphe called Trieste. The descent took nearly five hours, and a viewing window cracked on the way, so they spent only about twenty minutes on the bottom. Nobody went back in person for fifty-two years, until filmmaker James Cameron made a solo dive in twenty twelve. In twenty nineteen, explorer Victor Vescovo made four dives to the bottom in his submersible, the Limiting Factor.

The pollution doesn't stop at what you can see, either.
