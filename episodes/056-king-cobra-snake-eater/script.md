# Why King Cobras Hunt the Deadliest Snakes in Asia (draft 2)

[Opening frame: the thumbnail. A huge reared olive king cobra with pale chevron bands, the tail of a black-and-yellow banded krait hanging from its mouth. Beside it, a short queue of snakes. Doug last, in a snake costume, cap on top, holding a deli ticket.]

India's four deadliest snakes include a krait and a cobra. King cobras eat both kinds.

By the end, Doug is dressed as a king cobra, facing the thing that eats king cobras.

## The Krait

The Krait. Kraits are known for biting people while they sleep, so quietly that the sleeper may not even notice.

[NOW SERVING: 1. Night, a dark hut floor, a krait silhouette with a red glow. No people shown.]

In two thousand and five, snakebites killed an estimated forty-five thousand nine hundred people in India, and the common krait has long ranked among the four snakes most to blame.

In one Sri Lankan hospital study of common krait bites, nine in ten happened at night.

Kraits also eat other snakes, including each other. In western India, one common krait was recorded swallowing a smaller krait in about ten minutes.

So this is a night-time killer that eats its own relatives, and surely nothing out there would hunt that.

Here's the twist: the king cobra does.

[Reveal: a different krait, a black-and-yellow banded krait, matching the thumbnail, not the night-hut krait. The king cobra rears over it. Red arrow, label "SNAKE EATER".]

Not that exact krait, but its cousin: researchers have reported king cobras eating banded kraits in the wild, and the king cobra's genus name, Ophiophagus, literally means snake eater. For the king cobra, kraits are simply a menu item.

It's like learning that the burglar everyone locks their doors against is, in fact, somebody's takeaway.

[Doug in a banded krait costume: zipped black-and-yellow tube suit, face showing, cap on top, holding ticket 9.]

The king cobra hunts snakes, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as one.

For about four seconds, Doug is the most feared thing in the room.

[A large olive shape fills the doorway behind him. Cut away.]

Doug has died. Again.

And the krait was a treat, because most of the time, the king cobra eats something that can't poison it at all.

## The Scent Trail

Harmless.

The Scent Trail. In southern India, researchers watched a radio-tagged king cobra follow the scent of its prey twenty meters up into the trees.

[NOW SERVING: 2. A rice paddy, then a forest edge. Doug in a brown rat snake costume, leaving a dotted trail behind him.]

It hunts with its tongue: it flicks the air, picks up the smell another snake left behind, and follows it wherever it goes. That same cobra tracked one trail back and forth across a fast stream three times, and it kept following trails through heavy rain. Once, the smell was so strong that it bit a stone. Twice.

In India's Western Ghats, its usual target is the Indian rat snake. It's harmless to people, it averages about one point seven five meters, and it turns up around farms and rice fields, because farms have rats.

And the cobra covers serious ground to find one. That tagged male moved more than thirty-five kilometers in under six months, and in Thailand, tracked king cobras used areas averaging about five square kilometers each.

It's a bloodhound that only ever follows the scent of other dogs.

The strange part is the rat snake's defence. When it's cornered, it puffs up and growls, and researchers think that growl may be an imitation of the king cobra itself.

[Doug climbs a tree. The dotted line goes up the trunk after him. Below, a real rat snake slides past.]

Doug hides up a tree, the dotted line follows him up the trunk, and then the cobra finds a real rat snake first.

[Doug, from the branch, waves. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug is fine. He is up a tree, waving goodbye to someone he just met.

## The Swallow

The Swallow. A king cobra can swallow a rat snake more than half its own length, without hands, without legs and without chewing.

[NOW SERVING: 3. Side-by-side length bars: king cobra and rat snake. Labels "3 TO 4 m" and "1.75 m".]

In the Western Ghats, adult males usually measure three to four meters, and king cobras can reach about five and a half, the longest venomous snakes in the world. Even so, swallowing something that long is a project.

Here's how it works: a snake's jaw is built from two independent bones that are only loosely attached to the skull. So the cobra can move one side forward, then the other, and slowly walk its jaws along the prey toward the head. Small prey, it doesn't bother with any of that: it folds the snake in half and swallows it in a few minutes.

It's like eating a garden hose longer than your own arm span, one bite at a time, with your hands behind your back.

But that's not the uncomfortable part: rat snakes bite back, often right on the cobra's head, and the cobra doesn't seem to care. Researchers note it isn't fussy about which end it grabs first.

[Doug, still in the rat snake costume, lies straight on the ground like a hose. The cobra's jaws start at his feet.]

Doug, still in the rat snake costume, volunteers to be the hose.

[The costume goes in. The cap stays outside, on the grass.]

Doug has died. Again. The cap stayed outside.

And rat snakes are the easy ones, because the next few things on the menu are venomous.

## The Cobra

Venomous.

The Cobra. A king cobra will eat a real cobra. A BBC natural history crew filmed one in an Indian forest swallowing an Indian cobra, one of those four deadliest snakes from the start.

[NOW SERVING: 4. The king cobra beside a smaller, wide-hooded cobra. Label "NAJA" over the small one.]

The awkward part is that the king cobra isn't technically a cobra at all. True cobras belong to a group called Naja, and king cobras have a genus all to themselves, Ophiophagus. They are related, though, because both belong to the elapids, the same venomous family as kraits and mambas.

It's a shark that hunts other sharks and, on paper, isn't even a shark.

The worst part is the noise: most snakes hiss when they're upset, but a king cobra makes a sound so low that the Smithsonian's National Zoo compares it to a dog's growl.

[Doug in a cobra costume with a cardboard hood taped on. He puffs up.]

Doug has met a black mamba. This one eats things like it.

So today Doug is dressed as a cobra, with a cardboard hood taped on, and he plans to win this with his own hiss.

Doug did not agree to this; he agreed to the costume, not the hiss-off.

[Doug: "ssss." The king cobra: a huge, low growl. Red sound-wave lines. Doug's hood droops.]

Doug hisses, and the king cobra growls back.

Doug has died. Again.

And cobras are only the venomous snakes that stay on the ground. The next ones live up in the trees, and they bite back.

## The Pit Vipers

The Pit Vipers. One wild king cobra in southern India ate twenty-six venomous pit vipers in about four and a half months, and researchers watched almost every meal.

[NOW SERVING: 5. A rainforest canopy. Small green pit vipers on branches. Tally marks on a tree trunk.]

He was a male a little over three meters long, and he spent about half of his active hours hunting, with a team following him every day from morning until nightfall. Most of the vipers he caught were up in trees, shrubs or vines, and he usually grabbed them right behind the head.

Here's the catch: pit vipers bite back, and in nine of those hunts, the cobra appeared to get bitten.

Each time, he let go and waited, about ten minutes on average, for his own venom to finish the job, and then he went back and ate.

Once, a viper bit him on the side of the head, and he froze upright for half an hour with his mouth partly open, ignoring everything around him. Then he snapped out of it, went looking for the viper, and ate it anyway.

It's like ordering a dish that stabs you, sitting quietly for ten minutes, and then asking for the rest of your plate.

[Doug, out of costume, holding a clipboard, counting vipers. The cobra slides straight past his feet.]

Doug is out of costume today, counting vipers on a clipboard. The cobra slides right past his feet, because Doug isn't a snake.

Doug is fine. He has never been ignored so professionally.

On three separate days, that cobra ate two vipers in a single day, and the menu is about to get bigger.

## The Monitor Lizard

Bigger.

The Monitor Lizard. For an animal whose name means snake eater, the king cobra has a real lizard habit.

[NOW SERVING: 6. Night forest. A clouded monitor lizard, legs and claws, on a log.]

In Thailand, researchers tracking king cobras by radio caught them eating clouded monitor lizards, and they published it under a title calling the king cobra a supposed snake specialist. In Singapore, one was seen swallowing a clouded monitor at night, which is odd, because king cobras mostly hunt by day. And in two thousand and twenty-two, on the Andaman Islands, one was photographed eating a bullfrog, the first time anyone had recorded a king cobra eating a frog.

Researchers in India had already noted that monitor lizards were the only prey besides snakes that had been reliably reported from the wild. So the diet was never snakes only: it's mostly snakes, plus the occasional thing with legs.

It's a fish restaurant that every now and then, without warning, serves you a steak.

The uncomfortable part for Doug is that he was relying on the rule. Taking the costume off was supposed to make him safe, because it's snakes only, remember.

[Doug, out of costume, drops to all fours to look less like a snake. Side by side with the monitor, the silhouettes match.]

So Doug drops to all fours, to look as little like a snake as he possibly can, and now he looks exactly like a lizard.

Doug has died. Again. It was a very convincing lizard.

Lizards have claws, but the next thing on the menu has a hug.

## The Python

The Python. A king cobra about three point seven meters long once killed and swallowed a reticulated python two point eight meters long.

[NOW SERVING: 7. The two snakes drawn to scale. Label "3.7 m" and "2.8 m".]

That's a single meal roughly three quarters of its own length, and in India, unusually large males over four meters are known to hunt Indian pythons. In Singapore, king cobras have been recorded attacking and eating reticulated pythons, including one at a wetland reserve, written up in two thousand and twenty-one.

It's like trying to eat a sofa that is also hugging you.

Here's where it goes wrong: a python doesn't need venom, because it squeezes.

In two thousand and eighteen, a photo spread online of a king cobra and a reticulated python found dead together, probably somewhere in Southeast Asia. The python had been bitten behind the head, and it was still coiled around the cobra.

A Florida Museum herpetologist estimated that both could have been dead within about thirty minutes. Another expert's verdict was simple: if anyone was hunting, it was the cobra, and it didn't work out for either snake.

[Doug in a fat python costume. He wraps his arms around the cobra in a hug.]

Doug, in a python costume, tries the hug from his side.

[A cartoon knot of two snakes and one tube suit. The cap sits on top of the knot.]

Doug has died. Again. The narrator is not going to say who won.

So far, everything it has eaten has been somebody else's family.

## The Nest

Family.

The Nest. The king cobra is the only snake known to build a nest for its eggs.

[NOW SERVING: 8. Forest floor under a big tree. A mound of leaves rising.]

The female sweeps dead leaves together with her body and carries them in tight coils, for days on end. Then she crawls over the pile again and again to pack it down, and burrows inside to hollow out a chamber.

A finished nest can be about fifty-five centimeters high and almost one and a half meters wide. Inside go between fifteen and fifty eggs, for seventy-five to a hundred days, and she often stays on top of the nest the whole time.

It's like a parent building the nursery by hand, then sleeping on the roof of it for three months.

Here's the dark part: keepers at the Bronx Zoo describe her instincts pushing her away from the eggs as they hatch. Their explanation is that it stops her mistaking her own babies for food.

[Doug with a garden rake, "helping" with the leaves. The mother rears up over the mound. Doug freezes.]

Doug offers to help with the leaves, the mother rears up over the mound, and Doug freezes.

She's guarding, not hunting, and today, for once, Doug is not dressed as a snake.

[She lowers back onto the nest. Doug backs away slowly, still holding the rake.]

Doug is fine. We had honestly planned for this one to go differently.

Her babies are venomous from the moment they hatch, and they hatch into a forest that contains other king cobras.

## The Other King

The Other King. King cobras eat king cobras.

[NOW SERVING: 9. Doug's ticket says 9. He hasn't noticed.]

Researchers in India have watched it happen in captivity and in the wild. In Singapore, in June two thousand and twenty-four, a king cobra was filmed swallowing another king cobra while it was still alive.

That December, in a Singapore nature park, people watched two adults fight for more than five hours, biting each other's necks and rolling over and over. The smaller one broke free and fled into a pond, but the bigger one caught it, dragged it back out by the neck, and tried to swallow it.

Then the park closed at seven, the observers had to go home, and nobody knows how it ended.

It's like realising the person behind you in the queue at the deli has ordered you.

And here's the kicker: in two thousand and twenty-four, scientists split the king cobra into four species: northern, Sunda, Western Ghats and Luzon. The two in that pond were Sunda king cobras, and the pit-viper hunter from earlier was a Western Ghats king cobra. Officially, there are now four kings.

The king cobra is also listed as Vulnerable, because people clear its forests, catch it and kill it on sight. And some of the ones that are left are busy eating each other.

[Doug in a king cobra costume, olive with pale bands, narrow hood, cap on the hood. He holds up ticket 9. Behind him, a much bigger king cobra rears.]

Doug is dressed as a king cobra, and his ticket says nine.

[NOW SERVING flips to 9. Ding.]

His number has been called.

Doug has died. Again.

The cap is on the empty nest now, the only thing in this forest that nobody has tried to swallow.
