## Lucy's Fall

Lucy's Fall.

[The Lucy skeleton drawn flat on a white card, small. A red arrow points to the right upper arm. Label: "BROKEN".]

In twenty sixteen, a team that scanned Lucy's skeleton found broken bones they believe happened at the moment she died. She lived about three point two million years ago, in what is now Ethiopia, and she stood about one point one meters tall.

[Date card: "ABOUT 3.2 MILLION YEARS AGO". The time machine door opens onto the plain.]

John Kappelman, who led the team, says the break at the top of her right upper arm forms when a hand hits the ground hard and the shoulder bones are driven into each other.

Here's the problem.

[Two arm bones side by side, both with red cracks at the top. A red "!".]

Both of her upper arms were broken the same way. On the team's reading, she landed on her feet, pitched forward, and threw both arms out to catch herself. In her last second, she did exactly what you would do.

[A telephone pole next to the tree, the same height. Label: "12 M".]

Lucy walked upright, but she was small, and Kappelman thinks she probably looked for food in trees and climbed up to sleep in them. Using falls in chimpanzees, the team estimated a drop of more than twelve meters, about the height of a telephone pole, and a landing at over fifty-six kilometers an hour.

[A red "?" stamps next to the arms.]

Not everyone agrees. Donald Johanson, who discovered her in nineteen seventy-four, says the cracks come from the weight of rock pressing on the bones as they fossilized, which happens to lots of fossils there.

## The Sinkhole

The Sinkhole.

[A dark hole in a rocky hillside, grass around the rim. A red curved arrow points down into it.]

Two members of a species nobody had ever seen fell into the same hole in the ground almost two million years ago, and they were still lying together at the bottom. They were found in two thousand eight, when a nine-year-old named Matthew Berger noticed a collarbone sticking out of a rock outside Johannesburg.

[The time machine lands on the grassy hillside, Doug steps out. Date card: "ABOUT 2 MILLION YEARS AGO". Two plain bones on a white card, labelled "YOUNG" and "ADULT".]

His father, Lee Berger, named the species Australopithecus sediba, and the two skeletons belonged to a youngster of about eleven to thirteen and an adult woman.

[A cross-section of the hill. A thin shaft drops from the surface to a dark pool far below.]

The geologist Paul Dirks suggested that animals smelled water somewhere down in the cave and fell in while they were trying to reach it. The drop has been estimated at thirty to fifty meters, which is at least a ten-storey building, with nothing to hold on to.

[A ten-storey building outline beside the shaft, same height. Label: "30 TO 50 M".]

The fall itself was probably enough, and afterwards a flow of mud and water carried the bodies deeper into the cave, still together.

The strange part is how well that worked out for science.

[The pool at the bottom: two bone silhouettes beside a sabre-toothed cat skeleton, antelope, a hare, a mouse. A red "!" over the pile.]

No scavengers ever reached them down there, so the skeletons stayed together for two million years. Researchers think the cave worked as a natural trap, and the two of them shared the bottom with a sabre-toothed cat, antelope, hares and mice.

Doug did not agree to this.

At least that hole stayed still, which is more than you can say for the next one.

## The Cave Ceiling

The Cave Ceiling.

[A wide cave mouth in a mountainside. Inside, loose rocks hang from the roof. A yellow warning triangle.]

A Neanderthal man survived a smashed eye socket and a withered arm, and then, according to the archaeologist who found him, his own cave fell on him.

[The time machine lands at the cave mouth. Date card: "MORE THAN 45,000 YEARS AGO". Map of Iraqi Kurdistan with Shanidar Cave highlighted.]

He is called Shanidar One, after Shanidar Cave in the mountains of Iraqi Kurdistan, where Ralph Solecki dug in the nineteen fifties. Across four digging seasons ending in nineteen sixty, his team dug up nine Neanderthals there. Solecki's main trench went down fourteen meters, which is deeper than a four-storey building is tall.

[A four-storey building lowered into the trench, its roof still below the rim. Label: "14 M".]

The man's skull shows an old blow to the left side of his face that probably left him blind in that eye. His right arm was withered, and the lower part of it may have been lost, and bony growths in both ears were taking his hearing.

But that's not the disturbing part.

[Bone diagram with three red arrows: face, arm, leg. Each arrow gets a small green tick: "HEALED".]

All of it had healed, and he lived to about forty. The anthropologist Erik Trinkaus argued that he must have had help from others to survive his injuries for that long. Solecki concluded that after all of that, he was killed on the spot by rocks falling from the cave roof. Solecki thought falling rocks killed at least two other Neanderthals buried in the same cave, too.

[A red "?" on the roof.]

Later researchers have said Solecki's dig was too small to be certain about any of them.

[CASE FILE: "SHANIDAR 1. CAUSE: ROCKFALL?"]

[Doug walks in wearing a yellow hard hat over the cap, thumbs up. A rock wobbles above him.]

Doug has brought a hard hat, and he has read the label carefully, twice. Hard hats are rated for something a lot smaller than a cave.

[Cut to black. The hard hat sits on the rubble, the red cap neatly on top of it. DOUG DEATHS: 256.]

So far, nature didn't mean it, but the next ones did.

Hunted.

## The Eagle

The Eagle.

[A bird of prey on a high branch, silhouette with a red glow, then revealed: a crowned eagle, crest up, huge talons.]

The holes at the bottom of the Taung skull's eye sockets match the marks crowned eagles leave on monkeys today.

[The time machine lands in a forest clearing. Date card: "ABOUT 2.8 MILLION YEARS AGO". A small skull on a white card. Two red arrows point into the eye sockets.]

Quarry workers in South Africa found it in nineteen twenty-four, and the anatomist Raymond Dart recognised it as one of the first early human fossils ever found in Africa. Even so, it took more than twenty years before scientists accepted Africa as a major source of human evolution. The skull belonged to a youngster about three years old.

[A nest high in a tree. Below it, a heap of small bones. Label: "NEST".]

In two thousand six, an Ohio State team described monkey bones from under crowned eagle nests in Ivory Coast, with punctures and ragged cuts in the eye sockets. Of about twelve hundred bones from sixteen nests, more than half came from primates. Lee Berger read the study, checked the Taung skull, and found the same kind of hole at the base of each eye socket.

What nobody expected was the weight.

[A house cat on one side of a seesaw, a small backpack on the other side, four times bigger. The cat side stays down.]

A big female crowned eagle weighs up to about four point seven kilograms, roughly a large house cat, yet it can kill prey of up to twenty kilograms. The Taung youngster probably weighed around twelve, and Berger called the case proven beyond reasonable doubt, though not every researcher was convinced.

[CASE FILE: "TAUNG 1. CAUSE: EAGLE".]

[Doug standing in a clearing, hopeful smile. The eagle lands on a branch above him, tilts its head, and swoops. It flies off with the red cap. Capless Doug looks up.]

Doug weighs eighty kilograms, four eagle limits. The eagle takes the cap instead. Doug is fine. The cap is not.

[Two beats later, the cap drops back out of the sky onto Doug's head. DOUG DEATHS: still 256.]

The eagle wanted something smaller, but the next hunter wasn't picky, and it could climb.

## The Leopard

The Leopard.

[A skull cap on a white card, two neat round holes in the back. Above it, a leopard's lower jaw hovers. It drops, and its two fangs slot into the holes. Label: "PERFECT FIT".]

Two round holes in the back of a young relative's skull line up with the lower fangs of a leopard jaw found in the same cave.

[The time machine lands beside the tree at the cave mouth. Date card: "ABOUT 1.8 MILLION YEARS AGO". Map of South Africa, Swartkrans highlighted.]

The skull is called SK fifty-four, from Swartkrans Cave in South Africa, and it is around one point eight million years old. For decades it was filed as Paranthropus robustus, although a twenty twenty-four study argues that it actually belonged to early Homo.

[A dotted line between the two holes. Label: "33 MM". Beside it, two fingers held together, the same width.]

The holes are thirty-three millimeters apart, about the width of two fingers held together. The first guess was a weapon. Then the scientist Bob Brain held a leopard's lower jaw from the same deposits, catalogued SK three forty-nine, against the skull, and the fangs fit. He published the match in nineteen seventy.

[A tree growing beside the cave mouth. A leopard on a branch, a bundle in its paws, drawn as a dark shape. Small bones drop and roll into the cave.]

In Brain's picture, the leopard carried its kill up a tree outside the cave to eat in peace, and the leftover bones washed into the cave below.

Here's the twist.

[A book cover: "THE HUNTERS OR THE HUNTED?" with a big red "?".]

In nineteen eighty-one, Brain published a book called The Hunters or the Hunted, and it concluded that the human relatives in those caves had been brought in by carnivores. They were the food, not the hunters.

[CASE FILE: "SK 54. CAUSE: LEOPARD".]

[Doug in an adult-sized costume of a small upright primate, red cap on top, standing very still. The leopard looks at him, then up at its tree.]

The leopards here hunted animals the size of our relatives, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as one. He is playing it completely straight, and standing very still.

[Cut to the tree. Only the red cap hangs from a branch. DOUG DEATHS: 257.]

The leopard needed a tree, but the next one only needed a foot in the water.

## The Crocodile

The Crocodile.

[A lake edge at dawn. Two bumps on the water. A silhouette with a red glow slides closer, then the reveal: a big crocodile with small horns over its eyes.]

The crocodile that lived at Olduvai Gorge is called Crocodylus anthropophagus, and the second word means man-eating.

[The time machine lands on the lake shore, a little too close to the water. Date card: "ABOUT 1.8 MILLION YEARS AGO". Map of Tanzania, Olduvai Gorge highlighted.]

It was described in twenty ten from fossils about one point eight four million years old. Christopher Brochu, who named it, thinks this horned crocodile was probably as large as a modern Nile crocodile, up to about six meters long.

[The crocodile next to a family car. The crocodile is longer. Label: "ABOUT 6 M".]

That is longer than a family car. The name was chosen for a reason, because tooth-marked foot and leg bones of early humans from the gorge were blamed on crocodiles.

The darkest part is where the marks are.

[A drawn foot skeleton on a white card. Red arrows point to the ankle and foot bones. Label: "TOOTH MARKS".]

In twenty twelve, Jackson Njau and Robert Blumenschine described crocodile bite marks on the foot and ankle bones of a Homo habilis from Olduvai, the bones you put in the water first. Njau had fed crocodiles in experiments, to learn exactly what their teeth do to bone.

He knew the bite.

[A leg bone appears beside the foot on the card. A red "?" stamps over the leg only.]

A leg bone nearby had bites too, some more like a big cat's. A later study agreed for the foot, but not for the leg. Even the twenty twelve paper says it can't tell whether crocodiles or big cats were doing the hunting. Brochu is careful too, and says he can't guarantee the crocodiles were killing our relatives, only that they were certainly biting them.

[CASE FILE: "OH 8. CAUSE: CROCODILE?"]

[Doug at the lake edge, one foot dipped in, holding a cup. The bumps on the water are gone.]

Doug only wanted a drink, so he only put in one foot, which felt like a sensible compromise. The last crocodile just followed him, but this one doesn't bother.

[Cut to black. The red cap floats on the still lake. DOUG DEATHS: 258.]

Teeth are quick, but the next one took its time.

Slowly.

## The Bad Liver

The Bad Liver.

[A thigh bone on a white card. A thick, lumpy outer layer is shaded in. Red arrow. Label: "EXTRA BONE".]

A Homo erectus woman from Kenya has a layer of abnormal bone coating her skeleton, and the first explanation was that she ate the wrong liver.

[The time machine lands on dry, open ground. Date card: "ABOUT 1.7 MILLION YEARS AGO". Map of Kenya, Koobi Fora highlighted.]

Her skeleton is catalogued eighteen-oh-eight, and the fossil hunter Kamoya Kimeu found it at Koobi Fora in Kenya in the early nineteen seventies. It is around one point seven million years old.

[A cross-section of the thigh bone. A rough grey ring sits around the normal bone, like a lumpy coat of paint on a pipe.]

Coarse, irregular bone had built up along the shafts of her long bones, like a lumpy second coat of paint on a pipe. In nineteen eighty-two, Alan Walker, Michael Zimmerman and Richard Leakey said the changes fit chronic vitamin A poisoning. They blamed a diet of animal liver, most probably from carnivores, whose livers are rich in vitamin A. They reached that answer after consulting doctors and the accounts of wilderness explorers.

The worst part is the word chronic.

[A calendar. Its pages flip, one after another.]

This was not one bad dinner. Chronic means it went on for a long time, and her bones kept changing while it did. Not everyone accepts the liver, either, and a study in nineteen ninety-one suggested she could have got the same overdose by eating bee larvae for a long time. So nobody knows for sure.

[CASE FILE: "KNM-ER 1808. CAUSE: LIVER?"]

[Doug at a small table, napkin tucked in, cap on. On the plate, a cartoon liver with a little flag: "LEOPARD".]

Doug has been offered the liver of the animal that ate him two stops ago. He eats all of it, slowly, out of spite. Doug is fine.

[The calendar flips. Doug, slightly greyer, still at the table. It flips again. Cut to black. The cap sits on the empty plate. DOUG DEATHS: 259.]

She may have died from eating a carnivore. In the last cave, the carnivores did the eating.

Stored.

## The Giant Hyena

The Giant Hyena.

[A dark cave in a hillside. Outside, a heavy, low hyena with a huge head, silhouette with a red glow, then the reveal.]

Of the Homo erectus fossils studied from one cave near Beijing, sixty-seven percent show bite marks or other damage from large carnivores.

[The time machine lands on the hillside below the cave, very quietly. Date card: "ABOUT 780,000 TO 230,000 YEARS AGO". Map of China, Zhoukoudian highlighted.]

The cave is Locality One at Zhoukoudian, home of the fossils known as Peking Man, and the remains found there may represent as many as forty individuals. In two thousand four, a team led by Noel Boaz pinned most of that damage on a single animal.

[The hyena next to a lion. Same bulk, but the hyena is shorter. Beside it, a kitchen counter at the same height as its shoulder.]

The giant hyena, Pachycrocuta brevirostris, was as heavy as a lion but only about as tall as a spotted hyena. Picture a lion's weight packed into something the height of a kitchen counter, with a skull built for cracking bone.

That's where it gets worse.

[The cave cross-section. Inside, a pile of bones, a sleeping adult hyena and a cub. Red arrows show bones being carried in.]

For decades, people were blamed, but Boaz's team argued that the bones look like those in modern hyena dens, and that the cave was a giant hyena den. On that reading, our relatives didn't live there. They were carried there, and kept.

[CASE FILE: "PEKING MAN. CAUSE: HYENA".]

[Doug at the cave mouth, crouching, hopeful smile. A hyena cub looks up at him. Speech bubble: "hi friend?" A large shadow falls across them both from behind.]

Doug finds the den and crouches down to say hello to a hyena cub. The cub is very interested. So is its mother, who has just come home.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 260.]

Doug has died. Again.

[The CASE FILE gets its last handwritten line: "DOUG, 2026. CAUSE: HE WENT."]

Doug, twenty twenty-six. Cause: he went.

[A plaster cast of a skull on a shelf. A dotted outline beside it where the original should be. A red "?".]

Plaster casts of the Peking Man fossils still exist. In December nineteen forty-one, the original bones were packed up to be shipped out of China, and nobody has seen them since.

[The red cap sitting alone at the den mouth, untouched. Then the cap on a coat hook at home.]

Doug deaths: two hundred and sixty. The cap went missing in that cave. Unlike the fossils, it came home. Which death was worst? Tell us below.
