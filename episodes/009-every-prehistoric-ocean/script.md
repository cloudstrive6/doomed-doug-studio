## Anomalocaris

Anomalocaris.

## Endoceras

Endoceras.

About four hundred and sixty million years ago, one of the most dangerous shapes in the ocean was a very long, very straight cone.

[A long, straight, cone-shaped shell with tentacles at the open end. Label: "ORDOVICIAN".]

Endoceras was a nautiloid, an ancient cousin of today's nautilus, and it lived in a long, straight shell divided into chambers, with its tentacles reaching out of the open end.

In the Ordovician, shelled hunters like this were among the top predators in the sea, with a sturdy shell, a powerful beak and plenty of arms.

The biggest Endoceras fossil is a piece of shell three meters long, kept in a museum at Harvard University, and it's missing its tip. When scientists filled in the missing part, they got a full length of about five point seven meters.

That's longer than a big pickup truck.

But here's the problem. Nearly half of that length is an estimate.

## Jaekelopterus

Jaekelopterus.

Sea scorpions sound like something made up for a monster film, but this one is real, and it's the largest arthropod anyone has ever found.

[A giant sea scorpion in murky green water, two long claws held forward. Label: "ABOUT 400 MILLION YEARS AGO".]

Arthropods are the huge group that includes insects, spiders, crabs and lobsters. About four hundred million years ago, in what is now Germany, Jaekelopterus hunted in a shallow lagoon or river delta, where the water was probably not fully salty.

In two thousand and seven, scientists described a single claw found in a German quarry. Part of it was broken off, but complete, it would have been about forty-six centimeters long.

That's about the length of a computer keyboard, and it's only the claw.

[A keyboard next to the claw, same length. Label: "46 cm".]

Here's the unsettling part. The claw is all we have.

## Dunkleosteus

Dunkleosteus.

Most fish have teeth, but Dunkleosteus didn't need any, because the edges of its jaws were sharp blades of bone.

[A heavy armored fish with a bony head shield and blade-edged jaws. Label: "ABOUT 360 MILLION YEARS AGO".]

It lived about three hundred and sixty million years ago, in a shallow sea that covered what is now Ohio, and its head and chest were wrapped in thick plates of armor.

In two thousand and six, two scientists built a computer model of its jaw and estimated a bite force of more than four thousand four hundred newtons at the tip. That's roughly the weight of a grand piano pressing down on a single point.

It was also fast. The model suggested it could open its mouth in about one fiftieth of a second, quick enough to suck prey straight in. Most fish have either a strong bite or a fast bite, and Dunkleosteus had both.

But that's not the worst news. In twenty twenty-three, scientists shrank it.

[An old long, sleek reconstruction gets crossed out. A shorter, fatter one replaces it. Label: "LESS LONG. MORE FISH."]

Older books said it could reach about nine meters long. A new study, based on the proportions of its head, cut a typical adult down to about three point four meters, a bit shorter than a small car. The same study made it far chunkier, though, with a body shaped like a tuna's and a huge mouth.

## Helicoprion

Helicoprion.

In eighteen ninety-nine, a geologist in Russia described a fossil shaped like a spiral of teeth, and for the next century, nobody could say where it went.

[A coiled fossil, like a cinnamon roll made of teeth. Label: "ABOUT 280 MILLION YEARS AGO".]

The spiral is called a tooth whorl. New teeth grew at one end while the older ones rolled toward the middle, so the whole thing kept coiling up for the animal's entire life.

One whorl found at a mine in Idaho is about twenty centimeters across, a little smaller than a basketball. The biggest whorls point to an animal around six to seven and a half meters long.

The rest of the body was made of cartilage, which rarely fossilizes, so scientists had a spiral of teeth and no idea where to put it.

Here comes the strange part.

[Three old reconstructions side by side: whorl on the nose, whorl on the tail, whorl on the back fin.]

The first reconstruction put the spiral on the end of its nose, curled back over the head. Later guesses put it on the tail, on the back fin, hanging under the chin, or deep down in the throat.

The answer finally came in twenty thirteen, when researchers ran X-ray scans on a fossil in an Idaho museum that had first been described in nineteen sixty-six. The whorl filled the middle of the lower jaw like a circular saw, and there were no teeth at all in the upper jaw.

The scans also showed it wasn't a true shark. Its closest living relatives are ratfish, which live in the deep sea today.

[Doug holds up the three old reconstructions like flash cards. He points at the tail one. Speech bubble: "this one?"]

Doug holds up the three old drawings, studies them carefully, and tries to guess which one is right. He picks the tail.

## Cymbospondylus

Cymbospondylus.

After the worst mass extinction in Earth's history, the ocean was close to empty, but it did not stay that way for long.

[An open teal sea. One tiny Doug for scale. Label: "ABOUT 246 MILLION YEARS AGO".]

Doug was there for the emptying.

Cymbospondylus was an ichthyosaur, a sea reptile shaped a little like a dolphin. In twenty eleven, a team in the mountains of Nevada dug up its skull, part of its backbone and a front flipper. Freeing the bones from the rock took the better part of three years.

The skull alone is about two meters long, and the whole animal is estimated at more than seventeen meters.

That's about a city bus and a half.

[A city bus, then half a bus behind it, then the ichthyosaur drawn over both.]

It hunted in a sea full of squid-like ammonites and small eel-like animals called conodonts. Researchers think that food supply, filling the empty space the extinction left behind, is what let ichthyosaurs grow so big.

The incredible part is how fast it happened.

Cymbospondylus lived only about three million years after the first ichthyosaurs appeared, while whales took far longer to reach their biggest sizes. As far as scientists know, it was the first truly giant animal ever to live on Earth.

## Pliosaurus

Pliosaurus.

If you grew up on nature documentaries, you might remember a sea reptile twenty-five meters long, and that one was wrong.

[A TV set showing a huge cartoon sea reptile. Label: "25 m (1999)". A red stamp: "NOPE".]

In nineteen ninety-nine, a famous television series showed its relative Liopleurodon as a twenty-five-meter monster. The scientist who advised on it later said the number came from scraps of fossil, and a study published this July puts Liopleurodon at just over eight meters, tops.

So the TV version shrank by about two thirds.

Then there's Pliosaurus. About a hundred and fifty-five million years ago, off what is now the coast of Dorset, England, one of them left behind a skull more than two meters long.

That's about as tall as a front door, and it's only the head.

[A front door lying on its side next to the skull, the same length.]

A computer model of that skull found that its strongest bites came from the back of the jaws, between six thousand and eleven thousand pounds of force. It probably grabbed prey, worked it to the back of its mouth, and crushed it there.

However, the model found one weakness. Twisting or shaking big prey could have damaged its own skull, so it probably didn't shake its food at all. It just bit harder.

In twenty twenty-three, another nearly complete pliosaur skull came out of a Dorset cliff, about two meters long and holding a hundred and thirty teeth.

## Xiphactinus

Xiphactinus.

About eighty-five million years ago, Kansas was underwater, and one of the scariest things in it was a fish with an underbite full of fangs.

[A big bony fish with an upturned jaw and long front fangs. Label: "ABOUT 85 MILLION YEARS AGO". A small map: a sea splitting North America in two.]

A shallow sea ran through the middle of North America back then, and Xiphactinus hunted in it. It could reach about five meters long, and it could swallow a two-meter fish whole.

We know that because of one famous fossil.

In nineteen fifty-two, a fossil hunter named George Sternberg dug up a Xiphactinus about four meters long. Inside its ribcage was another fish, called Gillicus, nearly two meters long, which is about as long as a grown man is tall.

[The fish-within-a-fish fossil, drawn on a museum wall. A small label: "STERNBERG MUSEUM, HAYS, KANSAS".]

The fossil was so big that it didn't fit on his truck and had to be sawed in half to move it. Today it hangs in the Sternberg Museum in Hays, Kansas, and it's the most photographed fossil in their collection.

The disturbing part is the timing. The bones of the Gillicus were never digested.

That means the big fish died soon after its meal. The usual reading is that the meal itself was the problem, and that Xiphactinus took on more than it could survive.

## Tylosaurus

Tylosaurus.

Mosasaurs were giant sea lizards that lived alongside the dinosaurs, and Tylosaurus was one of the biggest of them all.

[A long, scaly marine reptile with a pointed snout and a paddle tail. Label: "ABOUT 84 MILLION YEARS AGO".]

It patrolled the same Kansas sea as Xiphactinus. The largest individuals are estimated at around thirteen or fourteen meters long, which is longer than a city bus.

We know what it ate. In nineteen eighteen, a fossil collector named Charles Sternberg found a Tylosaurus with the bones of a young plesiosaur, a long-necked sea reptile, inside its ribcage. Other Tylosaurus fossils show it also ate fish, seabirds, sharks and smaller mosasaurs. It was not a picky eater, and it was about to meet a very easy meal.

[A Tylosaurus skeleton with a smaller long-necked skeleton drawn inside its ribs. Label: "1918".]

The worst part is the roof of its mouth.

On top of the teeth lining its jaws, Tylosaurus had two extra rows of teeth on the roof of its mouth. They pointed backward, so once something was inside, those teeth helped move it toward the throat and made it very hard to back out.

Doug has seen this idea before, on a moray eel. Tylosaurus had the extra-large version.

## Basilosaurus

Basilosaurus.

Basilosaurus means king lizard. It is not a lizard, and it was never anybody's king.

[A long, snake-like whale with a toothy head. A label "KING LIZARD" crossed out, "WHALE" written under it. Label: "ABOUT 37 MILLION YEARS AGO".]

In eighteen thirty-four, the scientist who named it thought he'd found the bones of a giant sea reptile. A few years later, another scientist looked closely at its teeth and realized it was a mammal, an early whale. By then the name was stuck, because under the rules of naming animals, the first name wins.

Basilosaurus lived between about thirty-eight and thirty-four million years ago. One famous skeleton from the Valley of the Whales in Egypt is about fifteen meters long, with a long, almost eel-like body.

And it still had legs.

[Close-up: two tiny hind legs on the side of the long body, with knees, ankles and toes.]

Two tiny back legs stuck out of its sides, complete with knees, ankles and toes. Each one was about the size of a three-year-old child's leg, which is far too small to walk on.

Here comes the dark part.

In twenty nineteen, scientists studied what was preserved inside one of those skeletons. They found the bones of young Dorudon, a smaller kind of whale, along with a large fish. The Dorudon were so young they were probably still drinking milk, and bite marks on their skulls pointed to hunting, not scavenging.

So Basilosaurus was a whale that hunted other whales.

## Livyatan

Livyatan.

Today's sperm whales have teeth only in their lower jaw, and they mostly suck in squid, but their ancient cousin did something very different.

[A huge, blunt-headed whale with enormous teeth in both jaws. Label: "ABOUT 10 MILLION YEARS AGO". Map: the coast of Peru.]

About ten million years ago, off the coast of what is now Peru, Livyatan hunted with huge teeth in both its upper and lower jaws.

The biggest of them were up to thirty-six centimeters long. That's longer than a school ruler.

[A school ruler next to the tooth. The tooth wins.]

Guinness lists them as the largest teeth any animal has used for eating, and tusks don't count.

The whole animal is estimated at about thirteen and a half to eighteen and a half meters long. The scientists who described it think it most likely hunted medium-sized baleen whales, which were packed with fat.

Its name comes from Leviathan, the sea monster in the Bible, and from Herman Melville, who wrote Moby-Dick. The team first called it Leviathan, but that name already belonged to a fossil mastodon, so they switched to the Hebrew spelling.

But that's not the scariest thing in this water.

## Megalodon

Megalodon.

Megalodon means giant tooth, and teeth are almost all we have left of it.

[Near-black navy water. A single giant serrated tooth glows in the middle of the frame. Label: "23 TO 3.6 MILLION YEARS AGO".]

Its skeleton, jaws included, was made of cartilage, which broke down after death, so what survives is teeth and a few pieces of backbone.

The biggest teeth are more than eighteen centimeters long, about as long as a grown man's hand.

[A hand traced next to the tooth. Same length.]

Megalodon ruled the oceans from about twenty-three million years ago until about three point six million years ago, and its size has been argued about for decades.

One method, using the width of its teeth, gave a maximum of about twenty meters. Then, in twenty twenty-five, a study used a fossil backbone from Belgium, eleven meters long on its own, to estimate that one shark at about sixteen meters.

Some even bigger vertebrae suggest it may have reached twenty-four meters, which is two city buses parked end to end.

[Two city buses end to end. A long shark silhouette laid over them.]

The same study argued Megalodon was slimmer than people usually draw it, built more like a long, streamlined lemon shark than a bulky one.

It went after big prey. A whale rib from North Carolina, three to four million years old, carries three bite marks spaced to fit a Megalodon, and that whale survived the bite.

The most disturbing part is where Megalodon sat on the food chain.

In twenty twenty-two, scientists measured nitrogen locked inside fossil shark teeth, because the higher up the food chain an animal eats, the more of the heavy kind of nitrogen it carries. Megalodon's score was the highest ever measured.

To score that high, it must have been eating predators, and the predators that ate those predators.

[A food chain ladder. At the top, a tiny Doug from 008 waving. Above the ladder, a giant shark shadow.]

Doug once climbed the whole ocean food chain. Megalodon sat above the top of it.

So what finished it off? A second study used zinc in fossil teeth and found that early great white sharks were eating at nearly the same level, which suggests competition may have played a part, although that's not proven.
