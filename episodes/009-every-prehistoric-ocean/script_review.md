## 1. Facts
I checked 24 claims. Every source marked "search snippet" was re-opened where the site allowed it.

**Confirmed:**
- **Anomalocaris:** the Bicknell 2023 abstract says it "fed on soft-bodied animals swimming in a well-lit water column". The CSMonitor article gives about 16,000 lenses per eye and up to 28,000 for dragonflies.
- **Endoceras:** Wikipedia gives a 3 m Museum of Comparative Zoology specimen and a 5.73 m estimate. That is consistent with the script.
- **Jaekelopterus:** the 46 cm claw and the 2.33-2.59 m body estimate match the Braddy 2007 paper. The paper was opened directly.
- **Dunkleosteus (Anderson & Westneat):** the paper gives 4,414 N at the tip, peak gape in 20 ms ("one fiftieth of a second" is correct) and a modelled 6 m individual.
- **Helicoprion:**
  - The CT-scanned specimen was described in 1966 by Bendix-Almgreen.
  - The whorl sits in the lower jaw, the animal had "no upper teeth to speak of", and it belongs to the ratfish group (NatGeo).
  - The Idaho whorl is over 20 cm, "slightly smaller than an NBA basketball" (Live Science).
- **Cymbospondylus:** a skull of about 2 m and a body of about 17 m (Sci.News).
- **Pliosaurus:**
  - The Liopleurodon study came out 21 July 2026 in the *Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology* and gives "just over eight metres". Martill advised on *Walking with Dinosaurs* (Newswise).
  - Martill blamed the 25 m figure on "fragmentary material".
  - The 2023 Dorset skull is about 6.5 ft (Smithsonian).
- **Xiphactinus:** specimen FHSM VP-333, 1952, George F. Sternberg. The *Xiphactinus* is 4.0 m and the *Gillicus* 1.8 m. The prey is undigested, so the fish died soon after eating (Wikipedia, Gillicus).
- **Tylosaurus:** NatGeo gives more than 14 m, a diet of fish, seabirds, sharks, plesiosaurs and mosasaurs, and two extra rows of teeth on the roof of the mouth.
- **Basilosaurus:** Harlan named it in 1834. Owen re-identified it as a mammal in 1839. The name was kept because of the priority rule.
- **Livyatan:** Guinness lists it for the "largest teeth used for eating purposes", 36 cm, tusks excluded. The *Leviathan* Koch 1841 mastodon took the name first.
- **Megalodon:**
  - Florida Museum: teeth up to 7 in, about 65 ft from tooth width, a cartilage skeleton including the jaw.
  - Shimada 2025 via Sci.News: 11.1 m, 16.4 m, 24.3 m if the Danish vertebrae hold, and a lemon-shark shape.
  - Whale rib (Smithsonian): 3-4 million years old, "likely" megalodon, and the whale survived the attack.

**facts.md-only fix (not blocking for the script text):**
- **Tylosaurus 13-14 m.** facts.md credits this to "Early Campanian *T. proriger*". Since May 2026 (Zietlow et al., *Bull. AMNH*), the largest specimens, including the Kansas "Bunker" at about 13.2 m, belong to the new species *T. rex*. *T. proriger* is now 3.9-9.5 m.
  - The script line ("The largest individuals are estimated at around thirteen or fourteen meters") holds at genus level, so it can stay.
  - Update the citation to Zietlow 2026 or to NatGeo's "more than 14 m".

**Advisory (fix if cheap):**
- **Pliosaurus**, "a study published this July". This will read wrong from 2027 onward. Say "in twenty twenty-six".
- **Jaekelopterus**, "the biggest arthropod alive today, the Japanese spider crab". The cited NatGeo page only says "one of the world's largest". Say "the arthropod with the biggest leg span alive today".
- **Dunkleosteus**: the 4,400 N figure was modelled on a 6 m animal, and the next paragraph shrinks it to 3.4 m. Nothing is factually stated wrong, but the 6 m model could be noted in facts.md.

Required fixes:

5. **Endoceras (F5).** "In the Ordovician, shelled hunters like this were among the top predators in the sea, with a sturdy shell, a powerful beak and plenty of arms." Find a real source that names straight-shelled nautiloids or endocerids as top Ordovician predators, and drop "a powerful beak and plenty of arms" unless a source covers it. If no source is found, cut the sentence.
