### A kilometer.

**1. The Nose** (death 247)
- Facts:
  - Polar bears find ringed seals hidden in **birth lairs under the snow** by smell, then break in by rearing up and **slamming through the snow roof** with the forelegs
    (Stirling 1974 *Can. J. Zool.*; Smith 1980 *Can. J. Zool.* on ringed seal lairs; Stirling's book *Polar Bears* 2011).
  - Detection range: use only a sourced figure. Polar Bears International and Stirling give "about a kilometer" or "almost a mile" for seals.
    The internet's "32 km / 20 miles" is **unsourced and banned**.
  - Togunov et al. 2017 (*Scientific Reports*, "Windscapes and olfactory foraging in a large carnivore"): bears often travel **crosswind**, which lets them sample odours
    from a wide strip of ice. Word it as the paper does (a pattern in collar data, linked to scent sampling).
- Analogy: one sourced figure, a lair roof thickness or the detection range, against something everyday
  (for example, a kilometer is about ten football fields; the writer sources both sides).
- **Twist by 0:45 (about 145 words):** snow isn't cover. It's a smell window, and the bear walks sideways across the wind on purpose to read it.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug builds a neat little snow shelter "for safety" and seals himself in. A big white shape crosses the horizon sideways.
  - **"Doug did not agree to this."** (once, here)
  - The roof comes in. Death 247. Cut to black; the cap sits on the broken snow.

**2. The Fur** (death 248)
- Facts:
  - A polar bear's skin is **black**, and its hairs are **unpigmented and hollow**, not white (verify with a museum or peer-reviewed source).
  - **Myth to debunk:** "the hairs are fiber-optic tubes that pipe sunlight to the skin." Tested and rejected (Koon 1998, *Nature*; verify).
  - Polar bears lose so little heat that **thermal (infrared) cameras barely detect them**, except the face, nose and breath (Øritsland 1970s / Best 1982; verify).
    If no primary source can be reached, use an agency or university source, or cut it.
- Analogy: a bear the weight of a [sourced object] showing up on a thermal camera as just a nose. Keep it as an explanatory comparison.
- Twist: the one tool people built to find warm animals in the dark mostly sees a floating nose.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug scans the ice with a thermal camera. The screen is empty, then shows a small glowing nose, then two. Death 248.
  - Kicker: the camera works perfectly; the cap is the warmest thing on it.

### A hundred meters.

**3. The Stalk** (death 249)
- Facts:
  - On open ice, bears stalk seals lying beside their holes by **freezing whenever the seal lifts its head** and creeping in when it sleeps.
    They use ridges for cover and finish with a short rush (Stirling 1974; Stirling 2011).
  - **Myth to check:** the bear "covers its black nose with a paw." It comes from traditional accounts but is **not documented by researchers** (Stirling has said he never saw it; verify).
    Use it only as the myth, if it can be sourced that way; otherwise cut it.
- Analogy: the last rush distance from the source against a familiar one (a tennis court, a bus length).
- Twist: it moves only when you aren't looking, so every time you check, nothing has changed.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug watches the ice with binoculars. Each time he looks up, there is a white lump that wasn't there before, slightly closer. "rock?"
  - Death 249. The cap is left on the binoculars.

**4. The Swim** (Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine."; costume gag 1 of 1)
- Facts:
  - The **aquatic stalk:** the bear swims along the leads, surfaces quietly at the floe edge and lunges onto the ice at a seal lying there (Stirling 1974).
  - **Most hunts fail.** Use the success rate exactly as the source states it, for stalks in general and for the aquatic stalk in particular
    (Stirling 1974 or Stirling & Latour 1978; Stirling 2011). This is what makes the survival honest.
- Analogy: the success rate against an everyday ratio (for example, "about one try in ten" against a free-throw miss rate; source both or keep it as plain numbers).
- Twist: the most famous hunter in the Arctic misses most of the time, and it has to keep trying because it **has to**.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug lies on a floe in the seal costume (`seal_costume.json`), cap on top, "which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a seal".
  - **008 callback (one sentence):** last time he wore this near a polar bear he used the breathing hole; this time he stays on top.
  - The bear surges up the edge and misses. It slides back into the water. "Doug is fine." The narrator is quietly disappointed.
  - Counter stays at 249.

**6. The Town** (Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:

- Analogy: the cell count or the helicopter sling against something everyday.
- Twist: people solved the polar bear problem by building a jail for it, and the key rule is **don't feed the prisoners**, because the bear remembers.
