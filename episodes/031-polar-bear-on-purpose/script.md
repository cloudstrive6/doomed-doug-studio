The Nose.

## The Fur

The Fur.

[Split panel: left, a white bear. Right, the same bear with the fur peeled away as a flat cartoon outline, revealing solid black skin.]

Under all that white, a polar bear's skin is black. And the fur isn't white either. Its hairs have no pigment at all, and they are clear, only looking white because of the way they scatter light.

[A single clear hair on a lab bench. A red arrow follows a ray of sunlight down it to the skin. A big red "?".]

For years, a popular idea said the hairs worked like fiber-optic cables, carrying sunlight down to the black skin to warm it up, but nobody had tested it. ## The Swim

The Swim.

[An ice floe beside dark open water. Three bearded seals lying at the edge. A ripple in the water, then nothing.]

In two thousand fourteen, a polar bear in Svalbard stayed underwater for three minutes and ten seconds, while it was sneaking up on three seals.

It's called an aquatic stalk, where the bear swims toward seals resting at the edge of the ice and then dives, so that the final approach happens completely out of sight. The Thin Bear.

[A bear on bare autumn tundra, no ice in sight. A berry bush beside it. A small scale icon. Label: "MINUS 1 KG A DAY".]

When polar bears are stuck on land for the summer, they lose about a kilogram of body weight a day, even when they find things to eat.

[A grid of twenty small bear icons. Nineteen get a red down arrow. One gets a green up arrow next to a whale-shaped carcass.]

In a study published in twenty twenty-four, scientists followed twenty polar bears on land in Manitoba, from August to September, with collars that included cameras. The bears ate berries, grass, birds, bones and even caribou antlers, and more than half of them ate berries, but it barely helped. Nineteen of the twenty lost weight, and the only one that gained had found a dead marine mammal.
