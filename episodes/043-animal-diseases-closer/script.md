## Marburg

Marburg.

[A map of Europe. A red pin in a small German town labeled "MARBURG". A date card: "1967".]

This virus lives in African caves, but it is named after a university town in Germany, because that is where it first killed people.

[A sober card, plain text on beige: "1967. MARBURG, FRANKFURT, BELGRADE. 31 ILL. 7 DIED." No Doug. No joke.]

In nineteen sixty-seven, laboratory workers in Marburg, Frankfurt and Belgrade fell ill at almost the same time, and thirty-one people got sick, seven of whom died. Every case traced back to one shipment of African green monkeys, imported from Uganda for research.

[A crate labeled "UGANDA" with three monkey silhouettes. A red arrow from the crate, then a dotted line that just stops.]

The monkeys had passed the virus on, but nobody could say where the monkeys got it.

Here's the disturbing part.

[A calendar flips fast, page after page, from "1967" to "2007".]

It took about forty years to find the real carrier. After miners and tourists in Uganda caught the virus in two thousand seven and two thousand eight, scientists traced it to the Egyptian fruit bat. The bats were living in a mine and a cave, a few dozen kilometers apart.

[A sober card: "2007-2008. 2 TOURISTS INFECTED AFTER VISITING ONE CAVE. 1 DIED." No Doug.]

That cave holds about forty thousand of them.

[Python Cave cross-section. The ceiling is solid with tiny bat shapes. A stadium silhouette beside it, labeled "ABOUT THE SAME CROWD".]

Picture a sold-out stadium crowd, hanging upside down from the ceiling, and every single one of them looking perfectly healthy. The bats are not attacking anyone; they simply carry the virus without showing any sign of disease, which is exactly what makes them so good at it.

[Doug walks into the cave with a torch, cheerful, cap on. He waves the torch at the ceiling. Forty thousand tiny wings shift at once. It looks like the ceiling is waving back.]

Doug walked in with a torch and waved hello at the ceiling, and forty thousand bats moved at once. Doug did not agree to this, although he did wave first.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 330. The cap sits on top of a stalagmite. The HOW CLOSE sign gets its first plank: CAVE.]

Doug has died.

The next bat never needs a cave, because it comes to breakfast.

## The Date Palm

The Date Palm.

[A tall date palm at night. A clay pot tied high on the trunk, under a shaved strip of bark. A sap drip falls into it.]

In Bangladesh, one of the deadliest viruses on the planet can reach people in a sweet drink. The World Health Organization estimates that Nipah virus kills forty to seventy-five percent of the people it infects.

[Night. A big fruit bat lands on the trunk beside the pot. A bag of flour beside it for scale, labeled "SAME WEIGHT".]

In winter, collectors shave a strip of bark off a date palm and hang a clay pot under it overnight to catch the sweet sap. Big fruit bats, some as heavy as a bag of flour, visit those trees in the dark. When researchers pointed infrared cameras at the pots, they filmed the bats licking the sap and touching it as it collected.

[Morning. Someone pours fresh sap into a cup. A small faceless blob floats in the cup under a magnifying glass.]

When scientists compared people who got sick with healthy neighbours, the one exposure that clearly stood out was drinking raw date palm sap.

The worst part is that the bat doesn't even have to meet you.

[A map of Malaysia. A date card: "1998". A pig farm silhouette, a bat flying over it.]

When the virus was first identified, in Malaysia in nineteen ninety-eight, it had moved from bats into farm pigs, and the pigs passed it on to the people who worked with them.

[A sober card: "MALAYSIA, 1998-1999. 265 CASES. 105 DIED. ABOUT 1 MILLION PIGS CULLED." No Doug.]

Two hundred and sixty-five people fell ill, one hundred and five died, and about a million pigs were culled to stop it.

Doug has met things that live inside animals. These are worse. They leave.

[Dawn. Doug climbs the palm with a cup, cap on, very pleased with himself. He reaches the pot. A bat hangs right above it, licking its lips.]

Doug climbed the palm at dawn to get the freshest cup of the morning, but somebody had already taken the first sip.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 331. The cap hangs on the sap pot. HOW CLOSE adds a shorter plank: PALM TREE.]

The next animal isn't after Doug's breakfast, just his hat.

## The Temple Monkey

The Temple Monkey.

[A temple staircase. A row of macaques sitting on the railing. One faceless blob under a magnifying glass beside them, labeled "B VIRUS".]

Macaque monkeys commonly carry a virus that barely bothers them, and it is one of the deadliest things a monkey can give a person.

[A chart titled "SINCE 1932". Fifty little stick figures, twenty-one of them grey.]

It's called B virus, and in macaques it usually causes no symptoms at all, or something mild. In people, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention counts only fifty documented infections since the virus was identified in nineteen thirty-two, and twenty-one of those people died.

[A sober card: "2021. CHINA'S FIRST RECORDED HUMAN CASE. A VETERINARIAN. DIED." No Doug.]

Without treatment, about seventy percent of human infections are fatal. In two thousand twenty-one, China recorded its first human case, a veterinarian, who died.

But that's not the strange part.

[A yellow school bus with seventy-two seats. Fifty seats glow, twenty-two stay dark.]

The virus spreads mainly through bites and scratches, and yet across about ninety years it has reached only fifty people, which would not even fill one school bus. It is almost unbelievably deadly, and it almost never manages to get across.

[Doug on the temple steps. A macaque snatches his cap and sits on the railing with it. Doug holds his phone up for a selfie with the monkey, hopeful. The monkey bites his hand.]

When a macaque snatched Doug's cap, Doug tried to win it back with a selfie, and he was bitten.

[The narrator's hand holds the CDC chart up to Doug's face. Then pulls it back, checks it, and holds it up again.]

The narrator has now checked the chart twice, and Doug is still on the right side of it.

[The macaque drops the cap on Doug's head and walks off. HOW CLOSE adds a shorter plank: TEMPLE. DOUG DEATHS stays at 331.]

The monkey gave the cap back, because it didn't want it.

The next one doesn't bite at all. It floats in on the wind.

## The Goat Dust

The Goat Dust.

[A single tiny dot under a giant magnifying glass. Label: "1 TO 10".]

As few as one to ten of these bacteria may be enough to infect a person, and you never have to touch the animal that carries them.

[A goat barn. Dust drifts out of the door and across a field. Tiny dots ride on it.]

The disease is Q fever. Goats, sheep and cattle carry the bacterium, and they shed enormous numbers of it when they give birth. It dries into the barn dust, people catch it by breathing that dust in, and the wind can carry it a long way from the farm.

Here's the problem.

[A map of the southern Netherlands. Red dots spread across it. A date card: "2007-2010".]

Between two thousand seven and two thousand ten, the Netherlands had the largest Q fever outbreak ever reported, and more than four thousand people were notified as sick.

[A sober card: "THE NETHERLANDS, 2007-2010. MORE THAN 4,000 HUMAN CASES. ABOUT 50,000 GOATS CULLED." No Doug.]

To stop it, the country culled about fifty thousand goats. Around one Dutch town, people living within two kilometers of a single infected goat farm were about thirty-one times more likely to catch it than people living more than five kilometers away.

[Twenty football fields laid end to end, from a goat barn to a row of houses.]

Two kilometers is about twenty football fields laid end to end, so you only have to live downwind.

[Doug walks along the farm lane, cheeks puffed out, holding his breath. A gust of wind blows the cap off his head and down the lane. The dust follows Doug.]

Doug held his breath the whole way past the farm, and the wind was going his way anyway.

[Doug, red in the face, finally breathes out. He looks around. Thumbs up. DOUG DEATHS stays at 331. HOW CLOSE adds a shorter plank: FIELD.]

Doug is fine. Most people who catch it have no symptoms, or only mild ones, and Doug is one of them. The narrator would have preferred that he noticed.

## The Chicken

The Chicken.

[A crude chart: "SINCE 2003: ABOUT 1,000 CASES REPORTED". About half the stick figures are grey.]

Since two thousand three, about a thousand people have been reported to the World Health Organization with H five N one bird flu, and nearly half of them died.

[A chicken coop. Faceless blobs under a magnifying glass, hovering near the chickens.]

Most of those people caught it through close contact with infected birds or the places where they live. It is a bird virus, and before two thousand twenty-four, it had never been found in dairy cows.

Then came the cow.

[A date card: "MARCH 2024". A Holstein cow in a milking stall. A milk bucket with a faceless blob floating in it.]

In March, two thousand twenty-four, it turned up in dairy cattle in the United States, and scientists found the virus at high levels in the raw milk of infected cows. By late May, it had reached fifty-two herds in nine states.

[The cow next to a row of stick figures. Label: "HOLSTEIN, ABOUT 680 KG. DOUG, 80 KG."]

A grown Holstein cow weighs about as much as eight Dougs, and it is very much not a bird.

Most of the people infected in the United States so far have had mild illness, which is good news for people and no help at all to Doug.

[Doug walks into the coop with a clipboard, cap on, to check on the chickens. A chicken stares at him. He backs out quickly and goes next door to the milking stall. He pats the cow.]

Doug went into the coop with a clipboard to check on the chickens, then hurried next door to the milking stall, for safety. The cow is not safer.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 332. The cap sits in the coop nest with three eggs. HOW CLOSE adds a shorter plank: BARN.]

On the next farm, the killer isn't even alive, and it's on the menu.

## The Burger

The Burger.

[A crowd in a stadium doing the wave. One stick figure in the crowd is doing it upside down. Then the person next to him copies it. Then the next.]

The deadliest thing on this farm is not a germ at all, but a protein that has folded the wrong way.

[A crude diner. A date card on the wall: "UK, 1989". A burger on a plate.]

Prion diseases happen when proteins that are normally in the body fold into the wrong shape, and the bad shape makes the normal ones misfold too. It's like one fan at a stadium doing the wave wrong, until the whole stadium is doing it wrong.

[A map of Britain with a cow silhouette. A date card: "1996".]

In Britain in the late nineteen eighties, a prion disease spread through cattle, and people called it mad cow disease. In nineteen ninety-six, doctors recognised a new human version, linked to eating beef from infected cows.

The heat is where it gets worse.

[A kitchen oven dial at its top setting, then a thermometer climbing far past it. Label: "350 °C".]

Cooking can't be relied on to destroy it. In lab experiments, even three hundred and fifty degrees Celsius, far hotter than the top setting of a kitchen oven, only partly broke these agents down.

[A sober card: "UK. 178 CASES OF VARIANT CJD. ALL DIED. PEAK: 2000." No Doug.]

Britain has recorded one hundred and seventy-eight cases of the human disease, the worst year was two thousand, and every one of those people died, after a disease that can take years to show itself.

[Doug at the diner table, cap on, pointing at the menu. Speech bubble from the waiter: "HOW DO YOU WANT IT?"]

Doug ordered his burger well done, to be safe.

[A wall calendar flips through years: 1990, 1991, 1992, 1993. Doug sits in the same seat, smiling, the whole time. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 333. The cap lies on the diner table next to an empty plate. HOW CLOSE adds a shorter plank: PLATE.]

Doug has died. Again. It took years, and he never once complained about the food.

The next one doesn't wait for dinner, because it's already in the shed.

## The Shed

The Shed.

[A wooden shed at the edge of a yard. The door is shut. A date card: "MAY 1993".]

In the United States, the most dangerous part of meeting a deer mouse doesn't involve touching the mouse at all.

[The Four Corners: a crude map where four state outlines meet, with a red dot in the middle.]

In May, nineteen ninety-three, a cluster of people in the Four Corners region of the American Southwest suddenly developed severe breathing failure. Scientists traced it to a new hantavirus carried by the deer mouse, and named it Sin Nombre, which is Spanish for "without a name".

[A deer mouse beside four nickels on a balance. They balance.]

A deer mouse weighs about twenty grams, the same as four nickels.

[A sober card: "CDC: 38 OF EVERY 100 PEOPLE WHO DEVELOP BREATHING SYMPTOMS MAY DIE." No Doug.]

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, thirty-eight percent of people who develop breathing symptoms may die from it.

The disturbing part is the housework.

[Inside the dark shed. Fresh droppings and a mouse nest on the floor; a mouse peeks out. When the door opens, dust lifts off the floor into the air.]

People can catch it when fresh mouse urine, droppings or nesting material get stirred up, and tiny droplets carrying the virus float into the air. Opening up sheds and cabins that sat closed all winter is a known risk, especially out in the country.

In the United States, this strain doesn't spread from person to person, although one South American strain, the Andes virus, can.

[Doug opens the shed in spring, cap on, wearing an apron, holding a broom high like a trophy. He sweeps with huge enthusiasm. A giant dust cloud fills the doorway.]

Doug opened the shed for spring cleaning and swept with real enthusiasm. The mice had spent the whole winter in there, and they were still home.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 334. The cap hangs on the broom handle. HOW CLOSE adds a shorter plank: SHED.]

The next carrier lets itself in through the cat flap.

## The Cat

The Cat.

[A medieval town silhouette, then a modern prairie with prairie dog mounds. A date card: "1347", then "2026".]

The germ behind the Black Death never went away, and today it lives in wild rodents and their fleas across the American West.

[A flea beside a sesame seed. They're about the same size.]

Plague is caused by a bacterium carried by ground squirrels, prairie dogs and other rodents, and by the fleas that bite them. A flea is about the size of a sesame seed, and in the fourteenth century, fleas helped plague kill somewhere between thirty and fifty percent of Europe's people.

[A US map with dots in the Southwest and West. Label: "ABOUT 7 A YEAR".]

Today, the United States averages about seven human cases a year, mostly in the rural West, and plague can be treated with antibiotics.

Here's the twist.

[A plain grey house cat sitting calmly beside a ground squirrel mound. It looks like any cat.]

Pet cats can catch plague by hunting infected rodents. When a cat gets the lung form of it, people can catch it from the cat by breathing in the droplets. That lung form is the one that can also spread from person to person.

The cat is not doing this on purpose, and neither are the squirrels. They are carriers, not villains.

[Night. The cat flap swings open. The cat walks in with a cartoon ground squirrel, perfectly intact, and drops it at Doug's feet. Doug looks down. Speech bubble: "thank you?"]

The cat came in through the flap, very proud of itself, and dropped a present right at Doug's feet.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 335. The cap sits beside the cat's present on the doormat. The cat, unbothered, licks a paw. HOW CLOSE adds a short plank: CAT FLAP.]

The cat is fine. The last animal on this list doesn't need a flea, because it only needs Doug to love it.

## The Dog

The Dog.

[A plain brown dog sitting on a couch. Not cute, not sick. It just looks like a dog.]

Once its symptoms appear, rabies is virtually one hundred percent fatal.

[A world map. Africa and Asia shaded darker. Label: "TENS OF THOUSANDS OF DEATHS A YEAR".]

The World Health Organization's latest estimate is about forty-four thousand deaths a year, mostly in Asia and Africa, and in up to ninety-nine percent of human cases, the virus came from a dog.

[A clock face. One stick figure goes grey every time the minute hand passes twelve.]

That works out to about one person every twelve minutes, a little longer than this video has been playing.

[A crude nerve line running from a hand to a head. A faceless blob moves slowly along it.]

The virus usually gets in through a bite and then moves inside the nerves toward the brain. That trip usually takes two to three months, but it can be as short as a week or as long as a year. Later on, one of the signs can be a fear of water.

The cruel part is the timing.

[A calendar with weeks crossed off. Nothing on any of the days.]

While the virus travels, a person feels nothing at all, and by the time they feel anything, it is almost always too late. Only a few dozen people at most have ever survived after their symptoms began.

[A date card: "PASTEUR, 1885".]

The World Health Organization calls rabies vaccine-preventable, and the first rabies vaccine was given to a person in eighteen eighty-five.

[A bat silhouette flies past a US map.]

In the United States, where dog rabies has been eliminated, most people who catch it now get it from a bat. The list ends exactly where it started.

[Setting deliberately unplaced: Doug meets the stray on a generic street with no flags, signs, landmarks or prairie, and the couch room has no shed or cat flap in view. No US cues in this scene. Doug opens his front door. The stray dog walks in and climbs onto the couch. Doug sits beside it, smiling. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug brought home a stray and shared the couch.

[A wall calendar flips slowly, one week at a time. Doug sits on the couch, smiling, the dog asleep beside him. Nothing else happens. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 336. A big red stamp slams down on the last plank of HOW CLOSE: COUCH.]

Doug has died. Again. Nothing seemed wrong until the very end, which is how it works.

[A cardboard box on a shelf. The red cap sits inside. A label on the box: "UNDER OBSERVATION: 10 DAYS".]

In the United States, a healthy dog that bites someone can be watched for ten days. The cap is being watched for ten days. Doug was not that lucky, and nobody thought to watch him.

[End slate. HOW CLOSE: COUCH in red. DOUG DEATHS: 336.]
