## 2. Escalation axis and items
**Axis: distance to Doug's front door.** The animal that carries each disease lives closer to Doug at every stop, starting in a cave in
Uganda and ending on his couch. The physical axis carries the horror: you can avoid a cave, but you can't avoid your own house. The last
stop is also the deadliest, so the axis and severity agree at the boss.
Spoken bands, each said once the first time we enter it: **"The wild." / "The farm." / "The house."**
**Episode device:** a **HOW CLOSE** sign. It's a crude wooden fingerpost beside the death counter. Each stop adds a plank, and the planks get
shorter every time: CAVE, PALM TREE, TEMPLE, FIELD, BARN, PLATE, SHED, CAT FLAP, COUCH. It only moves toward the house, and COUCH is stamped
red at the boss. It's staging only and never read aloud. The rabies boss carries the episode's only "under observation" kicker (sec 2, item 9).

Deaths go **330 to 336** (7 deaths; items 3 and 4 survive). Nine items, the most extreme last. **Death 330 (item 1) is a round number: play it
flat.**

### The wild.
**1. Marburg** (one of the three strongest: the highest death rate in the wild band, plus the city twist)
- Facts to research: WHO fact sheet case fatality "24% to 88%" (use the exact WHO wording). **First identified in 1967 in Marburg and
  Frankfurt, Germany, and in Belgrade**, in lab workers handling African green monkeys imported from Uganda. The monkeys were not the source.
  The reservoir, the Egyptian fruit bat (*Rousettus aegyptiacus*), was confirmed about 40 years later (Towner et al. 2009, *PLoS Pathogens*,
  Kitaka/Python Cave, Uganda). The cave colony size, from Amman et al. 2012 if it gives one. Python Cave tourist cases in 2007-2008, as a sober
  card: numbers and years only, no names.
- **Twist (by 0:45):** the first outbreak wasn't in a jungle. It was in a German city, and it took four decades to find the bat.
- Scale analogy: the bat that carries it weighs about as much as a smartphone (check the mass). Or: tens of thousands of them in one cave is
  a stadium crowd hanging from the ceiling.
- Doug beat: "Doug did not agree to this." (once, here). Doug enters the cave with a torch and waves at the ceiling. The ceiling waves back.
  Death 330, played flat. Death visual: X eyes, lying flat, ghost Doug. **No bleeding of any kind** (it's a hemorrhagic fever: no symptom
  imagery).

**2. The Date Palm**
- Facts to research: Nipah virus, carried by *Pteropus* fruit bats. In Bangladesh, people catch it by drinking **raw date palm sap**: bats
  lick the sap as it drips into collection pots overnight (Luby et al. 2006, *Emerging Infectious Diseases*; Khan et al. 2010, infrared video
  of bats at the pots). WHO case fatality "40% to 75%". Malaysia 1998-99, where pigs passed it on (WHO case and death counts, plus the
  number of pigs culled).
- **Twist:** the deadliest step is a sweet drink at breakfast.
- Scale analogy: a collection pot about the size of a kitchen kettle, with a bat-sized straw it never asked for.
- Doug beat: Doug climbs the palm at dawn to "get the freshest cup". Someone already had the first sip. Death 331.

**3. The Temple Monkey** (survival)
- Facts to research: B virus (*Macacine alphaherpesvirus 1*). In macaques it's usually mild, similar to cold sores in people (CDC). In
  humans, CDC counts only about 50 documented cases since 1932, 21 of them fatal (verify both numbers). Untreated death rate about 70-80%
  (CDC wording). 2021: China's first recorded human case, a veterinarian, who died. Sober clause, no name.
- **Twist:** it's one of the deadliest viruses a primate can give a person, and it almost never manages to.
- Scale analogy: about 50 cases in ninety years is fewer than one person per two years. That's rarer than being struck by lightning (only use
  this comparison if a source supports it; otherwise use "fewer people than fit on one bus").
- Doug beat: a temple macaque takes the cap, and Doug takes a selfie to get it back. Bitten. **Survival: Doug is fine.** The odds were with
  him. The narrator checks the CDC chart twice. The cap comes back. The monkey didn't want it.

### The farm.
**4. The Goat Dust** (survival)
- Facts to research: Q fever (*Coxiella burnetii*). CDC: **a single bacterium may be enough to infect a person** (check the exact wording,
  e.g. "as few as 1-10 organisms"). It spreads in dust from goat and sheep birthing products and can travel on the wind (CDC; Dutch outbreak
  papers). **The Netherlands 2007-2010: the largest Q fever outbreak ever recorded**, about 4,000 notified human cases (Schneeberger et al. or
  RIVM figures). The goat cull number. About half of infected people never get symptoms (CDC).
- **Twist:** you don't have to touch a goat. You only have to live downwind of one.
- Scale analogy: one bacterium against a grain of sand is a grain of sand against a house (check the sizes; *C. burnetii* is about 0.2-0.4 µm
  wide, and a sand grain about 0.5 mm).
- Doug beat: Doug holds his breath walking past the farm. The wind is going his way anyway. **Survival: "Doug is fine."** (the only use). He
  is in the half that never notices. The narrator would have preferred that he noticed.

**5. The Chicken**
- Facts to research: H5N1 bird flu. WHO cumulative human cases and deaths since 2003, about half fatal "among cases reported to WHO" (use the
  latest WHO table and say "reported"). **2024: it reached US dairy cattle** (USDA/CDC), a mammal nobody expected. Most recent US human cases
  have been mild (CDC). No human-to-human spread claim beyond what WHO/CDC state. No pandemic predictions.
- **Twist:** a bird virus started showing up in cows' milk.
- Scale analogy: the WHO's count since 2003 would fit in one large school auditorium, and about half of it didn't leave (check the numbers).
- Doug beat: Doug enters the coop to "check on the chickens". Death 332. Then a beat at the milking stall: Doug moves to the cow, "for safety".
  The cow is not safer.

**6. The Burger**
- Facts to research: BSE ("mad cow disease") and variant CJD. A **prion** is a misfolded protein with no DNA or RNA, and it makes the brain's
  normal proteins misfold too (CDC or NIH). Normal cooking does not destroy it (CDC/WHO wording). UK vCJD case total, about 178 (UK National
  CJD Research & Surveillance Unit), and every case was fatal. Cases peaked around 2000. Incubation can last years.
- **Twist:** the deadliest thing on the farm isn't even alive. It's a protein folded the wrong way.
- Scale analogy: a prion is like one person in a stadium doing the wave wrong, until the whole stadium does it wrong (keep it a crowd
  analogy, never brain imagery).
- Doug beat: a diner date card ("UK, 1989"; no time machine, which belongs to `prehistoric`). Doug orders "well done, to be safe". Cooking
  doesn't help. A calendar flips through years. Death 333. **No brain or "spongy brain" visuals.** The death is the calendar plus a flat Doug.

### The house.
**7. The Shed**
- Facts to research: hantavirus pulmonary syndrome (Sin Nombre virus), carried by deer mice. **1993 Four Corners outbreak**, where the virus
  was identified (CDC). People catch it by breathing dust from droppings and urine stirred up while cleaning closed spaces (CDC). CDC: about
  38% of people with respiratory symptoms die. Sin Nombre doesn't spread person to person in the US (CDC). The Andes strain in South America
  can (CDC), as one clause.
- **Twist:** the dangerous moment is housework: the broom, not the mouse.
- Scale analogy: a deer mouse weighs about as much as two coins (check), and the shed holds a whole winter of them.
- Doug beat: Doug opens the cabin shed for spring cleaning and sweeps with enthusiasm. Death 334. **No cleaning guidance at all** (it reads
  as safety advice; sec 7). Don't mention any recent celebrity cases.

**8. The Cat**
- Facts to research: plague (*Yersinia pestis*) still lives in wild rodents and fleas in the western US. **CDC average of about 7 human cases a
  year.** Prairie dogs and ground squirrels carry it. **Pet cats can catch it and give people pneumonic plague**, the form that spreads person
  to person (CDC). One line on the Black Death's share of Europe killed (cite a historian's range). One fact line that antibiotics treat it (CDC
  states it). It's a fact, not advice.
- **Twist:** the Black Death never left. It moved to the prairie, and the cat brings it home.
- Scale analogy: a flea is smaller than a sesame seed (check), and it is the reason a third of Europe died (adjust to the cited range).
- Doug beat: the cat brings Doug a "present" through the cat flap: a cartoon ground squirrel, never a gory one. Doug says "thank you?". Death
  335. The cat looks unbothered. No cat is ever shown sick.

**9. The Dog** (the boss and the payoff promise)
- Facts to research: rabies. WHO: about **59,000 deaths a year**, with **dogs behind up to 99% of human cases**. **Virtually 100% fatal once
  symptoms appear** (WHO wording). It travels along the nerves to the brain, and the incubation is usually 2-3 months but can be from a week
  to a year (WHO). In the US, most human cases now come from bats (CDC). That's the callback to item 1: the list ends where it started.
  Hydrophobia, as one plain sentence of fact (WHO), with no visual. A handful of people have survived after symptoms began. Cite the count and
  call it extremely rare. Don't present the Milwaukee protocol as a cure.
- **Honesty line (required):** WHO calls rabies 100% vaccine-preventable. Say it once, as a fact about the disease (Pasteur, 1885, as a date
  card). No instructions.
- **Twist:** the deadliest animal disease on Earth is mostly carried by man's best friend, and by the time you feel anything, it's over.
- Scale analogy: 59,000 a year is about one person every nine minutes (check the arithmetic: 525,600 min / 59,000 ≈ 8.9).
- Doug beat: Doug brings home a stray and shares the couch. "hi friend?" (once, here). It's a slow calendar flip of weeks, played completely
  flat. Death 336. The red COUCH stamp lands. **Never show the dog as monstrous, foaming or sick.** It sits on the couch and looks like a dog.
  The horror is the calendar.
- **Closing (and the kicker):** the cap sits in a box marked "UNDER OBSERVATION: 10 DAYS". The CDC's 10-day dog-observation period is real:
  verify it and use it only as a fact about the cap. Kicker along the lines of "The cap is being watched for ten days. Doug was not that
  lucky." Outro of 30 words or fewer: death counter plus one CTA.

## 7. Risks (this is the most policy-sensitive topic we've done: read this section twice)
- **Medical misinformation policy:** every disease claim must match WHO or CDC (or a national health agency or peer-reviewed paper that
  doesn't contradict them). Don't speculate about the next pandemic, don't make lab-leak or origin claims, and **don't mention COVID-19 at
  all**. Don't hedge or contradict vaccine efficacy. Rabies prevention is stated once, as WHO's fact.
- **Never approve safety or medical advice:** no "what to do if bitten", no cleaning instructions, no "cook it to X degrees", no treatment
  recommendations. The two allowed fact lines are "plague is treatable with antibiotics (CDC)" and "rabies is vaccine-preventable (WHO)", and
  each is said once, as a fact rather than an instruction.
- **Gore and symptom imagery: none.** No bleeding (Marburg), no foaming mouth (rabies), no rashes, no brains, no hospital beds with tubes, no
  sick animals. Every death is the standard cartoon (X eyes, lying flat, ghost Doug), often after a calendar flip. Viruses and bacteria may be
  drawn as simple cartoon blobs under a magnifying glass, never as body damage. Symptoms appear in narration only, at most one plain
  clause per item, never as a "this is what it feels like" sequence.
- **Real victims:** statistics and years only. Don't name any patient, including the 2021 vet, the Python Cave tourists or recent celebrity
  hantavirus cases. Use sober cards with no Doug and no joke wherever real deaths are counted (Marburg 1967, Malaysia 1998-99, the Dutch Q fever
  outbreak, Four Corners 1993, rabies' annual toll).
- **Fear and stigma:** don't frame bats, dogs, cats or farmers as villains, and never encourage harming animals. The narrator states once that
  the animals aren't sick on purpose, and that bats are carriers, not attackers. Don't use regional or ethnic framing (Bangladesh, Malaysia,
  Uganda and China are places on a map, never punchlines).
- **Advertiser-friendly:** disease content can get limited ads if it turns graphic. Keep the tone documentary and the visuals cartoon. No
  thumbnail text like "DEADLY VIRUS" with medical imagery.
- **Kid appeal (medium: dogs, cats and monkeys):** no puppy eyes, no cute kitten, no cartoon-mascot germs with faces. Germs are faceless
  blobs. Jokes stay dry and adult-coded.
- **Facts that need strong sources:**
  - WHO fact sheets: Marburg (CFR range), Nipah (CFR range, Malaysia counts), rabies (59,000, 99% dogs, ~100% once symptomatic, incubation,
    vaccine-preventable), H5N1 cumulative table (latest date).
  - Towner et al. 2009 (Marburg in Egyptian fruit bats); Amman et al. 2012 if you use a colony size.
  - Luby et al. 2006 and Khan et al. 2010 (date palm sap and bats).
  - CDC B virus (cases since 1932, deaths, untreated CFR); the 2021 China case (China CDC Weekly).
  - CDC Q fever (infectious dose, airborne spread, share without symptoms); the Netherlands 2007-2010 case count (RIVM or Schneeberger et
    al.).
  - USDA/CDC on H5N1 in US dairy cattle (2024).
  - CDC prion/vCJD and UK NCJDRSU case totals; the cooking/heat-resistance wording.
  - CDC hantavirus (Four Corners 1993, ~38% fatality, route, no person-to-person for Sin Nombre, Andes exception).
  - CDC plague (~7 US cases a year, cats and pneumonic plague, antibiotics); one historian's range for the Black Death's toll.
  - Rabies survivors after symptom onset (count and source); the CDC 10-day observation period for the closing.
  - Every scale analogy number (bat mass, deer mouse mass, flea size, bacterium size).
- **Pronunciation (editor):** Marburg "MAR-burg", Nipah "NEE-pah", *Rousettus* "roo-SET-us", *Pteropus* "TERR-oh-pus", macaque "muh-KAK",
  *Coxiella burnetii* "cox-ee-EL-ah bur-NET-ee-eye", prion "PREE-on", Sin Nombre "seen NOM-bray", *Yersinia pestis* "yer-SIN-ee-ah PES-tis".
