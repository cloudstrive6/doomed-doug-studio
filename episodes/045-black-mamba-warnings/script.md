## The Speed

The Speed.

[A galloping horse with a stick-figure rider. A mamba behind it. Label: "THE LEGEND".]

The legends say a black mamba can run down a man on horseback, but field guides say twenty kilometers an hour is about its limit.

That is slower than the speed at which a European electric bike stops helping you pedal, which is twenty-five kilometers an hour.

[An e-bike with a speedometer stuck at 25. The mamba, well behind it. Label: "20 KM/H, TOPS".]

There are plenty of old reports of mambas chasing riders, and the South African herpetologist Johan Marais puts it plainly: the chasing part is simply not true.

Here's the problem.

The black mamba usually turns defensive when something blocks its direct path home.

[The termite mound again. The mamba races toward the hole. A stick-figure man is between them. Red arrow: snake to hole, not snake to man.]

It is shy, and in the wild it seldom lets people get closer than about forty meters, less than half a football pitch, before it leaves.

So most apparent chases are probably a snake trying to get back to its lair, while a person happens to be standing in the way.

It is not running at you. It is running through the space you are standing in.

Graham Alexander, a herpetologist at Wits University, says Durban's black mambas often live in the same refuge for years.

That refuge can be a termite mound, a hollow log or an abandoned building, and in a city the last of those can be uncomfortably close to people.

If nothing disturbs it, a mamba keeps a permanent lair and comes back to it between hunting, basking and mating.

In South Africa, mambas have been recorded basking from about seven to ten in the morning and again in the afternoon, often returning to the same sunny spot.

The Snake Eagle.

## The Head Lift

The Head Lift.

[A mamba cornered against a garden wall. Its head and front body rise off the ground. Label: "WARNING ONE". WARNINGS LEFT: 2.]

A cornered black mamba raises its head far off the ground, and that is its first warning.

[A kitchen counter drawn next to the raised mamba, the snake's head level with the counter top.]

The black mamba is not a cobra, but when it is cornered it spreads a hood like one.

[The mouth opens. WARNINGS LEFT: 1.]

At the same time it opens its mouth wide, spreads a narrow flap of skin at the neck, and sometimes hisses.

One university species account lists the whole display in order: head up, mouth open, hood out, a flick of the tongue, then the hiss. After that comes the strike.

The hood is much narrower than a cobra's. A cobra's hood is an open umbrella, and a mamba's is a folded one, but nobody should stand under either.

The strange part is the family resemblance.

Mambas and cobras belong to the same family of snakes, the elapids, front-fanged venomous snakes that also include kraits, coral snakes and sea snakes.

## The Second Strike

The Second Strike.

[A blur where the snake was. Three small red "!" marks in a row. WARNINGS LEFT: 0, in red.]

When a cornered black mamba strikes, it rarely strikes only once.

It can strike several times in quick succession, and it tends to bite and let go rather than hold on.

Its eyes are tuned to movement, and once it is cornered, any sudden movement by the intruder can set off a whole series of rapid strikes.

On average a single bite can carry about a hundred to a hundred and twenty milligrams of venom. The biggest dose ever recorded was four hundred.

The strikes come the way a dropped phone falls: by the time you react, it has already landed.

The worst part is the timing.

## The Dendrotoxin

The Dendrotoxin.

[A field guide label: "DENDROASPIS = TREE ASP". A mamba draped on a branch.]

The black mamba's scientific name, Dendroaspis, means tree asp, and its venom carries toxins named after it, the dendrotoxins.

The first one was pulled out of the venom of its cousin, the green mamba, in nineteen eighty, and the black mamba turned out to carry its own.

[Zoom into a crude MS Paint nerve: a wire running to a muscle, with a doorbell icon where they meet.]

Nerves pass messages to muscles in quick electrical pulses, and tiny potassium channels help each nerve reset after every pulse.

Dendrotoxins block some of those channels, and the very first one studied was found to make nerves release more of their chemical messenger.

A second set of toxins, the fasciculins, block the enzyme that clears that messenger away afterwards.

If a nerve is a doorbell, dendrotoxin glues the button down, and fasciculin unplugs the thing that makes it stop ringing.

[The doorbell icon ringing nonstop, sound lines everywhere.]

Here's the twist.

These toxins are so precise that scientists have used them for decades as lab tools. Dendrotoxins became widely used as probes for studying how potassium channels work.

One black mamba toxin mostly picks out a single type of potassium channel and leaves the others alone.

[Doug, shrunk to fit inside the nerve diagram, cap on, pulling at the glued doorbell button with both hands.]

Doug is shrunk down into the nerve diagram, and he spends the whole time trying to unstick the doorbell with his fingers.

The doorbell keeps ringing.

[Cut to the counter. DOUG DEATHS: 349.]

But the same venom also carries something that does the opposite, and switches pain off.

## The Painkiller

The Painkiller.

[A test tube labelled "MAMBALGIN". Next to it, a morphine bottle drawn as a plain brown bottle. A white lab mouse between them.]

Black mamba venom contains a painkiller that worked about as well as morphine. In mice.

In twenty twelve, a team writing in the journal Nature described a new group of peptides from black mamba venom and named them mambalgins.

They belong to the same three-finger family of small proteins as many of the snake world's nerve toxins, but these ones point the other way.

Mambalgins block acid-sensing ion channels, which are part of the pain pathway, and in mice they were not toxic.

They relieved pain about as strongly as morphine, the mice built up much less tolerance to them, and they caused no breathing trouble.

It is like finding a first-aid kit inside a fire alarm.

Here's the catch.

This was research in mice, and mambalgins are not a medicine, nobody is treating people with them, and the scientists themselves only claimed new targets worth studying.

[The test tube under a red stamp: "MICE ONLY".]

The same venom that jams nerves shut also contains something that turns pain down, and right now the only patients are rodents.

[Doug in a quiet library, cap on the desk, reading a printout titled "MAMBALGINS". A thumbs up.]

[The narrator's hand checks under the library desk. No snake.]

## The Long Ride

The Long Ride.

[A sober card, no Doug: "BEFORE 1962: 15 OF 35 DIED". Plain black text on white.]

Before nineteen sixty-two, people bitten by black mambas in southern Africa were given an antivenom that had no effect on mamba venom, and fifteen of thirty-five who received it died.

A mamba-specific antivenom arrived in nineteen sixty-two. Over the next years, five of thirty-eight bitten people given antivenom died.

[A second sober card: "WITH ANTIVENOM: 5 OF 38 DIED".]

In Durban today, Nick Evans says almost all patients survive, unless they do not go to hospital.

Here's the problem.

The hospital has to be there, and you have to reach it.

[A crude map of Africa with a few clinic dots and long red dotted lines between villages and the dots.]

The World Health Organization estimates that Africa has four hundred and thirty-five thousand to five hundred and eighty thousand snakebites a year that need treatment.

Many victims never reach a health centre at all, and in some areas antivenom has become scarce or disappeared.

At one hospital in northern Benin, snakebite patients took a median of seven hours to arrive, and the slowest took more than two days.

That median is a full working day, spent just getting through the hospital door.

Most tried traditional care first, and antivenom was the biggest cost of treatment, about a hundred and twenty-eight euros.

[The mamba sliding away under a bush. Label: "LEAVING".]

And the mamba itself would rather sneak past people, or hide, than fight them.
