# Facts: 045 Every Reason the Black Mamba Is the Snake Africa Fears Most (draft 2)

Every factual claim in script.md is listed here with its source. Script writer, 2026-11-12.

How each source was checked:
- **Fetched**: I read the page, raw wiki text or abstract myself (abstracts via the Europe PMC REST API).
- **Search**: confirmed only from search-result text quoting or summarising the page.
- **Secondary**: encyclopedia, zoo, news or Wikipedia page, not the original study. Wikipedia rows name the reference the sentence cites.

Main sources:
- **ADW**: Animal Diversity Web, *Dendroaspis polylepis* (University of Michigan): https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Dendroaspis_polylepis/ (Fetched, full text extracted).
- **ASI / Marais**: Johan Marais, "The truth about Black Mambas", African Snakebite Institute: https://www.africansnakebiteinstitute.com/articles/the-truth-about-black-mambas/ (Fetched).
- **Wits 2025**: Wits University news, "Africa's most feared snake can help to sniff out pollution" (19 Aug 2025), quoting herpetologist Graham Alexander and rescuer Nick Evans: https://www.wits.ac.za/news/latest-news/research-news/2025/2025-08/africas-most-feared-snake-can-help-to-sniff-out-pollution.html (Fetched).
- **WP-BM**: Wikipedia, "Black mamba": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_mamba (Fetched, raw wikitext searched; Secondary).
- **WP-Mamba**: Wikipedia, "Mamba": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamba (Fetched, raw wikitext; Secondary).

## FLAGGED (screener should look first)
1. **Title claim "the snake Africa fears most".** Supported two ways: WP-BM, citing Hodgson & Davidson 1996, *Wilderness and Environmental Medicine* 7(2):133-145: "The black mamba is the most feared snake in Africa because of its size, aggression, venom toxicity and speed of onset of symptoms". Wits 2025 headline: "Africa's most feared snake..." and body: "Africa's fastest and most feared snakes". I could not read the Hodgson & Davidson paper itself (no abstract online). The script never says it.
2. **Speed.** The brief's "11 km/h measured" figure traces only to a Wikipedia talk-page note about an unnamed German source, so it is **not used**. The script uses the ceiling: WP-BM "cannot move more quickly than 20 km/h", citing ADW and Spawls et al. 2017, *A Field Guide to the Reptiles of East Africa*; ADW "up to 20 km/h". The WP-BM lead also says "up to 16 km/h for short distances" (uncited), consistent with "cannot move faster than about twenty".
3. **"Most apparent chases"** is from WP-Mamba (cites *The New Encyclopedia of Reptiles (Serpent)*, Time Book 2002): "Most apparent cases of pursuit are likely examples of witnesses mistaking the snake's attempt to retreat to its lair when a human happens to be in the way." Backed by ADW: aggression "usually results when an intruder blocks the snake's direct path to its home or refuge". The script says "probably", matching "likely".
4. **Bite circumstances** are sourced to a snake rescuer quoted by a university, not to a clinical study (I found no clinical paper giving bite circumstances for mambas specifically). Worded as his statement, not as advice.
5. **Benin delay figures** are for all snakebites at one hospital (savannah, northern Benin), not black mamba bites. The script says "snakebite patients", not mamba patients.
6. **Before-1962 antivenom figures** (15 of 35; 5 of 38) come from WP-BM citing Christensen 1981, *South African Medical Journal*, "Snakebite and the use of antivenom in southern Africa". Primary not read.

| Claim | Source |

| First dendrotoxin isolated from green mamba venom in 1980 | Harvey 2001, "Twenty years of dendrotoxins", *Toxicon*, PMID 10936620 (abstract Fetched): isolated "20 years ago" from mamba venoms, citing Harvey & Karlsson 1980, green mamba *D. angusticeps* |
