Deaths go **344 to 350** (7 deaths; items 3 and 8 are survivals). Nine items, the most extreme last.

### It leaves.
**1. The Speed** (one of the three strongest: the cold-open item)
- Facts to research: the measured top speed of a black mamba (find the primary or best secondary source; the often-quoted 11 km/h measurement
  and the inflated 20+ km/h claims, and which is which). Legends of mambas "chasing" people or horses (cite a herpetologist or snake-rescue source
  that calls this a myth). The actual behaviour: when disturbed it heads for its retreat, and a person standing between the snake and its hole
  looks like the target (cite a herpetologist such as Branch, Marais, or a reputable snake-rescue organisation; word it as they do).
- **Cold-open fact (sourced):** "A black mamba that seems to be chasing you is usually trying to get home, and you are standing in front of the
  door." Exact wording to follow the source.
- **Twist (by 0:45):** it isn't chasing Doug at all. Doug is in the way. The speed number is real but slower than the legend; the problem is where
  Doug is standing.
- Scale analogy: the measured speed against something everyday (a brisk jog, a child on a scooter; pick the one that matches the sourced figure;
  do **not** reuse 010's "relaxed bike ride").
- Doug beat: Doug sprints away in a straight line, proud of his speed, straight toward the termite mound the snake is also racing to. They arrive
  together. Death 344, cut to black. "Doug did not agree to this." here, once.

**2. The Home**
- Facts to research: black mambas use a fixed retreat (termite mounds, hollow trees, rock crevices, sometimes roof spaces of houses) and come
  back to it for long periods (cite a radio-tracking or field study, or a field guide; give the duration only as the source does). Basking
  near the refuge in the morning. Snake-rescue removals from houses in KwaZulu-Natal (e.g. a rescuer such as Nick Evans; one sourced example,
  no named victims).
- Scale analogy: the mamba treats one termite mound the way a cat treats one sunny window: same spot, every morning, for months.
- Doug beat: Doug decides the termite mound is the perfect place to put up his tent. The field guide box "IT'S TRYING TO LEAVE" gets crossed out
  by Doug. The mamba comes home. Death 345.

**3. The Snake Eagle** (survival)
- Facts to research: who eats black mambas. Brown snake eagles and other snake eagles take mambas (cite a raptor study or handbook); mongooses
  and honey badgers are partly resistant to some snake venoms (cite the receptor-mutation work, e.g. Drabeck et al. 2015; one line only, keep the
  honey badger to a single mention for #75 spacing). Juvenile mambas are taken by many predators.
- **Twist:** the most feared snake in Africa spends part of its life as somebody else's lunch.
- Scale analogy: a brown snake eagle carrying off a mamba is like a man carrying a garden hose that is still arguing.
- Doug beat: Doug in a brown snake eagle costume, cap on top, "to make the snake respect him". The mamba respects the costume and leaves. **Survival:
  "Doug is fine."** The narrator notes the eagle costume is now worth more than Doug.

### It warns.
**4. The Head Lift** (WARNINGS LEFT 3 to 2)
- Facts to research: when cornered, a black mamba raises the front part of its body off the ground (sourced fraction or height; the often-quoted
  "a third of its length" or "eye level with a person" only if a source says it). It may strike from that raised posture at a distance (sourced
  strike range if available; otherwise cut).
- Scale analogy: a 2.5 m snake lifting a third of its body is a head at the height of a kitchen counter (recompute from the sourced figure).
- Doug beat: Doug lifts himself too, on tiptoe, "to look taller". He is now eye to eye. Death 346.

**5. The Gape and the Hood** (WARNINGS LEFT 2 to 1)
- Facts to research: the gape is spent in 010, so **one callback line only**: "Doug has seen the inside of this mouth before." New facts: it
  also spreads a narrow hood (much narrower than a cobra's) and hisses (sourced); the mamba is not a cobra but is in the same family, Elapidae
  (sourced).
- **Twist:** the hood is the closest the mamba gets to a cobra, and it means the same thing.
- Scale analogy: a cobra's hood is an umbrella; the mamba's is a folded one, but nobody should stand under either.
- Doug beat: Doug is grey-painted again out of habit (010 callback, visual only, no NOT A MOUSE sign). He hisses back. Death 347.

**6. The Second Strike** (WARNINGS LEFT 1 to 0, red)
- Facts to research: black mambas can strike repeatedly in quick succession and each bite can deliver venom (sourced); venom yield per bite
  (mg, sourced range); most bites to people happen when someone tries to catch, corner or kill the snake (cite a clinical or epidemiological
  study from southern or eastern Africa).
- **Twist:** the last warning is the one you don't see, because the first strike is already over.
- Scale analogy: the strike is faster than a blink (only if a source gives a strike speed or duration; otherwise compare it to a dropped phone you
  never catch).
- Doug beat: Doug holds a stick "to keep it at a safe distance". This is the exact thing the bite statistics warn about. Death 348, played flat:
  quick cut to black, no strike shown.

### It strikes.
**7. The Dendrotoxin** (WARNINGS LEFT 0)
- Facts to research: the genus name *Dendroaspis* ("tree asp"). Black mamba venom contains **dendrotoxins**, which block potassium channels in
  nerves and cause nerves to over-fire, plus three-finger toxins, including fasciculins that block acetylcholinesterase (cite a review, e.g.
  Harvey & Robertson 2004 on dendrotoxins, Laustsen et al. 2015 on the black mamba venome). Explain at the level of "messages between nerve and
  muscle get jammed". **No symptom list, no clinical course, no timeline of dying.**
- **Twist:** the toxin is so precise that neuroscientists use it as a lab tool to switch off one type of channel (cite).
- Scale analogy: a nerve is a doorbell; dendrotoxin glues the button down.
- Doug beat: Doug zoomed in to a cartoon nerve diagram (MS Paint wires, a doorbell icon). Doug tries to unglue the doorbell. Death 349, cut to the
  counter.

**8. The Painkiller** (survival)
- Facts to research: **mambalgins**, peptides from black mamba venom that block acid-sensing ion channels and reduced pain in mice about as well as
  morphine without some of its side effects (Diochot et al. 2012, *Nature*). Early research only; say it plainly: not a medicine, not in people.
- **Twist:** the same venom that shuts down nerves contains something that switches pain off.
- Scale analogy: like finding a first-aid kit inside a fire alarm.
- Doug beat: Doug reads the paper and decides he'll be fine. He is fine, for once: he never gets near a snake in this item, he is in a library.
  **Survival.** The narrator is quietly disappointed (no "Doug is fine." here, it's used at item 3). The narration states clearly that this is
  research in mice and not treatment. **No medical advice** of any kind.

**9. The Long Way to the Hospital** (the payoff promise, the boss)
- Facts to research: before antivenom, black mamba bites were reported as close to always fatal (010 said "almost always fatal", so give a new
  angle: the sourced historical figure or who produced the first effective antivenom and when, e.g. the South African Institute for Medical
  Research / now the South African Vaccine Producers polyvalent antivenom; cite). With antivenom, most people survive (sourced). Today the danger
  is distance and access: rural snakebite victims in sub-Saharan Africa often reach care hours late, and antivenom is scarce and expensive (cite
  WHO snakebite fact sheet, Chippaux 2011 or a recent Lancet / PLoS NTD paper). **Honesty line:** the mamba avoids people; most bites follow an
  attempt to handle or kill it (same source as item 6).
- Scale analogy: the venom works on the clock of a lunch break (010's "half an hour" stays spent; use only a new sourced figure, e.g. distance in
  km or hours to the nearest clinic), and the hospital is a day trip away.
- Doug beat: Doug, far out in the bush with a bicycle and a flat tyre, after ignoring every warning on the chalkboard. The map shows the clinic
  as a dot very far away. Death 350, played flat: cut to the bicycle wheel still turning, then black. **No first aid, no what-to-do steps, no
  depiction of treatment.**
- Closing: the termite mound at dawn, the mamba basking at its door (item 2 callback), the cap on top of the mound. The field guide sticky note
  "LET IT LEAVE." Kicker idea: "It gave Doug three warnings and a head start. Doug used all of them on something else." Outro 30 words or fewer:
  death counter plus one CTA.

## 7. Risks
- **Gore:** no bite wounds, no fang close-ups with blood, no swelling, no symptom imagery. Every death is a cut to black, X eyes, or the cap
  left behind. Items 6 and 9 show no strike at all.
- **Medical / safety advice (never approve):** no first aid, no "what to do if bitten", no "stand still / run in a zigzag" advice, no claims
  about tourniquets, no handling tips. Mambalgins are described as mouse research only. Item 6's "most bites happen when people try to catch or kill
  it" is a statistic, not advice; keep it phrased as a fact about the record.
- **Kid appeal:** medium. Snakes are popular with children. No cartoon-cute snake face, no googly eyes, no "snek" meme language, no nursery
  tone. Jokes stay dry and adult-coded.
- **Myths to avoid:** "it chases people" (debunk), "fastest snake in the world at 20 km/h+" (use only the sourced measurement), "kills in 20
  minutes" or any dramatic time-to-death figure, "the black mamba is black" (spent in 010), "the honey badger is immune" (it is partly resistant;
  say what the source says).
- **Facts that need strong sources:**
  - Measured speed, and the source calling the chasing story a myth.
  - Retreat-site fidelity (field study or handbook).
  - Predators: snake eagles; mongoose/honey badger receptor resistance (Drabeck et al. 2015 or equivalent).
  - Head lift posture, hood and hiss; Elapidae.
  - Repeated strikes and venom yield; bite circumstances (clinical/epidemiological study).
  - Dendrotoxin and fasciculin mechanism; lab-tool use.
  - Mambalgins (Diochot et al. 2012).
  - Antivenom history and producer; survival with treatment; access/distance problem (WHO, Chippaux, or a recent peer-reviewed paper).
  - The "Africa fears most" claim, if it stays in the title.
- **Pronunciation (editor):** *Dendroaspis polylepis* "den-droh-ASP-iss poh-lee-LEP-iss", dendrotoxin "DEN-droh-TOX-in", fasciculin
  "fa-SICK-yoo-lin", mambalgin "mam-BAL-jin", Elapidae "eh-LAP-ih-dee", KwaZulu-Natal "kwah-ZOO-loo nuh-TAHL".
