Off the Galápagos, scientists watched dolphins attack sperm whales about ten times their weight.

By the end, one swims straight at Doug carrying more than thirty kilos of tuna, and lets go.

Their fight.

## The Sperm Whales

The Sperm Whales.

[Date card: ABOUT 1 KM. Splashes and fins only around a tight group of sperm whales. No wounds, no blood.]

The attackers were false killer whales, and despite the name, they are not orcas at all. They are dolphins, among the largest members of the dolphin family, and they got their name because their skull looks like a killer whale's.

A big male can reach six meters and weigh about one and a third tonnes. A female sperm whale can be about twelve meters long, the length of a city bus, and weigh around thirteen tonnes.

Two researchers published the encounter in nineteen ninety-six, and the word they put in the title was attack.

Here's the strange part. Nobody could say what the attack was actually for, so the researchers laid out three options: hunting, competing for food, or simple robbery. Since then, false killer whales have been recorded harassing sperm whales and then eating the squid that the sperm whales threw up.

There is no evidence that any sperm whale died. They were not after the whale. They were after its lunch.

[Field guide FALSE KILLER WHALE page. FRIEND? box empty.]

Doug watched from a small boat, decided that the big ones needed help, and paddled straight into the middle of it. Doug did not agree to this. He agreed to the boat.

[Doug paddles into the circle. A sperm whale tail rises above him. Cut. Doug flat on the water, X eyes, cap floating beside him. DOUG DEATHS: 363. The narrator's hand writes "NO" in the FRIEND? box.]

The last sperm whale Doug met decided he was not food. These ones were too busy to decide anything at all, and the dolphins never noticed him, because they were saving their attention for other dolphins.

## The Dolphin Friends

The Dolphin Friends.

[Date card: ABOUT 500 M. Map of New Zealand's North Island, northeast coast highlighted.]

Off northeastern New Zealand, false killer whales were seen travelling with bottlenose dolphins in more than nine out of every ten encounters.

Researchers spent seventeen years photographing them there, and every false killer whale they identified turned out to belong to one connected social network. Some of the same dolphins kept turning up with the same whales for more than five years, in places up to six hundred and fifty kilometers apart. That is five years of the same carpool, and nobody has asked to be dropped off early. The two species even hunt side by side.

But that's not the whole story. Off Cocos Island in Costa Rica, researchers watched false killer whales aggressively chasing bottlenose dolphins, the very same species. And in the nineteen seventies, observers on tuna boats reported false killer whales attacking dolphins as they were let out of the nets.

Robin Baird, who has studied these whales off Hawaii for decades, points out that there is still no solid evidence they eat other dolphins. Chasing a neighbour is not the same thing as eating one.

So it is the same whale and the same dolphin, with a friendship in one ocean and a chase in the other.

[Doug in a bottlenose dolphin costume, cap on top, bobbing hopefully beside a sign reading COCOS ISLAND.]

Doug dressed as a bottlenose dolphin, because in New Zealand that is the best friend a false killer whale has. He picked the right dolphin. He picked the wrong ocean.

[Fins circle. Doug swims away very fast, then slower, then stops. The costume floats flat, X eyes, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 364.]

Doug kept up with the chase for eleven seconds. The whales never wanted him in the first place, because what they really wanted was fish.

Their dinner.

## The Swordfish

The Swordfish.

[Date card: ABOUT 200 M. Two dark backs at the surface with a long grey fish sandwiched between them, bill underwater. A seabird hovers.]

In July twenty twenty-four, off the Hawaiian island of Lanai, a photographer caught two false killer whales with a broadbill swordfish about three meters long pinned between them.

That swordfish was longer than a ping-pong table, and it still was not too big for them.

Around the main Hawaiian Islands, scientists have watched them take ten different kinds of ocean fish, including yellowfin tuna, albacore, mahi-mahi, wahoo and swordfish. Around the world, they have been recorded eating forty species of fish and twenty-two kinds of squid and octopus.

They do not only hunt at the surface, either. Satellite tags on Hawaiian false killer whales have recorded dives deeper than twelve hundred meters, and prey caught down there gets carried back up to the surface. Every so often, one of them hurls a fish clear of the water.

The unsettling part is the menu itself. Tuna, swordfish, mahi-mahi and wahoo are the same fish that people catch, sell and order for dinner, so this whale is not mainly competing with sharks for its food. It is competing with us.

[Doug in the water, a measuring tape stretched out, labelled BAIT FOR SCALE.]

Doug was in the water as bait for scale, holding out a measuring tape so that viewers could compare him with the swordfish.

[The swordfish arrives first, bill-first, from off-screen. Cut. Doug flat, X eyes, the tape still unreeling. DOUG DEATHS: 365. Then the two whales arrive.]

The swordfish arrived first and the whales arrived second. Doug never arrived at a measurement.

And because people and false killer whales want the same fish, sooner or later they meet on the same line.

## The Line

The Line.

[Date card: ABOUT 100 M. A fishing boat with a main line running off the edge of the frame, floats and hooks dotted along it.]

In Hawaii, no whale or dolphin gets caught by accident on tuna longline hooks more often than the false killer whale.

A single deep-set line runs for about forty-five to eighty kilometers, which is longer than a marathon. Weighted branch lines hang off it, each one ending in a baited hook, and one boat can put out around three thousand hooks in a single set.

To a false killer whale, that is a long row of fish that cannot swim away. They take bait and hooked fish straight off the lines, and fishermen have a word for it, which is depredation.

[Doug on deck counting hooks. He hauls in a line: a clean outline drawing of a fish head on the hook, nothing else. Again. Again.]

Doug was on the boat counting hooks, and every tuna he pulled up was only a head, so he leaned over the side to see who was doing it.

[The line snaps tight. Doug goes over the rail. Splash. The cap floats. DOUG DEATHS: 366.]

Here's the problem. When a whale takes a fish off a hook, it can end up hooked or tangled itself, and some of them are badly injured or killed. Around the main Hawaiian Islands, only about one hundred and thirty-nine of these whales were counted in twenty twenty-two, and that population has been listed as endangered since twenty twelve.

[Sober card: "ABOUT 139 LEFT". No Doug, no joke.]

On this line, the danger mostly runs the other way.

Their family.

## The Share

The Share.

[Date card: ABOUT 50 M. Several dorsal fins converging on one whale holding a mahi-mahi.]

When one false killer whale catches a big fish, the others usually converge on it, and they share.

In Hawaii, observers have watched a catch get passed from whale to whale, sometimes before anyone takes a bite. Even fish caught deep down get carried up to the surface to be divided. Robin Baird thinks the passing is about trust, a way for a group of hunters to keep their bonds strong.

It goes round the pod like a pizza box at an office party, except that here nobody takes the last slice and walks off with it.

Hunting as a team and splitting the kill are the orca half of this story. The other half is about who else gets offered a piece.

But here's the twist. The sharing does not always stop at the edge of the pod. In Hawaii and elsewhere, false killer whales have been seen passing caught fish to people, both on boats and in the water.

[Doug in a mahi-mahi costume, cap on top, floating between two whales.]

Doug was zipped into a mahi-mahi costume, because the pod treats a mahi-mahi as something to share, and Doug badly wanted to be included.

[He is passed nose to nose from whale to whale down the line. Cut. The costume floats flat, X eyes, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 367.]

He was passed all the way down the line, so that every whale could have a go. Doug has died. Again. But he was included.

## The Pod

The Pod.

[Date card: ABOUT 20 M. A photo-ID board of dorsal fins, each with a small number tag.]

In Hawaii, researchers have photographed the same false killer whale more than twenty years after they first saw it, and found pairs of whales still together after fifteen years.

These are not passing groups. The whales around the main Hawaiian Islands belong to a handful of social clusters, and one cluster can hold forty or fifty animals. Imagine a family reunion that never ends, where the same relatives sit at the same tables for the rest of their lives.

They also live a long time. Females can reach their early sixties, males have been aged at up to fifty-eight, and a mother usually waits six or seven years between calves.

The odd part is what happens in the middle of that long life. Females stop having calves in their early forties, and then keep living for roughly another twenty years. They are still in the group, still part of the hunt, and still part of the share.

[Doug in a dinghy, waving. Bubble: "hi friends?". The pod passes without turning.]

Doug waved at the pod from a dinghy and said hello, and not one whale looked at him.

Doug is fine. Of everything in the ocean, this is the only group that has never wanted anything from him.

Staying together is what keeps them alive at sea. On a beach, it works the other way.

## The Beach

The Beach.

[Sober card. Date card: 10 OCTOBER 1946, MAR DEL PLATA. An empty beach at dusk. No whales drawn. Doug stands on the sand, cap in his hands. No counter change.]

On the tenth of October, nineteen forty-six, an estimated eight hundred and thirty-five false killer whales stranded near Mar del Plata, in Argentina.

It is the largest mass stranding ever recorded for the species, and the groups that strand are often much bigger than the groups people see at sea. If that many animals were laid nose to tail, the line would stretch roughly four kilometers along the shore.

Strandings are also part of how science met this animal in the first place. The species was first described in eighteen forty-six, from a single old skull dug out of a fen near Stamford, in England. Living ones only came to the attention of science about fifteen years later, when one was caught near Kiel and several more turned up on the coast of Denmark.

The hardest part is that no one can fully explain why whole groups come ashore together. One idea is that when a sick or weakened whale heads into shallow water, the healthy ones follow it in rather than leave it behind. If that is right, then a mass stranding is a measure of how strong the bonds between them really are.

It remains an open question.

[Hold on the empty beach. Doug does not move.]

Doug's share.

## The Swimmer

The Swimmer.

[Date card: ABOUT 5 M. Doug snorkelling at the surface. A large dark shape rises beneath him.]

False killer whales have been documented offering fish to people, both to boaters and to divers in the water.

They often approach boats to ride the bow wave, and they tend to be openly curious about snorkelers and divers. That curiosity comes in a body that can weigh more than two concert grand pianos, and it likes to come in close.

In British Columbia and Washington, a lone false killer whale, far from where its kind usually lives, repeatedly caught large salmon and offered them to people on boats. In Hawaii, they have offered fish to snorkelers and divers too.

The weird part is how it made the offer. According to Baird's account, the whale would let the salmon go right beside the boat, and then grab it back as soon as it started to sink. It was a gift with a very short time limit.

[Doug in the water, mask on, cap over the strap. The whale surfaces in front of him with a salmon. It lets go. Doug looks at the fish. Doug looks at the narrator. The salmon sinks an inch. The whale takes it back and leaves.]

Doug was snorkelling when a whale showed him a salmon, and he hesitated for exactly as long as it takes a salmon to start sinking. The whale took it back.

Doug is fine.

The narrator had already drawn the death, and is now holding on to it. Next time, the whale doesn't take it back.

## The Offer

The Offer.

[Date card: 0 M. KONA, HAWAII, 1984. Clear blue water.]

In nineteen eighty-four, off Kona in Hawaii, a false killer whale swam straight at researcher Dan McSweeney with most of a yellowfin tuna in its mouth.

McSweeney was in the water, following a group, and the accounts of the fish differ. McSweeney remembered half of a much bigger tuna, around thirty-four kilos, while Robin Baird's book puts it at about forty-five. Either way, it was at least as heavy as a fully grown golden retriever, and it was moving at the speed of the whale that was carrying it.

According to Baird, the whale stopped a couple of meters away, opened its mouth, and let the fish go. The tuna kept coming, carried toward McSweeney by its own momentum.

And here's the part nobody expected. McSweeney pushed the fish back toward the whale, and the whale took it slowly, almost carefully. Then the group passed it around until every whale had a share.

Baird's own reading is that false killer whales may see humans as something like themselves. Nobody has proved it, and nobody can ask the whale. But the same animal that harasses sperm whales for their lunch tried to hand one of us a tuna.

[Doug in the water. The whale stops a couple of meters away and opens its mouth. Bubble: "for me?". The field guide's MAYBE is rubbed out.]

McSweeney pushed it back. Doug did not. Doug said thank you, and held out both arms.

[The tuna keeps coming. Cut. Doug flat on the water, X eyes, the tuna lying on top of him. The tuna drops onto DOUG'S PLATE, which is stamped red. DOUG DEATHS: 368.]

The whale meant well. More than thirty kilos of tuna, arriving at whale speed, also meant well.

[Closing: the pod swimming away. The red cap is passed from whale to whale, nose to nose, the first whale first. Field guide sticky note: "IT MEANT WELL."]

The cap has been shared. Everyone got a turn.
