### Sunlit.
**1. The Sharkcano** (the hook item; Kavachi, Solomon Islands, summit about 20-50 m, depth per source)
- Research: Kavachi is one of the most active submarine volcanoes in the Pacific and has breached the surface several times
  (Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program); a 2015 expedition lowered cameras into the crater between eruptions and filmed
  scalloped hammerheads, silky sharks and a sixgill stingray living inside it (Phillips et al. 2016, *Oceanography*,
  "Exploring the 'Sharkcano'"); the crater water was hot, acidic and full of sulphur and particles (as the paper states);
  NASA Earth Observatory images of later eruptions (2021-2022).
- Scale analogy: compare the summit depth with something everyday, such as how many school buses stacked on end (sourced depth).
- Twist (must land by 0:45, about 145 words): the sharks aren't passing through. They live **inside the crater of an
  erupting volcano**, and nobody knows how.
- Doug beat: Doug waves at a hammerhead ("hi friend?"); the hammerhead is fine with it. The volcano is not. Death 47:
  a cartoon burp of bubbles, then the cap pops up to the surface. **The sharks never hurt Doug**; we keep sharks
  non-villain (accurate, and it avoids shark-attack gore).

**2. The Underwater Crop Circles** (Doug survives)
- Research: off Amami-Oshima, Japan, divers found 2 m geometric circles in the sand at about 10-30 m from 1995 on; in 2011 the
  builder was found to be a male pufferfish of about 12 cm, described in 2014 as a new species, *Torquigener albomaculosus*
  (Kawase, Okata & Ito 2013, *Scientific Reports*; Matsuura 2014, *Ichthyological Research*); it builds the circle with its
  fins over about a week and females choose the male by it (as the paper states).
- Scale analogy: a 12 cm fish (about the length of a phone) builds a structure about the width of a double bed (sourced figures).
- Twist: the alien mystery was a fish trying to get a date. And the fish usually builds a new one each time.
- Doug beat: Doug builds his own circle next to it, lopsided. Nobody comes. He survives, and the narrator says "Doug is
  used to this." Counter stays at 47. **Adult-coded dating joke, never cute.** No pufferfish eating, and no toxin jokes that
  amount to food advice.

**3. The Bottomless Blue Hole** (Taam Ja', Chetumal Bay, Mexico, at least 420 m)
- Research: Taam Ja' was first measured at about 274 m in 2021 (Alcérreca-Huerta et al. 2023, *Frontiers in Marine Science*);
  a 2023 dive with a CTD instrument reached 420 m below sea level **without finding the bottom**, making it the deepest
  known blue hole (Alcérreca-Huerta et al. 2024, same journal); the previous record holder, the Sansha Yongle "Dragon Hole"
  in the South China Sea (about 300 m), has no oxygen below about 100 m and hydrogen sulphide in its deep water (sourced,
  for example Xie et al. 2019 or Yao et al. 2020).
- Scale analogy: 420 m against the height of the Empire State Building (library `empire_state_building.json`; use the
  sourced height).
- Twist: the instrument's cable ran out before the hole did. **Nobody knows how deep it goes** (stated as the paper states it,
  plus the possible link to caves under the sea).
- Doug beat: Doug jumps in with a snorkel and a flashlight to "find the bottom". The narrator mentions the no-oxygen layer at
  the Dragon Hole, which suggests the deep water of blue holes can be toxic (as sourced). Death 48: the flashlight beam goes
  down and down; the cap floats up. **Nothing about breathing or symptoms; no cave-diving content; no real diver deaths.**

### Pitch black.
**4. The Lost City** (Atlantis Massif, mid-Atlantic, about 700-800 m)
- Research: found by accident in December 2000 during a research cruise (Kelley et al. 2001, *Nature*); white carbonate
  chimneys, the tallest ("Poseidon") about 60 m; fed not by magma but by seawater reacting with mantle rock
  (serpentinisation), which releases hydrogen and methane; active for at least about 120,000 years (Früh-Green et al. 2003,
  *Science*); a candidate model for where life on Earth began (sourced framing, NOAA Ocean Exploration / University of
  Washington).
- Scale analogy: Poseidon at about 60 m is about as tall as a 20-storey building (sourced arithmetic).
- Twist: the ocean floor is **breathing out the ingredients of life** from rock, without sunlight and without a volcano, and it
  has done so since before modern humans left Africa (date comparison only if sourced).
- Doug beat: Doug climbs Poseidon to plant a flag. The chimney is carbonate, which is the stuff of chalk and limestone (sourced).
  It breaks. Death 49. Cartoon only.

**5. The Bloop** (the SOFAR channel, about 600-1,200 m, where the sound travelled)
- Research: in 1997 NOAA's hydrophones in the Pacific recorded an extremely loud, ultra-low-frequency sound, heard on sensors
  about 5,000 km apart; it was nicknamed "the Bloop" and sparked sea-monster theories; NOAA later concluded it matched the sound
  of a large icequake, an iceberg cracking off Antarctica (NOAA PMEL Acoustics Program, "Bloop"); the SOFAR channel, a
  layer where sound travels thousands of kilometres (NOAA).
- Scale analogy: a sound heard on sensors 5,000 km apart is like a shout in New York heard in London-plus (sourced distance comparison).
- Twist: the monster was ice. **But here's the problem:** NOAA has another sound, "Upsweep", recorded since 1991, which
  rises and falls with the seasons, and whose source is still not firmly identified (NOAA PMEL: possibly volcanic). This
  keeps the "unexplained" promise honestly.
- Optional line, **only if sourced**: the Bloop's location relative to the fictional sunken city in H. P. Lovecraft's
  stories. Cut it if no reliable source compares the two.
- Doug beat: Doug goes looking for the monster with a fishing rod (`fishing_rod.json`) and stands on the ice to cast.
  The ice makes the Bloop. Death 50: Doug and a slab of ice slide into the sea; the cap is left on the berg.

**6. The Jacuzzi of Despair** (Gulf of Mexico, about 1,000 m; the halfway mark)
- Research: a brine pool on the seafloor, found and filmed by a Temple University team led by Erik Cordes (about 2015;
  sourced year, size and depth); the brine is several times saltier than seawater, warmer than the water around it and full of
  methane and hydrogen sulphide; animals that swim or fall in are killed and preserved, "pickled" (as sourced); a lake **at the
  bottom of the sea**, with its own shoreline and surface waves (NOAA Ocean Exploration). Optional: the 2020 Red Sea brine
  pools at about 1,770 m, which preserve a record of past tsunamis and floods (Purkis et al. 2023, *Communications Earth &
  Environment*).
- Scale analogy: the pool's size against a swimming pool (`swimming_pool.json`) or a tennis court (sourced dimensions).
- Twist: crabs and eels hang around the edge, waiting for something to fall in (as sourced).
- Doug beat: Doug thinks it's a hot tub (`hot_tub.json` callback image), says "nice", and gets in. Death 51: the cap floats
  on the brine surface, perfectly preserved. **No description of what pickling does to a body.** One line at most: "anything
  that falls in stays there, looking exactly as it did."

**7. The Champagne Vent** (NW Eifuku volcano, Mariana arc, about 1,600 m)
- Research: a vent that releases droplets of **liquid** carbon dioxide that rise like champagne bubbles, found in 2004 (Lupton
  et al. 2006, *Geochemistry, Geophysics, Geosystems*; NOAA Ocean Exploration); at this depth and temperature CO2 is liquid
  and forms an ice-like hydrate skin on each droplet (sourced); related: pools of liquid CO2 trapped under the seafloor in the
  Okinawa Trough, capped by hydrate (Inagaki et al. 2006, *PNAS*).
- Scale analogy: the pressure at 1,600 m is about 160 times the pressure at the surface (sourced arithmetic), like a car parked on
  every thumbnail-sized patch of skin (do that math from the source; cut the image if it doesn't work).
- Twist: there are **lakes of liquid carbon dioxide** under the seafloor, sealed by a crust of something like ice.
- Doug beat: Doug brings two glasses and toasts the vent ("cheers"). The vent water is very acidic (as sourced). Death 52: a
  cartoon fizz, X eyes, the cap drifts upward with the bubbles. **No acid-on-skin description.**

**8. The Scaly-Foot Snail** (Indian Ocean vents, about 2,400-2,900 m)
- Research: a snail that builds armour from iron sulphide minerals (greigite and pyrite) on its foot and shell, found in
  2001 at the Kairei vent field (Warén et al. 2003, *Science*; Chen et al. 2015, *Journal of Molluscan Studies*); it lives
  next to black smokers that release water at about 360 °C (sourced figure for Kairei); in 2019 it became **the first animal
  listed as Endangered because of deep-sea mining** (IUCN Red List, Sigwart et al. 2019, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*).
- Scale analogy: the snail is about the size of a coin (sourced shell width); 360 °C water is hotter than a pizza oven (sourced
  oven temperature).
- Twist: the only animal known to wear iron, and mining licences already cover its home (state the IUCN reasoning, neutrally).
- Doug beat: Doug tapes a saucepan to his chest to "match" the snail and befriends it ("hi friend?"). Iron armour does not
  help with 360 °C water. Death 53: a cartoon steam puff, the saucepan clatters onto the vent, the snail is fine. **No burn
  description.**

### Rock bottom.
**9. The Asphalt Volcano** (Chapopote, southern Gulf of Mexico, about 2,900-3,000 m)
- Research: discovered in 2003; instead of lava, it oozes **asphalt** that hardens on the seafloor, and tubeworms, clams and
  bacteria live on the tar (MacDonald et al. 2004, *Science*; MARUM, University of Bremen expeditions).
- Scale analogy: the asphalt flows against a car park or a road (sourced area, if available).
- Twist: life down there **eats the tar**, through bacteria that break down oil (as sourced).
- Doug beat: Doug walks out onto the shiny black flow like a fresh road. It is a fresh road. He sticks. Death 54: Doug ends
  up as a stick-figure imprint in the tar, with the cap beside it. Kicker: one dry line about how the tubeworms didn't notice.

**10. The Golden Orb** (Gulf of Alaska, about 3,300 m; Doug survives)
- Research: on 30 August 2023 NOAA's *Okeanos Explorer* filmed a smooth golden dome about 10 cm across, with a hole in it, on a
  rock at about 3,300 m; scientists on the live stream couldn't identify it ("egg casing?"); it was collected for lab analysis
  (NOAA Ocean Exploration, 2023). **Writer: check the latest status.** Later lab work reportedly linked it to a deep-sea
  anemone (*Relicanthus daphneae*). If that is confirmed by NOAA or the Smithsonian, it becomes the anticlimax twist. If it
  is still unresolved, it is the episode's second open mystery. Do not guess either way.
- Scale analogy: about the size of a grapefruit or a cereal bowl (sourced size).
- Twist: either "it was the leftover skin of an anemone" (if sourced) or "it is still unidentified" (if sourced).
- Doug beat: Doug pokes it with a stick, braced for the worst. Nothing happens. **"Doug is fine."** The narrator is quietly
  disappointed (the 001 gag). Counter stays at 54.

**11. Dark Oxygen** (Clarion-Clipperton Zone, Pacific, about 4,000 m)
- Research: a team measured oxygen **rising** in sealed chambers on the seafloor, in total darkness where no photosynthesis
  can happen, and linked it to potato-sized metal nodules that may split seawater with tiny electrical voltages (Sweetman et al.
  2024, *Nature Geoscience*); the nodules hold metals used in batteries and are a target for deep-sea mining; mining companies
  and some scientists dispute the finding, and follow-up studies are under way (sourced, for example *Nature* news coverage and
  published rebuttals).
- Scale analogy: the nodules are about the size of a potato (sourced); the zone is about the size of the continental United
  States or Europe (sourced area comparison).
- Twist: oxygen being made at the bottom of the ocean without sunlight goes against what we thought we knew, and nobody agrees yet
  on whether it is real. **State it as disputed, with both sides in one line each. No advocacy for or against mining.**
- Doug beat: Doug tries to charge his phone on a nodule (`phone.json`, `matchbox_charger.json` precedent). One bar. Then he
  tries to breathe the "dark oxygen" through his snorkel. It is not that much oxygen. Death 55: cartoon only, the snorkel floats
  up with the cap.

**12. The Deepest Ecosystem Ever Found** (the boss; Kuril-Kamchatka and Aleutian trenches, down to 9,533 m; the longest item)
- Research: in 2024 dives with the Chinese crewed submersible *Fendouzhe* found dense communities of tubeworms and clams living
  off methane and hydrogen sulphide made by microbes, along about 2,500 km of trench floor, down to 9,533 m, the deepest
  chemosynthetic communities ever recorded (Du et al. 2025, *Nature*); the pressure at that depth is roughly 950 times the
  surface pressure (sourced arithmetic); the methane is made by microbes from organic material in the sediment, not from
  volcanic heat (as the paper states).
- Scale analogy: 9,533 m is deeper than Mount Everest is tall (`mount_everest.json`; sourced heights); 2,500 km is about the
  distance from [sourced city pair].
- Twist: we assumed the deepest trenches were nearly empty. **They are lined with gardens** that live on gas, in the dark,
  and for thousands of kilometres. One honest line on how much of the trench floor is still unseen (sourced).
- Doug beat: the gauge needle hits 9,533 m. Doug steps out of the submersible into a field of tubeworms, delighted
  ("whoa"), and waves at a clam. The pressure has been patient all episode. Death 56. **Pressure death is a cartoon flatten
  (the 001 pancake), described in zero bodily words.**
- One callback line to 001 is allowed: "Doug has been this deep before. He brought back a plastic bag."
- **Final image (new; not a fossil cap like 003 and not a time-machine return like 005):** the surface ship's monitor shows the
  submersible's camera feed ("LIVE"). The tubeworms sway. The red cap drifts into frame and settles among them. A clam
  closes on it. Kicker: one dry line about the cap. Then the outro.

**Death plan:** the counter is on screen from the first shot at **46** and ends at **56** (10 deaths; survivals at item 2, the
crop circles, and item 10, the golden orb). By band: Sunlit 2, Pitch black 5, Rock bottom 3.
