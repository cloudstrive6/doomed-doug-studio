## The Underwater Crop Circles

## The Bottomless Blue Hole

The Bottomless Blue Hole.

From a boat in Chetumal Bay, on the coast of Mexico, it looks like a dark stain in the water. In the Maya language, its name, Taam Ja', means deep water.

[Depth gauge starts dropping. Top view of bright shallow water with a perfect dark-blue circle in the middle.]

A blue hole is an underwater sinkhole, a shaft dropping straight down through the seafloor. When this one was first measured in twenty twenty-one, it came out at about two hundred and seventy-five meters, which was already close to the world record.

The record holder back then was the Dragon Hole in the South China Sea, at about three hundred meters, and it's not a place anyone should want to visit. Below about ninety meters, its water has no oxygen at all, and the deeper part is full of hydrogen sulfide.

[Side view of the Dragon Hole. A line at 90 m. Label: "NO OXYGEN". Yellow warning triangle.]

## The Lost City

The Lost City.

Most deep-sea vents are black chimneys pouring out water heated by magma. This one is white, and nothing about it is volcanic.

[Depth gauge: "~750 M". Navy background. A field of tall, ghostly white towers.]

In December two thousand, scientists exploring an underwater mountain in the middle of the Atlantic stumbled onto a field of pale towers about seven hundred and fifty meters down. They called it the Lost City.

The towers are almost pure carbonate, the same material as limestone. The tallest one, named Poseidon, rises about sixty meters off the seafloor, as tall as a twenty-story building.

[Poseidon next to a crude twenty-story apartment block. Same height. Label: "60 M".]

Nothing down here is heated by a magma chamber. Instead, seawater soaks into rock from Earth's mantle and reacts with it, slowly turning one mineral into another. That reaction gives off heat, and it also releases hydrogen and methane, which the microbes living on the chimneys use as food.

[Arrows: seawater going down into the rock, then hydrogen and methane bubbling up. Tiny microbe doodles on the chimney.]

The strange part comes from dating the oldest chimneys, which shows the Lost City has been venting for about a hundred and twenty thousand years.

So the seafloor here has been making the raw ingredients of life out of plain rock, with no sunlight and no volcano, for well over a thousand centuries. Its discoverers think the first life on Earth may have started in a place a lot like this.

[Doug climbs Poseidon with a little flag. Hopeful smile. Label: "LIMESTONE".]

## The Bloop

The Bloop.

In the summer of nineteen ninety-seven, United States government hydrophones in the Pacific picked up a sound so powerful it reached sensors more than five thousand kilometers apart.

[Depth gauge: "~900 M". A map of the Pacific with listening stations as dots. A dotted line between two far-apart dots. Label: "5,000 KM".]

That's farther than New York is from Los Angeles.

The ocean helps with that. In the tropics, about a kilometer down, sound travels at its slowest, and low sounds get trapped in that layer like in a pipe, carrying for thousands of kilometers.

[A wavy sound line bouncing along a horizontal band. Label: "DEEP SOUND CHANNEL".]

The recording rose quickly in pitch and was several times louder than a blue whale, the loudest animal ever recorded. It was nicknamed the Bloop, and for a while even some of the researchers thought it was probably made by an animal.

[Black silhouette of a giant sea monster with a red glow. A big red "?".]

It wasn't. The monster was ice. The government's acoustics team later matched the Bloop to icequakes, the sound of giant icebergs cracking and breaking apart, most likely somewhere between the Antarctic Peninsula and the Ross Sea.

[The monster silhouette turns into an iceberg with a crack down the middle. Red X over the monster.]

But that's not the disturbing part. The same team has another sound on file, called the Upsweep, and it was already there when they started listening in August nineteen ninety-one.

It peaks in spring and autumn, and it comes from a remote part of the South Pacific, near an area of suspected volcanic activity. More than thirty years later, its origin is still officially unresolved.

[A spectrogram line sweeping upward. Label: "UPSWEEP: UNRESOLVED".]

[Doug stands on a big iceberg with a fishing rod, casting into the sea. Speech bubble: "here monster".]

## The Champagne Vent

The Champagne Vent.

Plenty of underwater vents bubble gas. In two thousand four, scientists found one in the western Pacific that bubbles liquid.

[Depth gauge: "~1,600 M". An underwater volcano slope. Streams of little round droplets rising from cracks.]

It sits on an underwater volcano called Northwest Eifuku, in the Mariana chain, about one thousand six hundred meters down. Streams of shiny droplets rise out of the seafloor like bubbles in a glass, so the team named it Champagne vent.

But they aren't bubbles at all, they're drops of liquid carbon dioxide.

[A single droplet zoomed in, with a thin crust around it. Label: "LIQUID CO2".]

At that depth, the pressure is about one hundred and sixty times what it is at the surface, roughly the weight of two grown men standing on every fingernail.

Under that much pressure, in water barely above freezing, carbon dioxide stays liquid, and each drop grows a thin skin of hydrate, an ice-like cage of water and carbon dioxide. The drops were so sticky that they clung to the robot like clumps of grapes.

[The robot arm covered in droplet clusters like grapes.]

And that's only the warm-up. In the Okinawa Trough near Japan, about one thousand three hundred and eighty meters down, scientists found an actual lake of liquid carbon dioxide trapped under the seafloor.

[Cross-section: mud on top, a crust layer labelled "HYDRATE", and a pool of liquid underneath. Label: "CO2 LAKE".]

It's sealed in by a lid of the same ice-like hydrate, and microbes live in the mud on top of it, sitting on a lake of something that should be a gas.

[Doug kneels by the vent with two champagne glasses, holding one into the stream of droplets. Speech bubble: "cheers".]

## The Scaly-Foot Snail

The Scaly-Foot Snail.

Snails are not famous for their armor. This one builds its own, out of iron.

[Depth gauge: "~2,800 M". A snail with a dark metallic shell and a foot covered in overlapping black scales like chain mail.]

It was discovered in two thousand one at the Kairei vent field in the Indian Ocean, almost three kilometers down. Its foot is covered in overlapping scales, and its shell and scales are coated in iron sulfide, the same family of minerals as fool's gold.

It's the only animal known to use iron as a major part of its skeleton. An adult shell is usually about three centimeters wide, a little bigger than a quarter.

[The snail next to a coin. Label: "IRON ARMOR".]

And it lives right beside black smokers, where the water pours out at around three hundred and sixty degrees Celsius, more than three times the boiling point of water at the surface.

[A black smoker chimney belching black water. Thermometer pinned at the top. Label: "360 °C".]

What nobody expected was where the real threat would come from. In twenty nineteen, it became the first animal ever listed as Endangered because of deep-sea mining.

It's known from only three vent fields in the whole ocean, and two of them sit inside mining exploration licenses, because the minerals around these vents contain valuable metals. The assessment warned that even exploratory mining could damage the vents or smother the snails in sediment.

[A map of the Indian Ocean with three dots. Two of them inside a dashed box labelled "EXPLORATION LICENSE".]

[Doug with a saucepan taped to his chest, crouched next to the snail. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

## The Asphalt Volcano

The Asphalt Volcano.

In two thousand three, scientists found a volcano in the southern Gulf of Mexico, about three thousand meters down. It erupts, but not with lava.

[Depth gauge: "~3,000 M". Near-black background. A mound on the seafloor with glossy black flows running down its sides.]

It's called Chapopote, and what comes out of it is asphalt, the same black, sticky stuff used to make roads. It oozes out of the seafloor, then cools and hardens into flows with wrinkles and ropes, so much like real lava that geologists compare the two directly. It sits on the rim of a buried salt dome, surrounded by oil seeps and patches of frozen gas hydrate.

The hardened asphalt covers more than a square kilometer, which is about a hundred and forty soccer fields of fresh tar.

[A grid of tiny soccer fields laid over the black flows. Label: "1 KM²".]

You would expect a tar field three kilometers down, in total darkness, to be completely dead. Instead, it was crowded.

Here's the part that should bother you. The tar itself is part of the menu.

[Tubeworms sprouting from cracks in the asphalt. Clams clustered nearby. Tiny microbe doodles inside the tar.]

Tubeworms grow out of cracks in the asphalt, with clams and other shellfish settled nearby. The asphalt samples are partly digested, and researchers found signs of oil-eating bacteria living right inside them, while other microbes turn the oil into methane gas.

Life down here isn't just surviving next to the tar. It's slowly eating it. And the team that found Chapopote thinks asphalt volcanoes like this one, with life like this on them, may be widespread deep in the Gulf.

[Doug walks out onto the shiny black flow, whistling. Hopeful smile. Label: "FRESH ROAD".]

Doug sees what looks like a freshly paved road and goes for a stroll. It is a freshly paved road. He sticks.

[A Doug-shaped imprint in the tar, arms out, with the red cap lying beside it. DOUG DEATHS: 54]

[Depth gauge: "~3,250 M". A rock in the dark with a smooth, shiny golden dome stuck to it. A torn hole in one side.]

## Dark Oxygen

Dark Oxygen.

Nearly all the oxygen made on Earth comes from living things using sunlight. Four thousand meters down in the Pacific, there is no sunlight at all.

[Depth gauge: "~4,000 M". A pitch-black seafloor scattered with lumpy black rocks.]

That's the floor of the Clarion-Clipperton Zone, a deep plain between Hawaii and Mexico that is about as wide as the continental United States. It is covered in metal lumps called nodules, most of them about the size of a potato.

[A nodule next to a potato. Same size. Label: "NODULE".]

They contain nickel, cobalt, copper and manganese, metals used in batteries, which makes this plain one of the biggest targets for deep-sea mining.

In twenty twenty-four, a team led by Andrew Sweetman reported something odd. Sealed chambers they placed on this seafloor kept showing oxygen going up, in total darkness.

They proposed that the nodules act like natural batteries. They measured up to about one volt on a single nodule, and argued that clusters of nodules could split seawater into hydrogen and oxygen.

[A nodule with a battery symbol on it. Little bubbles labelled "O2" rising. Label: "GEOBATTERY?".]

The catch is that nobody agrees it's real.

A mining company and several independent researchers published rebuttals arguing the rising oxygen came from the equipment, not the nodules. The original team has since said the mechanism is still unclear, and in April twenty twenty-six the journal added a note saying its editors are considering concerns about the paper.

[A split screen: scientists on one side with "REAL", critics on the other with "ARTIFACT". A big red "?" in the middle.]

For now, both sides want more measurements before anyone calls it settled.

[Doug sits on the seafloor with his phone plugged into a nodule. The battery icon shows one bar. Speech bubble: "one bar".]

Doug tries to charge his phone on a nodule. One bar. Then he tries to breathe the dark oxygen through his snorkel.

[The snorkel floats up with the cap. DOUG DEATHS: 55]

## The Deepest Ecosystem Ever Found

The Deepest Ecosystem Ever Found.

At the bottom of the deepest trenches there is no light, very little food drifts down from above, and the pressure is extreme. You would expect almost nothing to live there.

[Depth gauge starts dropping fast, past "5,000 M", "7,000 M". Black background. A small submersible with a single headlight sinking.]

Between July and August twenty twenty-four, the Chinese crewed submersible Fendouzhe made more than twenty dives into the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench and the western Aleutian Trench, in the northwest Pacific between Russia and Alaska.

What the crew found was not empty.

[The headlight sweeps across a dense field of thin tubeworms and clusters of clams. Label: "THOUSANDS".]

The trench floor was covered in dense fields of tubeworms and clams, and the crew kept finding them along about two thousand five hundred kilometers of trench, roughly the distance from London to Moscow. The communities ran from five thousand eight hundred meters all the way down to nine thousand five hundred and thirty-three meters.

That's deeper than Mount Everest is tall, with almost seven hundred meters to spare.

[Mount Everest upside down inside the trench, its peak stopping short of the tubeworms. Label: "9,533 M".]

The pressure at the bottom is roughly nine hundred and fifty times what it is at the surface. That's close to a tonne on every square centimeter, about the weight of a small car on every fingernail.

None of this runs on sunlight. Deep in the mud, microbes turn the remains of dead organic matter into methane. That methane, along with hydrogen sulfide, seeps up through faults in the trench floor, and bacteria living inside the tubeworms and clams turn those chemicals into food.

[Arrows from deep mud up through a crack in the trench floor, into the tubeworms. Labels: "METHANE" and "NO SUN".]

The unsettling part is how much of this we haven't seen. These are the deepest communities of their kind ever recorded, and the team thinks similar ones may be common in other trenches too, in places nobody has looked.
