## Movile Cave

Movile Cave.

[Map of Romania, red dot near the Black Sea coast. A drilling rig on a flat field. Under it, layers of clay and limestone, and a small cave with a lake. DOUG DEPTH: 18 m.]

In nineteen eighty-six, workers drilling near the Black Sea coast of Romania broke into a cave that had no entrance at all.

It sits under about eighteen meters of clay and limestone, and today the only way in is down a narrow shaft with an airtight gate on top.

[Doug climbs down the shaft. The gate clangs shut above him.]

Inside, there is a warm underground lake, and floating on it is a thick layer of bacterial scum. Nothing down here runs on sunlight. The bacteria live on chemicals that seep up through the water, and everything else in the cave eats the bacteria.

Here's the problem. The air itself is wrong, with about a hundred times more carbon dioxide than the air outside, plus methane and hydrogen sulfide, and in places only a third of the normal oxygen.

[Label: "100x CO2". A thermometer-style gauge for oxygen, needle low.]

And the animals breathing it have been cut off for a very long time. Scientists estimate this little world has been sealed away for somewhere between two and a half and five and a half million years. Even the low end is about eight times longer than our own species has existed.

## Lechuguilla Cave

Lechuguilla Cave.

[Map of New Mexico, red dot in Carlsbad Caverns National Park. A small pit in the desert with rubble at the bottom. DOUG DEPTH: 489 m.]

For decades, Lechuguilla was a dull hole in the New Mexico desert, just a short pit with a little dead-end passage and a pile of rubble.

Then cavers got permission to dig. In May nineteen eighty-six, they broke through the rubble into a cave that simply did not stop.

[The rubble falls away. Passages branch out in every direction across the screen.]

Since then, explorers have mapped more than two hundred and thirty kilometers of passages, more than five marathons laid end to end, in total darkness. The deepest point is four hundred and eighty-nine meters down.

It is also one of the most beautiful caves ever found, with gypsum formations called chandeliers that hang up to six meters long. Today, only approved science and survey teams are allowed in.

[Sober card, no Doug: a plain dark panel with a stretcher icon. Text: "1991. Rescued. Survived."]

But that's not the disturbing part. Scientists collected bacteria from a part of the cave cut off for more than four million years, long before anyone made a single medicine. Some of those bacteria were already resistant to as many as fourteen different antibiotics.

## The Devil Worm

The Devil Worm.

[Map of South Africa, red dot on the Beatrix gold mine. A mine shaft dropping into rock. DOUG DEPTH: 1,300 m.]

In two thousand eleven, scientists announced an animal living somewhere animals were not supposed to be, deep inside a gold mine in South Africa.

About one point three kilometers down, the height of roughly four Eiffel Towers stacked on top of each other, water trickles out of cracks in the rock.

[Four Eiffel Towers stacked beside the shaft. Dotted distance line: "1.3 KM".]

Carbon dating suggests this water had been cut off from the surface for somewhere between three thousand and twelve thousand years. Researchers filtered it, expecting bacteria. They found a worm.

It's a nematode, a kind of roundworm, and it grazes on the bacteria that live in the cracks. It copes with heat that would trouble most worms, and it can reproduce without a mate. It was the first multicellular creature ever found at that kind of depth.

They named it Halicephalobus mephisto, after Mephistopheles, the devil from the old Faust legend.

[A dark silhouette with a red glow fills the frame. Then the full reveal: the worm is tiny, next to a credit card edge.]

Here's the twist. The devil is half a millimeter long, which means a credit card is thicker than this worm is long.

[Label: "0.5 MM".]

[Doug crouches at the crack, waving at the speck.]

Doug crouched down beside the crack, waved at the speck and said hi friend. The devil worm did not look up.

[Speech bubble: "hi friend?" The worm keeps eating. Doug stands up, unharmed. DOUG DEATHS stays at 163. No sticker.]

Doug survived, and the narrator, who named this item after the devil for a reason, is quietly disappointed.

A worm named after the devil, living in water sealed off for thousands of years, and its whole plan is eating bacteria.

## Veryovkina Cave

Veryovkina Cave.

[Map of the Caucasus, red dot in Abkhazia, Georgia. A plateau of grey limestone with one dark hole. DOUG DEPTH: 2,209 m.]

High in the mountains of Abkhazia, in Georgia, a hole in a limestone plateau drops more than two thousand two hundred meters into the dark.

That's deeper than two and a half Burj Khalifas stacked end to end. It and a neighboring cave called Krubera keep trading the title of deepest known cave on Earth, a few meters at a time.

[Two Burj Khalifas and a half drawn beside the cave profile. Label: "2,200 M".]

Nobody just climbs down. Teams descend rope after rope, and they sleep in camps along the way, at six hundred meters, at one thousand three hundred and fifty, and at two thousand one hundred. A team in twenty eighteen took four days to reach the bottom.

## The Oldest Water

The Oldest Water.

[Map of Ontario, Canada, red dot on Timmins. A mine headframe over a deep shaft. DOUG DEPTH: 2,400 m.]

Near the town of Timmins in Ontario, the Kidd Creek mine goes down about three kilometers into some of the oldest rock in Canada.

In two thousand thirteen, scientists sampled water leaking from cracks in the rock about two point four kilometers down. They measured the noble gases dissolved in it, which build up slowly over time, like rust on a gate.

[A drop of water under a magnifying glass. Labels: "HELIUM", "XENON".]

The result was hard to believe, because this water had been sealed in the rock for at least a billion years.

Here's what nobody expected. Barbara Sherwood Lollar, the geochemist who led the work, tasted that billion-year-old water, and her review was short. It tastes terrible.

[Label in red handwriting: "TERRIBLE".]

In two thousand sixteen, the same team went deeper, to almost three kilometers, and found water about two billion years old. It is the oldest water ever found on Earth.

For scale, one of the oldest animals we know of from fossils lived about five hundred and fifty-eight million years ago. This water was sealed in more than three times further back than that.

[Timeline: "WATER SEALED" far left, "FIRST ANIMALS" near the right edge.]

It is about eight times saltier than seawater, and it carries sulfate, a kind of chemical food that some microbes can live on.

[Doug holds a full glass of the water, toasts the camera and drains it. His face goes flat-unimpressed.]

Doug drank a whole glass and made the same face she probably made.

Doug is fine.

[DOUG DEATHS stays at 164. No sticker.]

For the record, it is not drinking water.

Over a billion years in the dark, waiting for a visitor, and the review was three words long.

## The Loneliest Bacterium

The Loneliest Bacterium.

[Map of South Africa, red dot on the Mponeng gold mine. A crack in hot rock, water dripping. DOUG DEPTH: 2,800 m.]

About two point eight kilometers under South Africa, in the Mponeng gold mine, hot water seeps out of a crack in the rock at around sixty degrees Celsius.

In two thousand eight, scientists filtered about five thousand six hundred liters of it and pulled out all the DNA they could find. They expected a crowd of different microbes.

[A crowd of different microbe shapes appears. Then they all turn into the same rod shape.]

Instead, more than ninety-nine point nine percent of the DNA came from one single species of bacterium. If the sample were a stadium with a thousand fans, nine hundred and ninety-nine of them would be the same guy.

The crack it lives in has not touched the surface for millions of years.

The unsettling part is where its energy comes from, and it isn't the Sun. Uranium in the surrounding rock is slowly decaying, and that radiation helps produce hydrogen and sulfate in the water, which is what the bacterium lives on.

[Label: "RADIOACTIVE". Yellow warning triangle beside the rock.]

It is an entire ecosystem with one member, powered by radioactive rock. Its name is Desulforudis audaxviator, and the second part comes from a Latin line in Jules Verne's novel, Journey to the Center of the Earth. Descend, bold traveler, and you will reach the center of the Earth.

[Doug carries a small rug and a houseplant into the crack, sets them down and waves at the bacteria. Speech bubble: "roommates?"]

Doug moved into the crack with a rug and a houseplant, so the bacterium would finally have some company.

[Doug glows green. X eyes. He tips over onto the rug. The cap sits on the houseplant. Sticker "MPONENG". DOUG DEATHS: 165.]

It has lived alone in that crack for millions of years, and it was not looking for a roommate.

## Mponeng

Mponeng.

[The same mine, wider shot: a headframe, a cage, a shaft dropping out of frame. DOUG DEPTH: 4,000 m.]

Mponeng is the deepest mine on Earth. By twenty twelve, it was already working almost four kilometers down, and it has since pushed past the four kilometer mark.

Getting there starts with one of the tallest lifts in the world. A steel cage holds about a hundred and twenty workers and drops more than two kilometers in about three minutes, at up to sixty-four kilometers per hour. A second lift goes deeper.

[A cage packed with tiny stick figures plunging down the shaft. Speed lines.]

About four thousand people make this trip every day.

At the bottom, the rock itself is around sixty degrees Celsius, and the humidity can climb above ninety-five percent. Hot tubs are built so their water never goes above forty degrees, and the walls down here are hotter than that.

[Thermometer annotation: "HOT TUB 40" low on the scale, "ROCK 60" high.]

Here's the problem. Nobody could work in that, so on the surface a plant makes thousands of tonnes of ice, mixes it into a salty slush and pumps it down into deep reservoirs. Fans blow air across it, and the deepest tunnels come down to around twenty-nine degrees, which counts as the cool part of the day.

[A pipe of white slush running down the shaft into a reservoir. Fans. Label: "ICE".]

Doug has been this hot before, in a cave full of giant crystals.

[Doug waits on a platform beside the slush pipe, holding his suitcase. The pipe sputters and stops.]

## The Kola Superdeep Borehole

The Kola Superdeep Borehole.

[Map of Russia's Kola Peninsula near Norway. A rusty metal cap welded over a hole in a concrete floor. DOUG DEPTH: 12,262 m.]

In nineteen seventy, Soviet scientists started drilling a hole on the Kola Peninsula, near the border with Norway. They wanted to see how deep a drill could go.

By nineteen eighty-nine, one branch of it reached twelve thousand two hundred and sixty-two meters, and it is still the deepest hole humans have ever made. Some oil wells are longer, but they angle off sideways.

That's deeper than Mount Everest is tall, with more than three kilometers to spare, and yet the hole is only about twenty-three centimeters wide.

[Everest drawn upside down beside the borehole, the hole reaching further. Label: "12 KM".]

Along the way, they found microscopic fossils of plankton more than six kilometers down.

Then the bottom got hot. They had expected around one hundred degrees Celsius, and the rock was about one hundred and eighty. At that heat, the rock started behaving less like stone and more like plastic, and the hole kept trying to close itself. Drilling stopped in the early nineteen nineties.

And no, nobody recorded screams from hell down there, because that story is a hoax.

[Red X over a cartoon microphone.]

Now for the uncomfortable part. It is about six thousand three hundred and seventy-one kilometers to the center of the Earth, and twelve kilometers is about zero point two percent of the way.

[Cut to the cross-section Earth. The red dot sits on the surface line. It has not moved.]

If Earth were a ball one meter wide, the deepest hole ever drilled would be less than one millimeter deep.

## The Moho

The Moho.

[The cross-section Earth. For the first time, the red dot moves below the surface line. DOUG DEPTH: 35 km.]

In nineteen oh nine, a Croatian scientist named Andrija Mohorovičić was studying a Balkan earthquake. He noticed that at a certain depth, the earthquake's waves suddenly sped up.

He had found the bottom of the crust, and below it is the mantle, a different kind of rock. The line between them is named after him, and everyone calls it the Moho.

[A layered block: "CRUST" on top, "MANTLE" below. A wavy line between them labeled "MOHO".]

Under the continents, it sits about thirty-five kilometers down on average, the height of four Mount Everests stacked on top of each other. Under the ocean floor, it's much closer, about six kilometers.

So in nineteen sixty-one, American scientists tried to drill to it through the seafloor off Guadalupe Island, Mexico. Project Mohole got about one hundred and eighty-three meters into the seabed before the costs rose, and in nineteen sixty-six, Congress ended it.

[A rubber stamp on a stack of papers: "CANCELLED".]

Japan built a drilling ship called Chikyu that is designed, one day, to drill all the way through.

The awkward part is that the Moho is one of the most important lines inside our planet, and no drill has ever gone through it. Almost everything we know about it, we got from listening to earthquakes.

[A small capsule appears next to Doug: a crude drill pod with a nose drill and one porthole.]

For this part of the trip, Doug has a drill pod.

[The pod bores down through rock. It stops at the wavy line. The hatch opens. Doug is flat inside, X eyes. The cap rests on the porthole. Sticker "THE MOHO". DOUG DEATHS: 167.]

The drill pod reached the Moho in perfect condition. Doug did not.

Doug has died. Again, but deeper.

Doug is the first visitor to the Moho, and he will not be filing a report.

## The Hidden Ocean

The Hidden Ocean.

[The cross-section Earth, the red dot deeper in the mantle. DOUG DEPTH: 660 km.]

Between about four hundred and ten and six hundred and sixty kilometers down, there is a layer of the mantle called the transition zone. Nobody has ever been there, and nobody has ever sampled it directly.

Then, in two thousand eight, miners in Juína, Brazil, picked a small, scruffy diamond out of river gravel. A volcanic eruption had carried it up from deep inside the Earth.

[A dull brown diamond in a miner's palm. Zoom in: a tiny green speck inside it.]

Trapped inside it was a speck of a mineral called ringwoodite. Ringwoodite only forms at the pressures found in the transition zone, and in two thousand fourteen, scientists reported that this speck was about one and a half percent water by weight.

That sounds small, but this layer is enormous. One of the lead scientists said the transition zone might hold as much water as all the world's oceans put together.

[Label: "AS MUCH AS THE OCEANS?" with a red "?".]

That same year, another team reported earthquake data from about six hundred and sixty kilometers under North America that fit melting caused by water. Nobody knows yet whether that one diamond speaks for the whole planet.

Here's the catch. None of that water is liquid, because it is locked inside the crystals themselves as part of the rock. A hundred kilograms of this rock would hold about one and a half kilograms of water, about one large bottle's worth, and you could never pour it out.

[A big rock on a scale, a water bottle beside it. Label: "1.5%".]

[Doug in swim trunks and a snorkel, standing in the drill pod hatch, ready to jump.]

Doug packed a snorkel, opened the hatch ready for a swim, and found solid rock in every direction.

[Doug squashed flat, X eyes. The snorkel sticks straight up. The cap rests on the snorkel. Sticker "HIDDEN OCEAN". DOUG DEATHS: 168.]

Doug has died. Again.

There may be an ocean's worth of water down here, and not one drop of it is wet.

## The Outer Core

The Outer Core.

[The cross-section Earth. The red dot passes into a glowing orange layer. DOUG DEPTH: 2,890 km.]

About two thousand eight hundred and ninety kilometers down, the solid rock of the mantle ends. What starts there is a sea of liquid metal, mostly iron and nickel.

It is more than four thousand degrees Celsius, and the whole core, this liquid layer and everything inside it, is wider than the planet Mars.

[Mars drawn beside Earth's core, slightly smaller. Label: "BIGGER THAN MARS".]

This metal is always moving, because heat escaping from deeper down stirs it and the spin of the planet twists the flow. Moving liquid iron makes electric currents, and those currents make Earth's magnetic field.

[Swirling arrows in the orange layer. Field lines loop out from the planet into space.]

That field reaches far out into space and shields the surface from some of the incoming cosmic radiation, while the wind of particles streaming from the Sun gets pushed around it.

It also isn't fixed, because the magnetic poles wander, and every so often the whole field flips, so that north becomes south. Over the last two point six million years, it has fully reversed ten times. The most recent flip happened about seven hundred and eighty thousand years ago.

The irony is that the most hostile place on this whole trip, a sea of molten metal, helps keep the surface livable.

[Doug inside the drill pod holding a compass. The needle spins in circles.]

Doug brought a compass. Down here, it had no idea which way was north, and to be fair, neither does the planet, sometimes.

[The drill pod sags and melts into a cartoon blob. Only the suitcase and the cap float up out of the blob, bobbing in the orange. Sticker "OUTER CORE". DOUG DEATHS: 169.]

Doug has died. Again.

One of the things keeping life on the surface safe is a sea of molten iron nobody has ever seen.

## The Inner Core

The Inner Core.

[The cross-section Earth. The red dot reaches a white-hot ball at the very center. DOUG DEPTH: 6,371 km.]

In nineteen thirty-six, a Danish seismologist named Inge Lehmann looked at earthquake waves passing through the planet and saw something nobody had expected. Some of them were bouncing off something solid, deep inside the liquid core.

There is a ball at the center of the Earth, mostly iron and nickel, with a radius of about one thousand two hundred and twenty kilometers, roughly seventy percent of the Moon's.

[The Moon drawn beside the inner core, a little bigger. Label: "70% OF THE MOON".]

In twenty thirteen, scientists squeezed tiny samples of iron between two diamonds and heated them with lasers, to find out when iron melts at these pressures. Their answer put the edge of the inner core at about six thousand degrees Celsius, give or take five hundred.

The surface of the Sun is about five thousand five hundred.

[Thermometer annotation: "SUN SURFACE" and "INNER CORE" side by side, both near the top.]

It stays solid because the weight of the entire planet is pressing on it. At the edge of the inner core, the pressure is about three point three million times the air pressure you're sitting in right now, and the iron is squeezed so hard it cannot melt.

And here's the strangest part of the whole trip. This ball is not locked in place, and it turns on its own, a little out of step with the rest of the planet.

[The inner core drawn rotating slightly against the mantle, curved red arrows.]

In twenty twenty-three, two researchers reported that it had stopped pulling ahead of the surface around two thousand nine, and may be turning back, as part of a cycle that might take about seventy years. In twenty twenty-four, another team reported that it had begun moving slower than the surface around twenty ten. Other scientists still disagree, so the ball at the center of the world is being argued about right now.

The hottest place Doug has ever been is also the one place where nothing can melt.

[Doug at the exact center, planting a small flag. Arrows point away from him in every direction, each labeled "UP".]

Doug planted a flag at the exact center of the Earth, where every single direction is up.

[Doug flat, X eyes. The cap rests on the flagpole. Sticker "THE CENTER". The suitcase is full. DOUG DEATHS: 170.]

Doug has died. Again.

[Back on the surface. A plain field. A small hole that wasn't there before. The suitcase and the cap pop out of it together and land side by side. Twelve stops on the cross-section; nine stickers on the suitcase.]

Six thousand three hundred and seventy-one kilometers down, and the only things that came back were the cap and the luggage.
