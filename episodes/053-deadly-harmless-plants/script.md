## Water Hemlock

Water Hemlock.

[Doug in a meadow with a basket and the plant edition of his field guide, a pressed-flower page with an EDIBLE? box. He pencils YES. A tall plant with white umbrella-shaped flower clusters.]

The United States Department of Agriculture calls it the most violently toxic plant that grows in North America, and the worst of it sits underground, in the thick roots.

[Crude cutaway of a nerve cell with a brake pedal labelled "STOP". A cicutoxin blob wedges under the pedal.]

The poison is called cicutoxin, and it goes after the brakes in your brain. Your nerves run on two kinds of signal, the ones that say go and the ones that say stop. Cicutoxin blocks one of the brain's main stop receptors, so the go signals keep firing and nothing is left to calm them down.

Here's the part that ruins dinner.

[A family photo of vegetables: carrot, parsnip, celery, parsley. Water hemlock stands in the back row, smiling a little too normally.]

Water hemlock belongs to the carrot family, alongside the carrot, the parsnip, the celery and the parsley, which is basically the whole bag of a supermarket soup mix. ## The Yew

The Yew.

[A dark, dense evergreen tree beside a small stone church. Gravestones. A picnic blanket.]

Almost every part of this tree carries poisons that can stop a heart, and people have been planting it in churchyards for centuries.

The Woodland Trust estimates that the oldest yew in Britain, at Fortingall in Scotland, is between two and three thousand years old, and other guesses run far higher. Nobody can simply count its rings, because the middle of the trunk rotted away long ago.

[Cutaway of a heart as a pump with a little electric spark running through it. Taxine blobs block the wires.]

The poisons are called taxine alkaloids. According to veterinary toxicologists at Cornell University, they block channels in heart muscle cells and scramble the electrical signals that keep the beat steady, which often ends in sudden death.

Farm vets see it again and again, and in early twenty twenty-four, four beef cattle in New England were found dead over three days after reaching a yew. One was seen eating normally moments before it went down.

Here's the problem.

[Close-up of a yew berry: a bright red fleshy cup with a dark seed sitting inside it. Label: "SAFE" on the cup, "NOT SAFE" on the seed.]

The only part of the yew that isn't toxic is the bright red cup, and the seed sitting inside that cup is toxic. It works like a cherry jelly sweet with a poison pellet for a centre.

[Doug on the picnic blanket, a handful of red berries. Field guide EDIBLE? YES, overwritten NO.]

Doug ate a handful of the berries, seeds included, because they looked festive. He said grace first, to be safe.

[Cut to a blank gravestone. The cap rests on top. DOUG DEATHS: 393.]

Doug has died.

The yew was standing there long before the church, and it plans to be there long after. The next plant goes for the mind long before it bothers with the heart.

## Deadly Nightshade

Deadly Nightshade.

[A leafy shrub with glossy black berries and dull purple bell flowers.]

Its berries are dark and shiny and, according to the United States Forest Service, sweet, and the plant carries powerful alkaloids, including atropine and scopolamine.

[Cutaway of a nerve passing a message to a gland. Atropine blobs sit on the receivers like tape over a doorbell.]

Those two block a messenger chemical that your body uses to run its quiet, automatic jobs, like making spit and sweat, slowing the heart and focusing the eyes. Shut those jobs down and the body overheats, dries out and speeds up.

Doctors learn the result with a rhyme: hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, and mad as a hatter.

The worst part is the hatter, but the eyes go first.

[Doug's eyes widen in steps, pupils swallowing the whole iris like a camera aperture opening all the way.]

The pupils grow from a pinhead to the whole coloured ring, like a camera opening its shutter as wide as it will go. The story goes that ladies at the Venetian court dripped belladonna into their eyes for exactly this look, since bella donna means beautiful woman.

Poisoning brings a racing heart, delirium and hallucinations, and none of it is fun, just confusion, heat and a body that will not settle down.

[Doug, flat red face, enormous black pupils, sitting in a hallway talking earnestly to a hat stand. Field guide: YES, overwritten NO.]

Doug is fine. He has spent the whole afternoon explaining his day to a hat stand, which he believes is the narrator.

The narrator had the counter ready, and the next plant is one Doug can see from the highway.

## Oleander

Oleander.

[A freeway at rush hour. A median strip packed with pink and white flowering shrubs, one after another into the distance.]

California has an estimated twenty-five million oleanders planted along its highways and roadsides, and every part of every one of them is poisonous. They shrug off long droughts, which is exactly why they line so many median strips.

The toxin is called oleandrin, and toxicologists compare it to digoxin, a heart medicine that is only safe in carefully measured amounts.

[Cutaway of a heart cell with a little revolving door labelled "PUMP". Oleandrin blobs jam the door.]

Each heart cell has tiny pumps that shuffle salts in and out, and the rhythm of every beat depends on them. Oleandrin jams those pumps, so the heartbeat slows down, skips and stumbles, like a metronome on a piano while someone keeps nudging the arm.

But that's not the strange part.

[A campfire with hot dogs on long sticks. A big red "?" stamp.]

The most famous oleander story is about campers who died after roasting hot dogs on oleander sticks. In twenty twenty, two toxicologists actually tried it, and the hot dogs picked up only a negligible amount of toxin. On top of that, fresh branches thin enough to use as skewers were too weak to hold a hot dog.

The skewer story is probably a myth, but the leaves are not.

[Doug in a stopped car, window down, a pink oleander branch within reach. Field guide: YES, overwritten NO.]

Traffic hadn't moved in forty minutes, so Doug reached out of the window and chewed a leaf out of pure boredom.

## Mad Honey

Mad Honey.

[A crude Greek army camp in green hills above a dark sea. Soldiers in helmets eat honeycomb.]

More than two thousand four hundred years ago, a Greek army ate wild honey near the Black Sea, and every man who ate it went down.

The general Xenophon wrote it up himself, and the men who ate the honey lost their senses and could not stand. They lay across the ground in such numbers that the camp looked as if the army had lost a battle.

[Hillside covered in flat soldiers, lined up like fallen dominoes.]

Picture a hillside of dominoes, all knocked over at once. Nobody had died by the next day, and on the third or fourth day the men got up again, as if from a drugging.

[Bees on pink and yellow rhododendron flowers. Arrows from flower to bee to honey jar.]

The real culprit was the flowers. In the eastern Black Sea region of Turkey, bees feed on certain rhododendrons, and those flowers carry poisons called grayanotoxins straight into the honey.

Here comes the worst bit.

[Cutaway of a nerve with a light switch labelled "OFF". Grayanotoxin tape holds it at "ON".]

Grayanotoxins stop nerve channels from switching off, which cranks up the nerve that slows your heart, so the heartbeat drops, the blood pressure falls, and the room starts to spin. A review in the journal Cardiovascular Toxicology says the symptoms usually last a day or two, and that in humans it is rarely lethal.

[Doug lies among the soldiers, a crude helmet on top of the red cap. Field guide: YES, overwritten NO.]

Technically, Doug wasn't poisoned by a plant at all, but by breakfast.

Doug is fine. Eventually.

The honey was the bees' fault, and the next one is entirely the cow's.

## White Snakeroot

White Snakeroot.

[An eighteen-hundreds frontier cabin in Indiana. A cow grazes at the edge of a forest full of small, fuzzy white flowers.]

On the American frontier, a glass of milk could kill you. Smithsonian Magazine says milk sickness killed thousands of settlers and farmers in the Midwest, and nobody had poisoned the milk on purpose.

[Arrow from flower to cow to bucket to glass.]

When a cow grazes on white snakeroot, a toxin from the plant passes straight into her milk. The cows themselves got what farmers called the trembles, with shaking that was worst in the legs and muzzle, and the people who drank the milk got milk sickness.

Here's the cruel part.

[A cow with a little green arrow leaving her body and a red arrow pointing into the bucket.]

According to a Texas A and M University guide to range plants, sending the toxin into her milk protects the mother animal and poisons the young. Every time the cow got milked, a little of the poison left her and went into the bucket the settlers drank from. One glass of milk, and the cow did all the work.

[Plain card, no Doug: "NANCY HANKS LINCOLN, 1818".]

In October eighteen eighteen, Abraham Lincoln's mother, Nancy Hanks Lincoln, died in Indiana, and the National Park Service says it was milk sickness.

Scientists only published a chemical analysis of the plant's toxins in nineteen twenty-seven, more than a century after the settlers started dying.

[Doug as a settler with a straw hat over the cap, holding a glass of milk beside a visibly trembling cow. Field guide: YES, overwritten NO.]

Doug noticed that the cow was shaking, and he decided she was probably just cold.

## Giant Hogweed

Giant Hogweed.

[A huge plant with white umbrella-shaped flower heads towering over a basketball hoop. Doug for scale, tiny.]

According to the Royal Horticultural Society, this weed can grow five meters tall or more, and gardeners used to plant it on purpose because it looked so grand. A basketball hoop hangs about three meters off the ground, so the hogweed clears it with room left over.

It was brought to Britain from the Caucasus Mountains in the early eighteen-hundreds, and then it escaped the gardens and kept going.

[Cutaway of skin with a thin layer of sap on top. A cloud covers the sun. Nothing happens.]

Its sap contains furanocoumarins, chemicals that make skin extremely sensitive to sunlight. Brushing against the plant can leave that sap on your arm without you feeling a thing, and for a while nothing happens.

Except the sap has a silent partner.

[The cloud moves. The sun comes out. The sap layer turns red.]

The damage only comes when sap on the skin meets sunlight, and King County in Washington State warns that the combination can cause severe, painful burns. The same agency says the skin's sensitivity to sunlight can carry on for years.

So the real weapon isn't the plant at all but the sun, and the plant just hands it a target.

[Doug strolls past the hogweed in the shade, brushing a leaf. Thumbs up. Field guide EDIBLE? YES, overwritten NO, with "TOUCH?" added underneath in red.]

Doug brushed past one in the shade, felt nothing at all, and then stepped out into a lovely sunny day.

[Doug turns flat cartoon pink and dives under an umbrella. He stays there.]

Doug is fine. Indoors.

The hogweed needed help from the sun, and the next plant doesn't need help from anything.

## Gympie-Gympie

Gympie-Gympie.

[A Queensland rainforest. A shrub with big, soft-looking, heart-shaped leaves.]

University of Queensland researchers describe this rainforest plant as one of the most venomous plants in the world. With its big, soft, heart-shaped leaves, it looks like it would make a decent cushion.

[Close-up of a leaf covered in fine needles. A label: "HAIRS ~5 mm".]

The leaves and stems are covered in stiff hairs about five millimeters long. Ecologist Marina Hurley, who studied these trees for years, says the hairs are partly made of silica, and they work like tiny hypodermic needles that inject venom on contact. Think of a glove knitted out of fibreglass insulation.

In twenty twenty, Edward Gilding's team identified the venom's pain toxins, the gympietides, which fold into shapes like spider and cone snail toxins and hit the same pain-sensing targets in the nerves.

[Doug pats one leaf with a single fingertip. Field guide: YES, overwritten NO.]

The sharp pain lasts for hours, and painful flares can keep coming back for days and weeks.

And then it gets stranger.

[A dusty museum drawer with a pressed leaf on card. A red "!" stamp.]

According to Hurley, dried specimens collected more than a hundred years ago can still sting. The plant has been dead for a century, and it still wins.

[A wall calendar behind Doug flips its pages. Doug flinches at every flip.]

Doug is fine. Doug will be fine in a few weeks. He has circled a date on the calendar, and he flinches every time a page turns.

Every plant so far needed Doug to do something, and the last one doesn't.

No touch.

## The Manchineel

The Manchineel.

[A friendly, round beach tree on white sand, small green apples, a red band painted around the trunk. CONTACT dial on NONE.]

Guinness World Records lists this as the most dangerous tree in the world. It grows on Caribbean and Florida beaches, and it looks like a lovely place to sit.

The Spanish called it manzanilla, little apple, but also the tree of death.

[Cutaway of a branch oozing white sap from a leaf, a twig and a fruit.]

Its leaves, twigs, bark and fruit all hold a milky sap that can raise burn-like blisters on skin.

[Rain falls. Drops run down the leaves, pick up white sap, and drip onto the sand below.]

When it rains, water off the leaves can carry the sap onto anyone sheltering underneath. Think of a garden sprinkler filled with something you would never water anything with.

[A campfire made from manchineel branches. The smoke drifts sideways.]

Burn the wood, and the smoke can cause temporary blindness.

[A beach in Tobago. A small green apple with one bite out of it. A red "!" stamp.]

The fruit is worse. In the British Medical Journal, a radiologist described biting one on a Tobago beach and finding it pleasantly sweet. Then her mouth went peppery, the burning grew, her throat tightened, and swallowing became very hard.

Many trees are painted with red rings or carry warning signs.

Here's the strangest part.

[A small official-looking shield over the tree: "PROTECTED".]

Florida lists it as a state endangered species, with only about twenty known populations, and these trees help shield the shore from storm winds. The state would like it to survive.

For all that, deaths are rarely documented, and one medical journal calls them more legend than fact.

Doug sheltered from the rain under it, made a fire from its branches to dry off, and then ate one of its apples.
