## 2. Escalation axis and items
**Axis: severity, meaning how much of the brain the parasite takes.** It starts with parasites that **steer** behaviour (the animal hosts),
then parasites that **live in** the human brain, and ends with ones that **destroy** brain tissue. Inside each band, items are ordered roughly by how
often they kill (the writer sources the numbers and may swap neighbours if the data disagree). The boss is the most destructive and the hardest to escape.

Spoken section headers (2 words, new to the channel): **"Steers you."** (folded into the opener, before item 1), **"Lives in you."** (before item
4, about 33%; the costume comes off here, at the shift to human hosts), **"Eats you."** (before item 8, about 75%).
Background ramp (the art director defines it): pale pond green and grass gold, then flesh-pink and lab cream, then deep maroon and near-black for the boss.

Every number needs a source; cut what you can't source. Deaths are cartoon only: X eyes, ghost Doug, the floating cap. **Never describe what a
parasite does to brain tissue in physical detail.** Symptoms are allowed as one clinical line per item at most (headache, confusion, seizures, a
stiff neck), and only as the patient would notice them. That is what "would feel like" means here.

### Steers you. (animal hosts, Doug in costume; the cap stays on top of every costume)

**1. Killifish Fluke** (Euhaplorchis californiensis; the hook item; Doug as a California killifish; death 115)
- Facts: the larval flukes form cysts in a thin layer over the fish's brain, and a single fish can carry hundreds to thousands of them (sourced:
  Lafferty & Morris 1996, *Ecology*, or Shaw et al. 2009); infected fish shimmy, flash their sides and swim near the surface; infected fish were
  **10-30 times more likely to be eaten by birds** (Lafferty & Morris 1996, quote the exact figure); the next host is the bird, and then a horn snail
  (sourced life cycle).
- Analogy idea: the cysts in one fish vs a sourced count (the number of seats in a cinema, or a stadium section); the fish is about the length of a
  sourced object (a finger, a pen).
- Twist (must land by 0:45, about 145 words; aim for 25-35 s): **the fish isn't sick. It is dancing, and the audience is a heron.**
- Costume gag: the narration says once that this fluke can't infect people ("which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a killifish").
- Doug beat: Doug the killifish starts doing a little shimmy at the surface, pleased with himself. A shadow. Only the cap floats on the water. Death 115.

**2. Lancet Liver Fluke** (Dicrocoelium dendriticum; Doug in an ant costume; death 116)
- Facts: the life cycle runs snail, ant, then grazing animal (sourced); one of the larvae in the ant moves to a nerve centre near its head, and
  infected ants climb a blade of grass at dusk and clamp on with their jaws (sourced: e.g., Martín-Vega et al. 2018, *Scientific Reports*);
  **when it warms up in the morning the ant lets go and acts normal again, then repeats it the next evening** (sourced, temperature-linked);
  the goal is to be eaten with the grass by a sheep or cow (sourced).
- Analogy idea: the ant on its grass blade vs a person on a sourced tall object (the height ratio, e.g., a skyscraper); verify the number before using it.
- Twist: **it isn't a zombie. It has a night shift.** Every evening the ant goes up and waits to be eaten, and every morning it gets its life back.
- Callback (allowed, one line): 002's zombie ant fungus. The difference is the point: that fungus keeps the ant, this one gives it back every morning.
- Doug beat: Doug the ant clamps on at dusk. Morning, climbs down, fine. Second night, the same. Third night, a cow's shadow. Death 116.

**3. Gid Tapeworm** (Taenia multiceps, "gid" or "sturdy" in sheep; Doug in a sheep costume; death 117)
- Facts: dogs carry the adult worm, and sheep swallow the eggs from grass (sourced, a veterinary source such as the Merck Veterinary Manual or a
  UK/AU agriculture body); the larval cyst grows in the sheep's brain and can reach the size of a sourced object (verify: golf ball or hen's egg);
  infected sheep walk in circles, often towards the side of the cyst, and lose their balance (sourced); the worm needs a dog to eat the sheep's head
  to finish the cycle (sourced).
- **Costume rule exception:** this one *can* infect people, very rarely (coenurosis, sourced). So the costume line must **not** say it can't infect
  people. Use: "In people it is extremely rare. In sheep it is not. Which is why Doug is dressed as a sheep."
- Analogy idea: the cyst vs the sheep's brain (a sourced size comparison only, e.g., a golf ball in an orange).
- Twist: **the cyst grows on one side, so the sheep walks in circles towards it. It is steering the sheep by taking up room.**
- Doug beat: Doug the sheep walks a perfect circle into the field's only sheepdog. "hi friend?" Death 117.

### Lives in you. (human hosts, Doug as himself; the costume comes off on screen)

**4. Rat Lungworm** (Angiostrongylus cantonensis; Doug survives, "Doug is fine. Mostly.")
- Facts: the normal life cycle runs rats to slugs or snails and back to rats; in a rat, the larvae go through the brain on their way to the lungs
  (sourced, CDC); **in a human they get lost and stay in the brain**, causing eosinophilic meningitis (CDC); cases have been recorded in Hawaii and
  across Asia and the Pacific (sourced, Hawaii Department of Health or CDC; give a count only if sourced); most people recover (CDC wording).
- Analogy idea: the larva is about as long as a sourced small object (verify; "smaller than a grain of rice"); the worm in a rat vs its name (the
  name is "lungworm", and the brain is just a stop on the way).
- Twist: **it's a lungworm. The brain was supposed to be a layover, and in humans it never finds the exit.**
- Doug beat: Doug finds a slug on his garden lettuce and waves ("hi friend?"). Headache card, a week in bed. "Doug is fine. Mostly." No death.
  **No slug-eating dares and no named victims** (the well-known Sydney case involved a 19-year-old; leave it out entirely).

**5. Pork Tapeworm** (Taenia solium, neurocysticercosis; death 118)
- Facts: the WHO calls neurocysticercosis a leading preventable cause of epilepsy and says it is behind **about 30% of epilepsy cases in places
  where the worm is common** (WHO fact sheet, quote exactly); it comes from **swallowing the worm's eggs**, which come from a person who carries the adult
  tapeworm, not from the pork itself (CDC; say it in one clinical line, no bathroom description); the cysts can sit in the brain for years before anyone
  notices (sourced); it causes tens of thousands of deaths a year (verify with a WHO/GBD figure or cut).
- Analogy idea: a cyst is about the size of a sourced object (a pea, a grape); the share of epilepsy cases against a sourced ratio (about 1 in 3).
- Twist: **eating the undercooked pork gives you the worm in your gut. It's the eggs from someone else's gut that end up in your brain.** It is the
  other person's tapeworm. Doug never even ate pork.
- Doug beat: Doug, now a vegetarian "to be safe", orders a salad. The cyst icon appears in his head outline. Death 118. **This is not advice:** no
  hand-washing or cooking tips. The joke is that Doug's precaution was aimed at the wrong thing.

**6. Spirometra** (sparganosis; the 2014 UK case; Doug survives, "Doug is fine.")
- Facts: a man in the UK had a tapeworm larva (Spirometra erinaceieuropaei) that **moved about 5 cm across his brain over 4 years**, tracked on
  repeat scans (Bennett et al. 2014, *Genome Biology*; BBC/Wellcome Sanger coverage); it was removed by surgery and its genome was sequenced, the
  first for this species (same source); people get it from raw frog or snake meat, poultices, or contaminated water (sourced, attribute the
  transmission routes carefully); the patient recovered (sourced; **no name, no age, no surgery detail**).
- Analogy idea: 5 cm in 4 years vs a sourced slow thing (e.g., how fast fingernails grow; verify the rate).
- Twist: **the doctors could see it on the scans, a little further across his brain each time.** Not a tumour. A traveller.
- Doug beat: Doug's scans become a flipbook: the worm-dot walks across Doug's head outline with a tiny suitcase (an allowed one-off nod to the `places`
  prop, drawn by the art director). Removed. "Doug is fine." The narrator had already updated the counter and has to change it back.

**7. Gnathostoma** (Gnathostoma spinigerum, gnathostomiasis; death 119)
- Facts: people get it from raw or undercooked freshwater fish and other hosts (CDC); the larva wanders through the body, showing up as a swelling
  that moves (CDC; one clinical line only); when it reaches the spinal cord or brain, it is far more serious, and a sourced share of those cases is
  fatal (e.g., a review figure of about 8-25%; verify the exact source or cut the number); cases are growing outside Asia with travel and food trends (sourced, CDC/EID).
- Analogy idea: the distance a larva can travel in the body vs a sourced length (a ruler, a forearm).
- Twist: **every other worm on this list picks a spot. This one keeps moving, and the brain is just where its trip ends.**
- Doug beat: a dotted line wanders around Doug's outline like a lost tourist on a map, and ends at the head. Death 119.

### Eats you.

**8. Halicephalobus** (Halicephalobus gingivalis; the horse worm; death 120)
- Facts: a tiny soil and manure nematode that is best known for invading **horses' brains** (sourced, veterinary literature); only a handful of human
  cases are known, linked to contact with soil or manure, and **all or nearly all were fatal** (CDC *Emerging Infectious Diseases* case reports or a
  review; give the exact count and survivors only as sourced); it reproduces inside the host without needing a mate (sourced: parthenogenesis), so
  numbers grow (sourced).
- Analogy idea: the worm is under a sourced size (smaller than a sourced object, e.g., the width of a hair; verify the length).
- Twist: **it doesn't need a partner, a second host or a plan. It just keeps making more of itself.**
- Doug beat: Doug pats a horse ("hi friend?"). The horse looks at Doug with something like sympathy. Death 120. **No horse suffering shown.**

**9. Balamuthia** (Balamuthia mandrillaris; the boss; death 121)
- Facts: a free-living amoeba found in soil (CDC); it can enter through a skin wound or by breathing in dust, and it causes granulomatous amoebic
  encephalitis (CDC); **about 90% of known cases have been fatal** (CDC, quote exactly); it was first found in 1986 in a mandrill that died at the San
  Diego Zoo (sourced, the name comes from it); it can infect **healthy people** and develops over weeks to months, which makes it hard to diagnose,
  and many cases are only identified after death (sourced); give a US case count since its discovery if CDC prints one.
- Analogy idea: the amoeba is too small to see, a sourced fraction of a full stop on a page; the number of known US cases vs a sourced small crowd (one bus).
- Callback (exactly one line, required): 002's boss, Naegleria, the brain-eating amoeba that killed 97% in days. Balamuthia is its slower cousin. **It is
  in garden soil, not a warm lake, and it takes its time.**
- Twist: **the fast one at least lets you know. This one takes weeks, and it is in ordinary dirt.**
- Doug beat: Doug gardening, very happy, a tiny scratch on his hand, a flowerpot. Long hold. Death 121. Cut to the cap sitting on the empty flowerpot.
- **Closing image:** the cap on the flowerpot, with one dry kicker about the cap (the only thing on this list that never had a brain to lose).

Deaths: 7 (115 to 121). Survivals: Rat Lungworm ("Doug is fine. Mostly.") and Spirometra ("Doug is fine.", the narrator has to change the counter back). 9 items.

## 7. Risks
- **Medical advice (HIGHEST for this topic):** no prevention or treatment advice of any kind. No "cook your pork", "wash your hands", "wash your
  lettuce", "don't eat raw fish", "wear gloves when gardening", "see a doctor if…", no drug names, no symptoms checklist. Doug's precautions are always
  wrong and never framed as tips. The titler adds one description line: "Entertainment and education only. Nothing here is medical advice."
- **Gore (HIGH):** brains, cysts and worms in the head are the topic. **Never describe tissue damage, surgery or the inside of the skull.** No real
  MRI/CT images, no real patient or animal photos, no anatomical brain drawings. The parasite is a cartoon icon inside Doug's head outline. Symptoms are one
  clinical line, as the person would notice them. No body-horror close-ups in the thumbnail.
- **Real patients (HIGH):** the 2014 UK Spirometra case and any Halicephalobus or Balamuthia case reports are real people. Use the year, country and
  outcome only: **no names, no ages, no families.** No children in any fact (this is why the well-known Sydney rat-lungworm case and paediatric
  Balamuthia cases are left out). Real cases get zero jokes in the fact lines; the Doug beat comes after, about Doug.
- **Stigma:** neurocysticercosis is a disease of poverty and poor sanitation. **No jokes about countries, cultures, food traditions or hygiene.** The joke
  is only Doug's useless salad.
- **Kid-appeal risk (medium):** three costumes (fish, ant, sheep) in a row could tip into nursery tone. The narrator stays dry; no mascot voices, no
  cute framing, no cartoon-animal "friends" with faces beyond the standard library style.
- **Animal welfare:** the sheep, horse and killifish are never shown suffering. The sheep walks in circles; the horse looks sympathetic.
- **Facts that need strong sources (cut if unsourced):** killifish cyst counts and the predation multiplier (Lafferty & Morris 1996); lancet fluke's
  temperature-linked clamping (Martín-Vega 2018); gid cyst size and the circling behaviour; rat lungworm's brain-to-lung route and "most recover" (CDC);
  the WHO "about 30% of epilepsy" figure and the egg-not-pork transmission (WHO/CDC); the Spirometra 5 cm / 4 years and first genome (Bennett 2014);
  Gnathostoma CNS fatality; Halicephalobus human case count and outcomes; Balamuthia's ~90% fatality, 1986 mandrill discovery and soil/skin route (CDC).
- **Myths to avoid:** "Toxoplasma makes people reckless" (not an item, and the human evidence is contested; don't mention it); "eating undercooked pork
  puts tapeworms in your brain" (wrong, and the episode corrects it); "brain-eating amoebas come from tap water" (Naegleria nuance; it is only a callback,
  so say nothing about water sources); "Halicephalobus is spreading" (it isn't; it is rare). Every rate is "about" or a range.
- **Duplication:** don't reuse quack doc's or Bacterium's items, wording or order. Our twists carry the video: the fish is dancing for a heron, the ant has
  a night shift, the cyst steers by taking up room, the brain was a layover, it's someone else's tapeworm, a traveller on the scans, the worm whose trip ends
  in the brain, the worm that doesn't need a partner, and the slow cousin in ordinary dirt.
