## Killifish Fluke

Killifish Fluke.

In the salt marshes of California lives a small silver fish called the California killifish, and its entire job is to not be noticed by anything with a beak.

[A small silver fish hiding in murky marsh water. Label: "KILLIFISH".]

This fluke starts life inside a horn snail on the mudflats, then its larvae swim off, push their way into a killifish through its skin, and head straight for the brain.

They settle on its surface in tiny cysts. Wild fish usually carry hundreds, and the most heavily infected fish carry more than eight thousand.

That is more parasites in one small fish's head than there are seats in London's Royal Albert Hall.

[A cartoon fish head outline filling with tiny dots. Inset: a concert hall, label "5,272 SEATS".]

Here's the strange part. The fish isn't sick. It's dancing.

Infected killifish swim up near the surface, flash their silver sides, shimmy and jerk, and in one study they made these showy moves about four times as often as healthy fish.

And the audience is a heron.

In a field study from nineteen ninety-six, infected fish ended up eaten by birds somewhere between ten and thirty times more often than healthy ones.

[A long-legged bird shadow on the water. Red arrow from the fish to the bird.]

The bird is where the fluke wanted to go all along, because it grows up inside the bird, and its eggs make their way back out to the mudflat and the snails.

This fluke finishes its life in birds and has no use for people, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a killifish.

[Doug in a killifish costume, red cap on top, deadpan.]

Doug the killifish swims up to the surface, does a little shimmy, and is quietly pleased with himself.

A shadow passes over the water.

[Only the red cap floats on the surface. DOUG DEATHS: 115.]

The fluke never hunted anything. It just got Doug to audition for the heron.

## Lancet Liver Fluke

Lancet Liver Fluke.

Doug has worn the ant costume before, and last time a fungus took the ant and kept it. This fluke is more polite, because it gives the ant back every morning.

[Doug zipping up the ant costume, cap on top. Small inset: a callback icon of the fungus ant from episode two.]

The lancet liver fluke wants to end up in the liver of a grazing animal, like a sheep or a cow, and getting there is the hard part.

The adult fluke is six to ten millimeters long, about the length of a grain of rice, and a heavily infected sheep can carry up to fifty thousand of them.

It starts in a land snail, which releases the young flukes in little balls of slime. Ants eat the slime, and most of the flukes settle down quietly in the ant's abdomen.

One or a few do not. They move into a nerve cluster in the ant's head.

That's the driver.

[Ant outline with a tiny dot in its head. Label: "DRIVER".]

When the air cools in the evening, the infected ant climbs to the top of a blade of grass, bites down with its jaws, and locks in place. It hangs there head down, waiting for something big to eat the grass.

But that's not the disturbing part. It isn't a zombie. It has a night shift.

Researchers in Denmark individually tagged infected ants and followed them in the field, and almost every ant that was attached in the evening was still attached the following morning.

Then, as the temperature rose during the day, the jaws unlocked, and the ant climbed down and returned to ordinary ant behavior, as if nothing had happened.

The fluke runs it like a thermostat. Every single degree Celsius of warming cut the number of clinging ants by about eleven percent, so cold switches it on and warmth switches it off.

[A thermometer annotation beside the grass blade, the ant going up and down with the temperature.]

The researchers think this protects the ant from the midday heat, which could kill it long before any cow arrived, and a dead ant is completely useless to the fluke.

[Night one: Doug the ant clamps on. Morning: climbs down, fine. Night two: the same. Night three: a cow's shadow.]

On the first night, Doug clamps on, and in the morning he climbs down completely fine. The second night goes the same way. On the third night, a cow arrives.

[The red cap left on the tip of a grass blade. DOUG DEATHS: 116.]

The fluke was never in a hurry. It only needed one cool evening with a cow in it.

## Gid Tapeworm

Gid Tapeworm.

Sheep farmers have several names for what this worm does, including gid, sturdy, and staggers, and none of them are good news for the sheep.

[Doug in a sheep costume, cap on top, standing in a grass-gold field.]

The adult tapeworm lives in the gut of dogs and their wild relatives, and it can grow up to a meter long in there.

Its eggs end up on the grass, and a grazing sheep swallows them without ever knowing.

The larvae travel through the bloodstream to the brain, where one of them develops into a cyst filled with fluid, and then the cyst gradually starts to grow.

When it is fully grown, it can be five to six centimeters across, which is bigger than a golf ball, sitting inside a sheep's head.

[A cartoon sheep head outline with a growing circle inside. A golf ball beside it for scale.]

Here's the problem. The cyst doesn't need to eat anything. It just takes up room.

As it grows, the pressure inside the skull increases, and the sheep begins to stumble, hold its head at an unusual angle, and walk in slow circles.

Vets have even used the direction of the circling and the head tilt to guess which side of the head the cyst is on. In one study of sheep in Ethiopia, that guess was right about six times out of ten.

[Overhead view: a dotted circle path in the grass. Red "?" over one side of the sheep's head.]

The worm's life only carries on if a dog eats the infected sheep's head, because a dog only starts spreading the eggs after eating an infected brain. So the whole plan is a loop of sheep, dog, grass, and sheep again.

In people, this worm is extremely rare. In sheep, it is not. Which is why Doug is dressed as a sheep.

[Doug the sheep walking a slow, perfect circle that ends at the field's only sheepdog.]

Doug walks a slow and extremely tidy circle across the field, which happens to finish directly in front of the field's only sheepdog.

[Speech bubble: "hi friend?" Cut. DOUG DEATHS: 117.]

The cyst never moved an inch. It just made sure the sheep did all the walking.

Lives in you.

## Rat Lungworm

Rat Lungworm.

The costume is off, so everything from here onward works on Doug exactly as he is. The name of this parasite tells you where it intends to live, and it is not the brain.

The adult worms live in the arteries of a rat's lungs, their larvae are released from the rat, and slugs and snails eventually pick them up.

When a rat eats one of those slugs, the larvae travel through its circulation to the brain, develop there for a while, and then move on to the lungs to finish the job.

At this stage a larva is about half a millimeter long, which is roughly the size of a grain of sand.

[A slug on a leaf. A tiny dot beside it, label "0.5 mm". Dotted path: rat head, then rat lungs.]

People pick it up by eating raw or undercooked snails or slugs, or a tiny slug hiding on raw produce. Frogs, crabs and freshwater shrimp can carry the larvae too. Most cases come from Southeast Asia and the Pacific islands, and it has also turned up in Hawaii and, more recently, the southern United States.

The worst part is what happens next in a human.

The larvae head for the brain exactly as they would in a rat, but a human is the wrong host, so they can't finish growing and never move on to the lungs. They die where they are.

The body reacts with a particular type of meningitis, and people notice a severe headache, a stiff neck, and unusual tingling sensations in the skin.

[Doug's head outline with a tiny worm icon, a red "?" over it.]

It's a lungworm. The brain was supposed to be a layover, and in a human it never finds the exit.

Most people recover on their own, although the symptoms can continue for several weeks or even months while the immune system deals with the dying parasites.

[Doug in a garden. A small slug on his lettuce. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug discovers a small slug on his garden lettuce and waves at it enthusiastically. A week later there is a headache, a stiff neck, and a lot of time in bed.

[Doug in bed, flat-unimpressed, ice pack on his head. Counter stays at 117.]

Doug is fine. Mostly. The worm is not, and it never planned to stay this long.

## Pork Tapeworm

Pork Tapeworm.

Most people think they understand how this one works: undercooked pork, a worm, and then the brain. That story is incorrect in one important place.

[A pork chop icon with an arrow to a head outline. A red X crosses out the arrow.]

If you eat undercooked pork that contains the larval cysts, you get the adult tapeworm in your intestine, where it lives quietly and usually causes very few symptoms.

The brain disease is something else entirely. It's called neurocysticercosis, and it comes from swallowing the tapeworm's eggs.

Those eggs come from a person who carries the adult worm, and they spread through contaminated food, water, or hands.

[An egg icon traveling from one stick figure to another. No detail.]

Once they are swallowed, the eggs hatch, the larvae penetrate the intestinal wall into the bloodstream, and over about two months they develop into cysts in the body's tissues. Some of those cysts end up in the brain.

Most of them are between half a centimeter and one and a half centimeters across, so smaller than a grape. Infected people may have no symptoms at all for many years.

The World Health Organization estimates that somewhere between about two and a half million and eight million people are living with it.

Here's what makes it worse. The World Health Organization calls this the most common preventable cause of epilepsy in the world, and in many places where the worm is common, it is behind about thirty percent of epilepsy cases.

That's nearly one in three.

[A row of three head outlines. One has a small cyst icon. Label: "1 IN 3".]

For the person, it usually shows up as seizures, sometimes years after the eggs were swallowed, and the World Health Organization notes that it can be fatal.

So here is the twist. Eating the undercooked pork gives you the worm in your gut, but the cysts in your brain come from someone else's worm.

[Doug at a café table with a salad. A sign on his chest: "VEGETARIAN".]

Doug has become a vegetarian, just to be safe, and he orders a salad and feels extremely responsible about it.

[A cyst icon appears in Doug's head outline. X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 118.]

Doug gave up pork for nothing. It was never his tapeworm in the first place.

## Spirometra

Spirometra.

This one comes from a real medical case, and it is the only item on this list where the doctors were able to watch the parasite travel.

In two thousand and eight, a man in the United Kingdom went to his doctors with headaches, seizures, and memory problems. His scans showed a small ring-shaped spot in his brain that nobody could explain.

[A plain cartoon head outline with a small ring icon. Not a real scan.]

Over the next four years he had repeated scans, and every time the spot was visible again, but somewhere different. It started on the right side, passed through the middle, and continued.

It crossed from one side of his brain to the other, a distance of at least five centimeters.

Five centimeters in four years works out to about a millimeter a month, and your fingernails grow more than three times faster than that.

[A fingernail and a ruler side by side. Label: "3x FASTER".]

The strangest part is what it turned out to be. Not a tumor. A traveler.

In twenty twelve, doctors finally removed it. It was a tapeworm larva about one centimeter long, from a species so rarely seen that this was the first case of its kind ever recorded in the United Kingdom.

Scientists then sequenced its entire genome, the first ever for this species, and at the time it was the largest genome reported for any flatworm.

People usually catch it by eating undercooked frog or snake, or by swallowing tiny infected crustaceans in untreated water. One review counted about three hundred cases worldwide between nineteen fifty-three and two thousand and three.

The man recovered.

[Doug's scans as a flipbook: a tiny worm-dot with a tiny suitcase walks across Doug's head outline, one frame per year.]

Doug's scans become a flipbook, with a tiny dot carrying a tiny suitcase strolling across his head one year at a time, until it is eventually removed.

[DOUG DEATHS ticks to 119, pauses, then ticks back to 118.]

Doug is fine. The counter had already been updated, and it has now been changed back, with a sigh.

## Gnathostoma

Gnathostoma.

The last worm wandered around inside the brain. This one wanders through the whole body first, and it can take years to get anywhere near your head.

[A tiny worm icon with walking boots, a dotted line trailing behind it.]

People usually get it from raw or undercooked freshwater fish, although frogs and chicken can carry it too.

In its natural animal hosts, the larva develops into an adult worm, but in a human it can't, so instead it migrates.

The larva is about three millimeters long, which is roughly half the length of a grain of long-grain rice.

[A grain of rice with the larva beside it for scale.]

Most of the time it travels around underneath the skin, and people notice a swelling that appears, disappears, and reappears somewhere different each time.

It can continue this migration through the body's soft tissue for as long as ten years, so the trouble can begin long after the meal that started it.

The worst part is where some of them go next. A larva can follow a nerve into the spinal cord and then climb all the way up to the brain, and one patient's repeat scans in Thailand showed that climb taking several years.

That's where it gets serious. In published case series, between about seven and twenty-five percent of people with this brain form died. In one large group of patients, about a third either died or were left with lasting disability.

In one review, two hundred and forty-one of two hundred and forty-eight cases of the brain form came from Thailand. But doctors have also seen it in travelers returning to Europe, and they expect more cases in Europe and North America as people travel more.

[A world map with a dotted travel line out of Southeast Asia.]

It was never really aiming for your head. It just keeps moving, and the brain is simply where the trip ends.

[A dotted line wanders around Doug's body outline like a lost tourist on a map, loops twice, then ends at the head. X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 119.]

Doug never left home. The worm did all the sightseeing.

Eats you.

## Halicephalobus

Halicephalobus.

This is a worm that isn't supposed to be a parasite at all, because it normally lives in soil, compost, and manure, eating things that are already rotting.

[A pile of compost under a small horse-shaped shadow at the edge of the frame.]

It was first described in nineteen fifty-four, from a lump in the gums of a horse in Poland, and it has been appearing in horses periodically ever since.

It's tiny. The adults are less than half a millimeter long, so you could comfortably fit two of them inside the smallest division on a ruler.

[A ruler, zoomed to the first millimeter. Two tiny worm icons inside it.]

Every so often it gets into a horse, and when it does, it most often ends up in the brain, the skin or the kidneys.

Nobody is completely sure how it enters the body, and the leading explanation is contamination of a skin wound or the inside of the mouth. According to a review of the reported cases, its life cycle and the risk factors for infection are still poorly understood.

Here's the part that should bother you. Inside a host, scientists have only ever found females, young worms, and eggs, and never a single male.

That's because it doesn't need a mate, and it can reproduce entirely on its own.

So it doesn't need a partner, a second host, or a plan. It just keeps making more of itself.

[One tiny worm icon becomes two, then four, then eight. Label: "NO MATE NEEDED".]

Of all the reported horse cases, only four horses are known to have survived.

It can infect people too, although it is extremely rare. A review in twenty seventeen listed seven human cases between nineteen seventy-five and twenty fifteen, from Canada, the United States, Australia and Germany.

Every one of them was fatal.

[A plain card: "7 HUMAN CASES". No Doug, no joke. Hold.]

[Doug in a paddock, smiling, reaching to pat a horse. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug affectionately pats a horse, and the horse looks back at him with something very close to sympathy.

[X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 120. The horse, unharmed, looks down at the cap.]

It never chose Doug. It was already living in the dirt beside the horse, and the dirt was close enough.

## Balamuthia

Balamuthia.

In nineteen eighty-six, a mandrill at the San Diego Wild Animal Park died of a brain infection that nobody could explain.

[Background near-black. A cartoon mandrill silhouette with a red glow, then the reveal: a plain, small amoeba icon.]

The cause turned out to be an amoeba that no one had ever described before. When it was formally named in nineteen ninety-three, the first half honored a parasitologist, William Balamuth, and the second half honored the animal it was found in: Balamuthia mandrillaris.

It lives in soil, dust, and water, and it gets into the body through cuts and skin wounds, or when someone breathes in dust that contains it.

It's very small, and even the biggest ones are only about as wide as a fine human hair.

[A hair strand drawn across the frame, with the amoeba icon sitting on it for scale.]

Once it reaches the brain, it causes a rare type of encephalitis. It doesn't only go after people who are already sick, either. Many of the patients outside the United States were otherwise healthy.

It also doesn't spread from person to person in everyday life, which somehow isn't very reassuring.

Doug has met its cousin before, on the first parasite list: the brain-eating amoeba from the warm lake, the one that kills in days. Balamuthia is the slow cousin, it lives in garden soil, and it takes its time.

[A callback inset: the lake from episode two, then a hard cut to a garden bed.]

And that's the most disturbing part.

It can feel mild at first, and then, over weeks or months, it gets harder and harder to walk and to speak.

Because it progresses so slowly and resembles so many other illnesses, it is extremely difficult to identify, and most cases are only diagnosed just before the person dies, or afterwards.

Nine out of ten people with this brain infection do not survive. That's a slightly smaller percentage than the worm before it, but a far bigger number. The worm has seven known human cases in forty years. In the United States alone, this amoeba has one hundred and fifty-five, diagnosed between nineteen seventy-four and twenty twenty-five.

The fast one at least lets you know it's there. This one takes weeks, and it lives in ordinary dirt.

Doug is gardening, and he is having the most wonderful afternoon of his life. There is a tiny scratch on the back of his hand, and a flowerpot full of perfectly ordinary soil.

The cap was fine. It's the only thing on this list that never had a brain to lose.
