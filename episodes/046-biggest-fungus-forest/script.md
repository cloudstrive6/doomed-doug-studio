## The Corpse Flower

The Corpse Flower.

[HOW BIG: 1 M]

Most flowers are the decoration on top of a plant, but this one is the only part of the plant that you will ever see.

Its name is Rafflesia arnoldii, from the rainforests of Sumatra, and a single bloom can weigh up to eleven kilograms. Laid flat on the forest floor, it is wider than a car tyre.

The smell is the whole strategy, because it pulls in carrion flies, which crawl over the bloom the way they would crawl over a carcass and then carry its pollen to the next flower.

Here's the disturbing part. There is no plant underneath it.

The rest of Rafflesia lives as thin strands of cells inside a jungle vine called Tetrastigma. The strands are threaded so closely through the vine's tissue that, under a microscope, it is hard to tell whose cells are whose. When the parasite is ready, a bud pushes out through the vine's bark, swells up and opens.

Scientists studying a close relative could not even find a working chloroplast genome, the set of genes plants use to make food from sunlight. It doesn't need sunlight. It has a vine.

[Doug zipped into a Tetrastigma vine costume, cap on top]

Rafflesia only grows inside that one kind of vine and never in people, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a vine today. Doug did not agree to this.

[The bud opens on his costume. The flies arrive.]

Doug is fine, although he smells like it now.

That one found its host and moved in. The next one has to go looking, and it hunts by smell.

## The Dodder

The Dodder.

[HOW BIG: A FEW M]

Dodder is a vine with no leaves, and it spends its whole adult life wrapped around somebody else. It looks like a handful of orange string that someone spilled over a hedge.

A dodder seedling has only a few days to find a plant to feed on before it starves, and it cannot see. So it smells.

In two thousand six, researchers at Penn State showed that dodder seedlings grow toward the scent of a tomato plant. Offered a tomato or some wheat, they leaned toward the tomato, and one chemical in the wheat's scent actually pushed them away. It is like walking into a kitchen with your eyes shut and following your nose to the right pan.

Once it touches a stem, it winds itself around it and pushes feeding pegs called haustoria into the host's plumbing. After that, its own root dies off, because it doesn't need the ground anymore. It has a tomato.

But that's not the unsettling part. In two thousand fourteen, a team found that dodder and its hosts swap messenger RNA in enormous numbers, in both directions, and those are the working copies of genes. The parasite and the plant are reading each other's notes.

[Doug in a tomato costume, cap on top, beside a Doug-sized wheat stalk decoy]

Dodder only feeds on plants, so today Doug is a tomato, and next to him, as a precaution, stands some wheat.

[The seedling sways, ignores the wheat. Orange threads wrap the costume. Cut to the flat costume with the cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 351]

Doug has died. Again.

The next one doesn't need a costume, because it lives in people.

## The Tapeworm

The Tapeworm.

[HOW BIG: 10 M]

The broad fish tapeworm can grow to more than ten meters long inside a person, and most people who carry one never notice it.

Its name is Dibothriocephalus latus, and people pick it up from raw or undercooked freshwater fish, like perch and pike.

Once it settles in, it grows into a flat ribbon of more than three thousand segments. The CDC puts the adults at anywhere from two to fifteen meters, with the occasional longer one. Ten meters is about the length of a city bus, packed into a single person, and an infection can last for up to twenty-five years.

Here's the catch. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, most infections cause no symptoms at all. You can share your life with a lodger the length of a bus for decades, and feel nothing in particular.

[Costume off. Doug at a dubious stall with a plate of raw lake fish. He eats.]

The costume is off for this one, because this time the host is supposed to be a person, and Doug eats the fish.

[The HOW BIG tape measure unrolls out of frame. The camera follows it into the next room.]

Doug is fine. The narrator had a death prepared for this one and is quietly disappointed.

[Doug looks at the tape measure: "roommate?"]

That was ten meters of something hiding inside one person, and the next one hides under an entire field.

Under the ground.

## The Cable-Cutting Tree

The Cable-Cutting Tree.

[HOW BIG: OVER 100 M (estimate)]

In Western Australia, there is a tree that has been caught attacking underground telephone cables.

It's Nuytsia floribunda, the Western Australian Christmas tree, and every summer, right around Christmas, its crown turns into a mass of golden-orange flowers.

It looks like a normal tree. It isn't. It is a parasite, and it does all of its stealing underground, where nobody is watching.

Its roots spread for tens of meters, and some reports put them up to a hundred and ten meters from the trunk. That is longer than a football pitch, reaching under a whole field to sip from the neighbours. Whenever one of those roots touches another plant's root, it grows a collar of tissue around it, and a blade forms inside the collar. The blade cuts into the root, and the tree drinks the sap from grasses, weeds, shrubs, gum trees and almost anything else that is green.

The worst part is that it doesn't check what it has grabbed. Its collars close around buried cables too. Botanists have found it damaging underground telephone lines, and power has failed when it went after buried cables by mistake.

[Doug, no costume, lying in the grass on a phone call, holding the cable]

For this one, Doug doesn't need a costume, just a phone and a nice patch of grass.

[The collar closes on the cable. "hello?" Cut to black on the dial tone. DOUG DEATHS: 352]

The tree only went for the cable. Doug was holding it.

That tree is one plant pretending to be a normal tree, and the next one is one plant pretending to be a forest.

## Pando

Pando.

[HOW BIG: 43 HECTARES]

About forty-seven thousand aspen trunks in Utah are all one single living thing.

It's a quaking aspen in Fishlake National Forest, nicknamed the Trembling Giant, and every trunk sprouted from the same root system.

It covers forty-three hectares, which is about sixty football pitches of what looks like a wood and is really one tree. The Forest Service puts it at nearly thirteen million pounds, about six thousand tonnes, and believes it is the largest organism ever found.

When an old trunk dies, the root sends up new shoots to replace it, and that is how it has kept itself going for a very long time.

Here's the problem. The new shoots are being eaten.

A two thousand eighteen study found that mule deer browse the young shoots so heavily that big parts of Pando have stopped replacing their dead trunks. Inside one fence that kept the deer out, young aspens came up thick and fast. Inside a second fence, the deer got in anyway, and the new stems were eaten right back down.

The heaviest living thing we know of is losing to deer.

[Doug in an aspen sprout costume, cap on top, the newest stem in the clone]

Doug is the newest stem, because it's a family business.

[A mule deer walks over and eats the sprout. The cap drops onto the bitten stump. DOUG DEATHS: 353]

Pando is not a parasite, it's a victim. Something else under the ground is also eating trees, and it spreads across far more of the forest.

Under a forest.

## The Humongous Fungus

The Humongous Fungus.

[HOW BIG: 75 HECTARES]

In Michigan's Upper Peninsula, one fungus weighs about four hundred tonnes, and nearly all of it is underground.

That is close to three blue whales of honey fungus, Armillaria gallica, spread out under about seventy-five hectares of forest.

In nineteen ninety-two, scientists announced that it was one single individual covering at least thirty-seven hectares. That made headlines. It was also an underestimate.

It travels through the soil on black cords called rhizomorphs, which look a lot like bootlaces. They grow the way roots do, hunting for dead wood and weakened trees to feed on, and wherever they find food, the fungus spreads. Wherever it spreads, it is still the same individual.

The strange part came when scientists went back. Between two thousand fifteen and two thousand seventeen, a team collected hundreds of samples and sequenced its genome. It covered twice the area they had first measured, weighed about four times as much, and was at least two thousand five hundred years old. Across all of that growth, its DNA had barely changed.

[Doug in a red oak costume, cap on top. Black bootlaces creep toward his roots.]

Doug is a tree for this one, and the bootlaces find him.

[Mould death: fuzzy white Doug silhouette, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 354]

Doug has been mould before. It went about the same.

And this is the gentle one, because its cousin out west goes after living trees.

## The Glowing Forest

The Glowing Forest.

[HOW BIG: ABOUT 600 HECTARES]

In nineteen ninety-two, foresters in Washington State announced a single fungus covering about six hundred hectares, or six square kilometers of forest.

It's Armillaria ostoyae, in the foothills south of Mount Adams, and its age was put at somewhere between four hundred and a thousand years.

Unlike the one in Michigan, this species doesn't wait for trees to weaken. It kills conifers.

Its bootlaces creep through the soil until they touch a living root. From there, the fungus spreads up under the bark as a white sheet, like a layer of dried paint glued between the bark and the wood. The tree leaks resin and its roots rot away, so it dies from the bottom up, while the fungus moves through the touching roots to the next tree. What it leaves behind are gaps in the forest where the trees used to be.

And now the eerie part. The threads of honey fungus give off a very faint light called foxfire. It is a weak greenish glow, so dim that you need to sit in the dark for fifteen or twenty minutes before you can see it.

Some nights, the forest is lit from underneath by the thing that is killing it.

[Night. Doug in a pine costume, cap on top, wakes up. The ground around his roots is glowing faintly.]

Doug wakes up as a pine tree in the middle of the night. The ground around his roots is glowing, and he takes it as a welcome.

[Doug: "hi friend?" Mould death, glowing faintly, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 355]

Six hundred hectares was the record, but it did not last very long.

## The Blue Mountains Fungus

The Blue Mountains Fungus.

[HOW BIG: ABOUT 965 HECTARES, stamped red]

The biggest fungus ever found covers nine hundred and sixty-five hectares of Oregon, and you could walk across all of it without ever seeing it.

It's another Armillaria ostoyae, in the Blue Mountains of Malheur National Forest, and nine hundred and sixty-five hectares is about one thousand three hundred and fifty football pitches.

When it was found in nineteen ninety-eight, it took the record straight off Washington.

Nobody could see its edges, so scientists sampled them. They grew fungus from different spots in dishes, checked which samples fused together as one, and then used DNA fingerprinting to find where one individual ended and the next began. Its age comes from how fast it grows, which puts it at least two thousand four hundred years old, and maybe as much as eight thousand six hundred and fifty. Across that whole area, it does exactly what its cousin does. It spreads from root to root, and it kills the conifers.

Here's the part nobody can see. From the ground, it is just a forest with dead and dying trees in it, because the fungus itself is under the bark and in the soil. After the first rains each autumn, clumps of honey mushrooms come up at the base of the trees it has infected, and that is the only part of it that ever shows.

To be fair, it is not the biggest living thing on Earth. A single seagrass in Shark Bay, Australia, has spread across about two hundred square kilometers of meadow, roughly twenty times the area. But this is the biggest fungus anyone has ever found.

[Doug in a Douglas fir costume, cap on top, in the middle of grey, dying trees]

Doug is dressed as a Douglas fir, and nobody here is proud of that.

[Calendar pages flip. Years pass. Doug stands still.]

He stands in the middle of it for years, perfectly calm, and only gets nervous one autumn, when honey mushrooms come up around his feet.

[The costume tips over with no sound. The cap rolls into the mushroom cluster. DOUG DEATHS: 356]

It has been eating this forest for thousands of years. It has not noticed Doug.

[Closing: night, aerial view of the forest with the dead patch outlined. The cap sits on a honey mushroom cluster with a faint foxfire glow under it.]

Doug deaths: three hundred and fifty-six. The fungus is still there. Tell us which one you would least like to stand on.
