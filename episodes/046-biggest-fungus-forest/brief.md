## 2. Escalation axis and items
**Axis: size of a single living thing, small to enormous, where the more of it there is, the more of it is hidden.** Every item is one individual
organism, and its real body lives inside a host or under the ground. The visible part shrinks as the hidden part grows: a 1 m flower, then a
forest you only see as dead trees. Three spoken bands, each said once as a two-word sentence when we enter it: **"Inside something." / "Under the
ground." / "Under a forest."**
**Episode device: HOW BIG**, a crude MS Paint tape measure beside the death counter that only grows: 1 M, A FEW M, 10 M, OVER 100 M (estimate),
43 HECTARES, 75 HECTARES, ABOUT 600 HECTARES, ABOUT 965 HECTARES. The last value is stamped red. It is staging only and never read aloud, and it uses
only the sourced figures (round them the way the source does).
**Costume gag** (series bible, from 002/017/021): most of these things cannot infect people, so Doug is zipped into a costume of the host. The cap
always sits on top. The "can't infect people" line is said once, at item 1, and varied after that. The costume comes off for item 3 (the one that
does live in people) and goes back on for the trees.

Deaths go **351 to 356** (6 deaths; items 1 and 3 are survivals). Eight items, the most extreme last.

### Inside something.
**1. The Corpse Flower** (*Rafflesia arnoldii*; one of the three strongest, the cold-open item; survival)
- Facts to research: the largest single flower known, about 1 m across and up to about 10-11 kg (cite a botanic garden or a review). It has no
  leaves, no stem and no roots, and lives as thread-like strands inside a *Tetrastigma* vine. Only the flower ever comes out. The carrion smell
  draws flies as pollinators. Bonus twist if sourced: *Rafflesia* (Molina et al. 2014, *R. lagascae*) has lost most or all of its chloroplast
  genome, so it is one of the few plants that has given up on photosynthesis entirely.
- **Cold-open fact (sourced):** "The biggest flower on Earth is about a meter wide, smells like a dead animal, and has no leaves, no stem and no
  roots." Follow the source's wording on size.
- **Twist (by 0:45):** the flower is all of it you will ever see. The rest is threads inside a vine, and the vine doesn't know.
- Scale analogy: the flower is about the size of a car tyre lying flat (check against the sourced diameter).
- Doug beat: Doug in a *Tetrastigma* vine costume, cap on top ("Rafflesia can't grow in people, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a vine").
  The flower opens on him. The flies arrive. **Survival: "Doug is fine."** He smells like it. The narrator notes that the flies disagree about
  whether he is alive. "Doug did not agree to this." here, once.

**2. The Dodder** (*Cuscuta*; death 351)
- Facts to research: a leafless, nearly rootless parasitic vine that drops its root once it has latched on and feeds through haustoria pushed into
  the host's stem. Seedlings find hosts **by smell**: they grew toward tomato volatiles and away from wheat (Runyon, Mescher & De Moraes 2006,
  *Science*). One dodder plant can link several host plants at once, and mRNA moves between dodder and its hosts (Kim et al. 2014, *Science*).
  Common names "strangleweed" and "devil's hair" only if a source uses them.
- **Twist:** the plant without eyes picks the tastier neighbour.
- Scale analogy: a seedling sniffing the air is like walking into a kitchen and following the smell to the right pan.
- Doug beat: Doug in a tomato costume, next to a Doug-sized wheat stalk decoy. The dodder ignores the wheat. Death 351 (orange threads wrap the
  costume, then a cut to the flat costume with the cap on top). Re-hook: "The next one doesn't need a costume. It lives in people."

**3. The Tapeworm** (*Dibothriocephalus latus*, the broad fish tapeworm; survival)
- Facts to research: the longest tapeworm that infects people, reported at up to about 10 m or more (CDC; give the CDC figure exactly). People get
  it from raw or undercooked freshwater fish. Most infections cause mild or no symptoms (CDC). Twist: the record holders live in whales. Tapeworms
  reported at about 30 m in sperm whales, and *Placentonema gigantissima*, a nematode up to about 8.4 m in sperm-whale placentas (verify both;
  cut what can't be sourced).
- **Twist:** you can carry ten metres of something and not notice.
- Scale analogy: ten metres is a city bus parked inside one stick man (keep it as a tape measure gag on screen, not anatomy).
- Doug beat: Doug, no costume, eats sushi from a dubious stall. The HOW BIG tape measure unrolls out of frame, the narrator follows it to the
  next room. **Survival** (the honest one, per CDC). The narrator is quietly disappointed, and Doug asks "roommate?". **No cooking or food-safety
  advice**, no medical advice, no internal anatomy, no worm emerging from anything. A cartoon tape measure is the only visual.

### Under the ground.
**4. The Christmas Tree That Cuts Cables** (*Nuytsia floribunda*, Western Australia; death 352)
- Facts to research: a tree that is a root hemiparasite. Its roots spread a long way from the trunk (sourced distance only), and it clamps ring-like
  haustoria onto the roots of almost any plant nearby, including grasses. The haustorium has a hard, blade-like tissue that cuts into the host root
  (Calladine & Pate 2000, or a Kings Park / Australian National Botanic Gardens source). It is known for cutting into **underground power and
  telephone cables** (cite a utility or botanic source). It flowers bright orange around Christmas.
- **Twist:** it can't tell a root from a phone line, and it doesn't try.
- Scale analogy: a tree reaching under a football pitch to sip from its neighbours (adjust to the sourced root distance).
- Doug beat: Doug lies in the grass on a phone call, holding the cable. The haustorium closes on the cable. The call drops ("hello?"). Death 352,
  cut to black on the dial tone. The field note says it only went for the cable, and Doug was holding it.

**5. Pando** (the Trembling Giant, Utah; the giant is the victim; death 353)
- Facts to research: one male quaking-aspen clone, about 43 ha and about 47,000 stems sharing one root system, often given as roughly 6,000 t
  (USDA Forest Service / Fishlake National Forest; follow the source's wording on "heaviest"). It is not a parasite. The twist is that it is being
  eaten: mule deer and cattle browse the new shoots, so parts of it have stopped regenerating, and fencing is helping (Rogers & McAvoy 2018,
  *PLOS ONE*; Rogers & Gale 2017).
- **Twist:** the heaviest living thing we know of is losing to deer.
- Scale analogy: 43 ha is about 60 football pitches of what looks like a forest and is one tree.
- Doug beat: Doug in an aspen-sprout costume, the newest stem in the clone ("it's a family business"). A mule deer eats the sprout. Death 353,
  played flat. Kicker and re-hook: "Something else under the ground is also eating trees, and it's much bigger."

### Under a forest.
**6. The Humongous Fungus** (*Armillaria gallica*, Crystal Falls, Michigan; death 354)
- Facts to research: discovered as a single genetic individual in 1992 (Smith, Bruhn & Anderson, *Nature*). Remeasured in 2018: at least about
  75 ha, about 400 t and at least about 2,500 years old, with a surprisingly low mutation rate (Anderson et al. 2018, *Proc. R. Soc. B*). It spreads
  through the soil by black rhizomorphs ("bootlaces"). The town holds a "Humongous Fungus" festival (verify; one line only).
- **Twist:** the size was underestimated by about four times, and it barely changes its DNA as it grows.
- Scale analogy: 400 t is about the weight of three blue whales (check against a sourced blue-whale mass, or use a fleet of buses).
- Doug beat: Doug in a red-oak costume. Black bootlaces creep up to the roots. The mould death (series bible, from 017): a fuzzy white Doug
  silhouette with the cap on top. Death 354.

**7. The Bootlaces** (*Armillaria ostoyae*, near Mount Adams, Washington; death 355)
- Facts to research: about 600 ha (about 1,500 acres), reported in 1992 (Shaw & Roth; cite the original or a USFS summary). How *A. ostoyae* kills
  conifers: rhizomorphs reach a root, white mycelial fans spread under the bark, and the tree dies of root rot from the bottom up (USDA Forest
  Service *Armillaria* root disease guide). Armillaria mycelium and infected wood glow faintly in the dark ("foxfire"; cite a bioluminescence
  source).
- **Twist:** the forest is lit from underneath, faintly, by the thing killing it.
- Scale analogy: the white fan under the bark is like wallpaper glued between the tree and its own skin. (No skin imagery on screen. This is a
  wording idea only; switch to "between the bark and the wood" if the screener prefers.)
- Doug beat: Doug in a pine costume wakes up at night. The ground around his roots is glowing faintly, and he takes it as a welcome ("hi
  friend?"). Death 355 (mould death variant, glowing faintly).

**8. The Blue Mountains Fungus** (*Armillaria ostoyae*, Malheur National Forest, Oregon; the boss, death 356, played flat)
- Facts to research: about 965 ha (2,385 acres), mapped by sampling infected trees and matching their DNA. It was described in 2000 and its age is
  estimated at about 1,900-8,650 years (Ferguson et al. 2003, *Can. J. For. Res.*; USDA Forest Service). It kills conifers across the area by root
  disease, and from the air it shows as patches of dead and dying trees. Its mushrooms only appear for a few weeks in autumn. **Honesty line:** by
  area, the Shark Bay seagrass is bigger (Edgecombe et al. 2022), and "largest organism" claims are argued over, but this is the biggest fungus
  ever found.
- **Twist:** the biggest fungus ever found is mostly invisible. You can walk through the whole thing, and all you will see are dead trees.
- Scale analogy: about 965 ha is roughly 1,350 football pitches. Recompute from the source and pick a single comparison (pitches or "a small
  town").
- Doug beat: Doug in a Douglas-fir costume. The pun is said once, flat, and the narrator does not like it either. He stands in the middle of the
  forest for a cut of years (calendar pages), surrounded by grey trees, and only gets nervous when the honey mushrooms come up around his feet in
  autumn. Death 356, played flat: the costume tips over with no sound, and the cap rolls into the mushroom cluster.
- Closing: night, an aerial view of the forest with the dead patch outlined. The cap sits on a honey-mushroom cluster with a faint foxfire glow
  under it. Kicker idea: "It has been eating this forest since before anyone wrote anything down. It has not noticed Doug." Outro of 30 words or
  fewer: the death counter plus one CTA.

## 7. Risks
- **Gore / body horror:** low except at item 3. The tapeworm is a tape measure gag only: no intestines, no worm leaving a body, no symptoms. Deaths
  are a flat costume, cut to black, the mould silhouette or the cap left behind. No rot, no decay textures on Doug.
- **Medical / safety advice (never approve):** no "cook your fish to X degrees", no deworming or treatment talk, no "don't eat wild mushrooms"
  lines, and no statements about whether honey mushrooms are edible. Item 3 is a CDC statistic, not advice.
- **Kid appeal:** low to medium (flowers, trees, costumes). Keep the costumes deadpan with no mascot voices, no cute faces on plants or fungi, and
  no googly-eyed mushrooms. The jokes stay dry and adult-coded.
- **Overclaim risks:**
  - "Largest living thing" (contested: Posidonia, Pando by mass). Use "biggest fungus ever found".
  - Ages are estimates with wide ranges (give the range for Malheur, "at least" for Michigan).
  - Rafflesia size and weight (follow the source exactly).
  - Nuytsia root distance and cable cutting (needs a utility or botanic-garden source, not a listicle).
  - The whale tapeworm and *Placentonema* lengths (cut if only listicles report them).
  - Armillaria bioluminescence (cite it, or cut the glow and keep the closing on the mushrooms).
- **Facts that need strong sources:**
  - Rafflesia size, endoparasitic lifestyle and plastome loss (Molina et al. 2014).
  - Dodder host-finding by smell (Runyon et al. 2006) and mRNA exchange (Kim et al. 2014).
  - D. latus length and mostly mild symptoms (CDC DPDx / CDC parasites pages).
  - Nuytsia haustoria and cable damage.
  - Pando size and stem count, and the browsing decline (USFS; Rogers & McAvoy 2018).
  - A. gallica 1992 discovery and 2018 remeasure (Smith et al. 1992; Anderson et al. 2018).
  - A. ostoyae Washington 600 ha (Shaw & Roth 1992), and Malheur 965 ha with its age range (Ferguson et al. 2003).
  - Posidonia 200 km² (Edgecombe et al. 2022).
- **Pronunciation (editor):** *Rafflesia arnoldii* "ruh-FLEE-zee-uh ar-NOL-dee-eye", *Tetrastigma* "tet-ruh-STIG-muh", *Cuscuta* "kus-KYOO-tuh",
