## The Dagga Boy

The Dagga Boy.

[A mud wallow at midday. One old bull lies in it up to the shoulders, only the horns and the stare above the mud. Label: "DAGGA BOY".]

Safari guides will tell you that the buffalo to worry about is not the big herd, but the lone old bull lying in the mud.

[Field guide CAPE BUFFALO page. FRIEND? box empty.]

Guides call these bulls dagga boys, a name that comes from udaka, the Zulu word for mud, because they spend the hot part of the day lying in wallows. Old bulls are often seen caked in a crust of dried mud.

[The bull stands up. Dried mud cracks off him in plates. Red arrow: "DRIED MUD".]

The nickname Black Death comes from hunting stories, not from any measured ranking of dangerous animals. But the stories keep pointing at the same kind of buffalo.

Here's the strange part.

[A crude family photo of a breeding herd. One bull is walking out of the frame with his back turned. Label: "ABOUT 12 YEARS".]

The Black Death is mostly retired, because at around twelve years old, most bulls move away from the breeding herd and the safety that comes with it. They live alone, or in small bachelor groups of other old males, usually somewhere close to water and mud.

[The old bull glaring over a garden fence at nothing. Label: "GET OFF MY GRASS".]

Picture the retired neighbour who shouts at anyone who walks too close to his lawn. Now give him horns, a bad back, and the weight of a small car.

[Doug in a safari vest, camera up, tiptoeing toward the wallow. Speech bubble: "say cheese".]

Doug signed up for a photo safari. Doug did not agree to this. He crept right to the edge of the mud for a close-up, because the light was perfect and the bull looked asleep.

[Cut to black. The red cap floats alone on the mud hole. DOUG DEATHS: 397. Field guide: red "NO".]

Doug deaths: three hundred and ninety-seven. The photo came out blurry, and that bull is not finished with this episode yet.

## The Boss

One.

The Boss.

[Close-up of the bull's head. A thick ridge of horn runs across the forehead like a helmet. Label: "THE BOSS".]

A mature buffalo bull wears a helmet across his forehead, and he grew it himself.

[Diagram: two horn bases drawn as separate lumps, then thicker, then fused into one shield.]

As a young bull gets older, the bases of his horns thicken and spread until they meet in the middle of his head. The result is one continuous shield over the top of the skull, solid bone covered in horn, and it is called the boss. On a big bull, the tips of the horns can be more than a metre apart.

[A small two-seat city car on a weighing scale next to the bull. Readings: bull "800+ KG", car "ABOUT 730 KG". The car wears a crash helmet.]

A big bull can weigh more than eight hundred kilograms. That is heavier than a small two-seat city car, if the car were wearing a crash helmet and had opinions.

But that's not the interesting part.

[A calendar flips through years. The boss shades from grey to solid black. Labels: "SOFT", then "HARD: 8 TO 9 YEARS".]

The helmet takes years to finish. A bull's horns are fully grown by about five or six, but the boss does not become hard until he is around eight or nine years old. So the pensioner from a minute ago is the one wearing the finished version.

[Doug halfway up an acacia tree, holding on with both arms. The bull lies at the bottom, chewing, looking up. A page-a-day calendar on the branch flips.]

Doug saw the boss coming and went straight up the nearest tree. The bull lay down underneath it and began to chew, with no plans to be anywhere else.

[Field guide: "MAYBE". DOUG DEATHS stays at 397.]

Doug is fine. Doug has a new address, and it is a branch.

On the third day the bull wandered off, and it turned out he had been carrying passengers the whole time.

## The Hitchhikers

The Hitchhikers.

[A buffalo's back with five small brown birds clinging to it, beaks bright red and yellow. Label: "OXPECKERS".]

The birds riding around on a buffalo's back may not be working for the buffalo at all.

[A bird pulls a tick off the hide. A green tick mark appears beside it.]

They are oxpeckers, red-billed and yellow-billed, and they clamber over large African mammals picking off ticks and other parasites. In one study in Kruger, yellow-billed oxpeckers were only ever seen feeding on Cape buffalo. From a distance, it looks like a very good cleaning service.

The worst part is the menu.

[The green tick mark turns into a red question mark. A cattle pen in Zimbabwe, half the cows behind a net with no birds.]

Oxpeckers also eat earwax, dead skin and wound tissue, and they are known to drink blood. When a scientist kept the birds away from cattle in Zimbabwe, the tick numbers did not change, but the wounds on those cattle healed faster without them.

[A clipboard: "KRUGER: 6 WOUND FEEDS IN 855". Nothing is drawn on the hide.]

In the wild, the picture is less clear, because the Kruger study saw wound feeding only six times in eight hundred and fifty-five observations. But the birds are always there, and the buffalo lets them stay.

[A cleaner in an apron dusting a shelf, then sipping from a tiny red juice box.]

Imagine hiring a cleaner who tidies the whole house and then, now and then, has a little sip of you.

[Doug lying flat in the grass, holding a small sign: "BUFFALO". An oxpecker lands on his chest, walks up to his face, inspects his ear, looks at the camera, and leaves.]

Doug lay down in the grass to blend in with the herd. An oxpecker landed on him, checked him from his boots to his cap, found no ticks, and flew away.

[Field guide: "MAYBE". DOUG DEATHS stays at 397.]

Doug is fine. Doug was not worth the landing, which was honestly a disappointment.

The bird went back to its buffalo, and the buffalo walked into the reeds to get out of the sun.

## The Thicket

The Thicket.

[A wall of reeds, much taller than Doug. A trail of hoofprints leads straight into it. Label: "IN HERE".]

In the heat of the day, a buffalo you cannot see may be lying a few steps away from you.

[Cross-section of the reeds: a dark bull-shaped silhouette with a red glow, lying down inside.]

Buffalo look for refuge from heat and danger in woodland, thickets and reed beds. In one Kruger study, herds rested more by day and fed more at night when the weather was hotter. A grown bull can settle into the cover and simply vanish.

[A car-wash brush next to the reeds. Label: "THIS THICK".]

The reeds are taller than Doug and about as dense as the spinning brushes in a car wash. Once you are inside, you can see roughly one arm's length ahead, and a buffalo lying still is just one more dark shape among the stems.

[An old leather book titled "HUNTING STORIES". A dotted line loops back on itself and ends behind a bush.]

Old hunting stories go further than that. They claim a buffalo will loop back along its own tracks and lie quietly in the thick cover, waiting for whoever is following it.

Here's the problem.

[The book gets a red question mark stamped on the cover.]

Nobody has ever measured that, and there is no study showing a buffalo planning an ambush or holding a grudge against anyone. What guides do agree on is much simpler: the lone old bulls are the ones to watch, and reeds hide them extremely well.

[Doug, camera up, stepping into the reeds. Speech bubble: "for the light".]

Doug found a line of fresh tracks leading into the reeds, and he followed them, for the light.

[The reeds sway. Then every reed goes perfectly still. Two eyes in the dark. Hold one beat.]

Then the reeds stopped moving.

[Cut to black. The red cap hangs on a single reed. DOUG DEATHS: 398. Field guide: red "NO".]

Doug deaths: three hundred and ninety-eight, from a single buffalo, and most buffalo do not live alone.

## The Vote

The herd.

The Vote.

[A herd lying in the grass at dusk. Dozens of identical buffalo heads. HEAD COUNT board: dozens. Label: "THE COWS DECIDE".]

A buffalo herd decides where to go next by holding a vote, and mostly only the adult cows take part.

[One cow stands, stares hard to the left, then lies back down. Another stands and stares the same way.]

The ecologist Herbert Prins watched a herd of almost a thousand buffalo at Lake Manyara in Tanzania. In the hour before the evening feed, cows would stand up one at a time, stare in a single direction for about a minute, and then lie back down.

[Small red arrows appear over each standing cow, pointing in slightly different directions. One big arrow averages them.]

Then the whole herd would rise together and walk off in the average direction of all those stares. The bulls and the young animals mostly stayed out of it, and a review in the journal Nature later described this as voting.

Here's the uncomfortable part.

[A meeting room. Everyone points. Then the meeting walks over the table.]

It is a meeting where everybody votes by pointing, and afterwards the meeting walks straight over you.

[Doug stands up in the middle of the herd and points the other way. Speech bubble: "this way".]

Doug stood up in the middle of the resting herd and cast his vote in the opposite direction.

[Every buffalo head turns. Then every buffalo stands.]

Every cow in the herd had voted the other way, and nobody called a second vote on whether to walk around him.

[A wall of buffalo walks off in the chosen direction, which is through Doug. Cut to black. The red cap sits in the flattened grass. DOUG DEATHS: 399. Field guide: red "NO".]

The herd left in the direction it had chosen, and Doug was standing in that direction.

Doug deaths: three hundred and ninety-nine. The herd never noticed, and its next weapon does not need to touch anyone.

## The Carrier

The Carrier.

[A map of Botswana crossed by long red fence lines. Label: "10,000+ KM".]

Botswana has built more than ten thousand kilometres of fences, and a big reason is the buffalo.

[A globe with a dotted line wrapped a quarter of the way around it.]

That is about a quarter of the way around the planet. Some of those fences, like the Southern Buffalo Fence finished in nineteen eighty-two, went up mainly to keep buffalo in the wild areas and away from the cattle farms.

[A buffalo next to a faceless green blob under a magnifying glass. Label: "FOOT-AND-MOUTH". The buffalo looks completely healthy.]

Buffalo carry the virus behind foot-and-mouth disease, and infected buffalo show few or no signs of being ill, while the cattle on the other side of the fence are not so lucky.

But that's not the only thing they carry.

[A tick crawls from the buffalo onto a cow. Second faceless blob. Label: "CORRIDOR DISEASE".]

A tick can pick up a tiny parasite from a buffalo and pass it on to cattle. In cattle, the result is Corridor disease, which kills about eight in every ten animals that get it, while in buffalo the same parasite is usually harmless. It is named after its first South African outbreak, in a corridor between two game reserves in KwaZulu-Natal.

[A fence stretching to the horizon, with one buffalo behind it, chewing.]

The buffalo's most expensive weapon never touches anyone.

It just stands there, chewing.

[Doug in a black-and-white cow costume, red cap on top, grazing beside the herd. A single tick climbs his leg.]

Corridor disease is a cattle disease, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a cow. He grazed politely beside the herd for one afternoon, and a tick found him.

[The costume lies flat in the grass, X eyes on the cow face, the cap still on top. DOUG DEATHS: 400. Field guide: red "NO".]

Doug deaths: four hundred, and the herd has not even started defending itself yet.

## The Bodyguards

The Bodyguards.

[A herd bunched tight, horns all pointing out. A lion crouches in the grass, rethinking things. Label: "THEY COME BACK".]

Buffalo come back for each other, and a herd under attack can stop being the prey and start being the problem.

[The herd runs at the lion. The lion runs.]

When lions get too close, a herd bunches together, turns to face them and chases them away. And if lions do manage to catch one, other members of the herd may come back to drive the lions off the animal.

[A neighbourhood watch sign: "WE ARE WATCHING. COWS: UP TO HALF A TONNE. BULLS: MORE."]

Think of a neighbourhood watch that actually turns up, where the cows weigh up to half a tonne and the bulls weigh more.

Here's the part tourists filmed.

[A watering hole. A small safari vehicle on the far bank. Label: "KRUGER, 2004".]

In September two thousand and four, at a watering hole in Kruger National Park, tourists in a safari vehicle filmed lions catching a buffalo calf at the water's edge.

[A crocodile surfaces and grabs at the scene. Shown as silhouettes only; no calf injury drawn.]

Then a crocodile came out of the water and tried to take the calf away from the lions. Then the herd came back.

[The herd surrounds the lions. The lions scatter. The calf stands up and trots into the herd.]

The buffalo surrounded the lions and drove them off one after another, and the calf got free and walked back into the herd. The video went online in two thousand and seven as Battle at Kruger, and it has since been watched tens of millions of times.

[Doug in the back of a safari jeep, camera up, door shut. Field guide: "MAYBE". DOUG DEATHS stays at 400.]

Doug watched the whole thing from inside the vehicle, exactly like the people who filmed it.

Doug is fine. For once, Doug stayed in the car.

The lions went home hungry, and for them this was a fairly normal day at work.

## The Lion Killer

The Lion Killer.

[A lion with a lunchbox, looking at a buffalo. The buffalo looks back. Label: "LUNCH?".]

For a lion, the buffalo is a favourite lunch, and sometimes that lunch turns around and kills the lion.

[A pride of lions surrounding one buffalo. Label: "TEAM JOB".]

In Kruger, a long-term study of hunting lions found that the males mainly caught buffalo. But it is rarely a job for one lion, since to bring down an adult buffalo, lions usually need a pride or a coalition of males working together. The same study found that the size of the hunting group shaped both how often lions succeeded and what they chose to chase.

[Speed lines. A buffalo outpaces a lion. Label: "ABOUT 10 KM/H FASTER".]

They also have to get close before anything starts. A buffalo can outrun a lion by about ten kilometres an hour, so lions have to ambush them from short range.

The ugly part is what happens next.

[A lion walking away from a buffalo very quickly, ears back. No injury drawn.]

The buffalo does not run every time. Lions that are too confident, or simply unlucky, have been killed by a buffalo's horns and its crushing weight.

[A job advert: "LION. PERKS: LUNCH. RISKS: LUNCH."]

Imagine a job where the main perk is a free lunch, and the main workplace hazard is also the lunch.

[Doug standing among the lions, wearing a name badge: "INTERN".]

Doug joined the pride as an intern, with a name badge. Doug has worked with lions before, and he did not learn anything then either.

[The herd turns to face the pride. The lions leave. Doug stays where he is, holding his badge.]

The herd turned around. The lions knew what that meant, and Doug did not.

[Cut to black. The red cap sits on a lion's head, far away. DOUG DEATHS: 401. Field guide: red "NO".]

Doug deaths: four hundred and one, and so far Doug has met one buffalo and one herd. The dry season puts all of them in one place.

## The Megaherd

All of them.

The Megaherd.

[A shrinking waterhole under a hard sun. On the horizon, a brown cloud. Label: "DRY SEASON".]

In the dry season, buffalo herds merge at the last water into super-herds of a thousand or more.

[The cloud gets bigger. Inside it, rows of buffalo heads. HEAD COUNT board: hundreds.]

As the pools dry up, herds that split apart during the rains converge on the rivers and waterholes that are left. A group of a few dozen animals swells into hundreds, and sometimes well over a thousand.

[Map of Botswana with one region highlighted. Label: "MABABE DEPRESSION".]

In Botswana's Mababe Depression, one guide looked across a single plain and estimated three thousand buffalo.

[A town sign: "POPULATION: 3,000". Every adult resident is a buffalo.]

That is a crowd the size of a small town, where every adult resident outweighs an upright piano.

Here's the problem.

[A brown dust cloud on the horizon, growing until it fills the sky. HEAD COUNT board: "5,000?".]

A crowd that size does not stand still. On a later drive, a visiting wildlife writer followed a dust cloud on the horizon and found about five thousand buffalo moving together.

[A red question mark over the number "200".]

You may have heard that buffalo kill two hundred people every year, but we could not find any published count behind that number.

[A herd charging; lions in full retreat.]

What is documented is how a big herd treats lions. Together, the herd can turn on the lions that are hunting it and send them running.

[Doug at the edge of the waterhole beside a lion. Doug still wears the "INTERN" badge. The lion looks at the badge, then at the dust cloud.]

Doug is still wearing the lion badge.

[A thousand buffalo arrive at the water. Every head turns toward the lion and Doug.]

A thousand buffalo arrived at the water and looked at the lion, and then at the small man wearing its badge.

[The lion runs. Doug does not.]

The lion ran, and Doug did not.

[Cut to black. HEAD COUNT board stamped red: "1,000+". DOUG DEATHS: 402. Field guide: red "NO", underlined twice.]

Doug has died. Again.

[Closing: the dust settles. The waterhole is empty. Far off, the herd walks away. On one buffalo's back, among the oxpeckers, sits the red cap. An oxpecker inspects it.]

When the dust settled, the herd was already leaving, and the red cap was riding away on a buffalo's back among the oxpeckers. It was the only part of Doug the herd agreed to keep.

Doug deaths: four hundred and two. The number never goes down. Subscribe, and watch where it goes up next.
