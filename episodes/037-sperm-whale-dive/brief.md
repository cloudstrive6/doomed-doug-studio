### The surface.

**1. The Head** (hook item, one of the three strongest; 0 m; death 288)
- Facts:
  - The sperm whale has the largest brain of any animal known, living or extinct (about 7.8-9 kg; verify the figure and the "any animal" wording).
  - The head is up to about a third of the body length. Most of it is the spermaceti organ and the "junk" above the jaw: a giant sound-making nose, not a brain case (Cranford 1999, *Marine Mammal Science*; verify).
  - In 1820, a sperm whale rammed the whaleship *Essex* twice in the Pacific, and it sank (Owen Chase's 1821 *Narrative*; Philbrick 2000, *In the Heart of the Sea*).
    The *Essex* inspired *Moby-Dick*. A second ship, the *Ann Alexander*, was sunk in 1851 (verify).
  - Whether the head evolved partly as a battering ram is a **debated hypothesis** (Carrier, Deban & Otterstrom 2002, *J. Exp. Biol.*; Panagiotopoulou et al. 2016, *PeerJ*, on the junk as a shock absorber). Say it as a hypothesis.
- Analogy: the head alone is about the length of a car or a small bus (use the measured head length from the source, and pick a car or bus whose length is sourced).
- **Cold-open fact (first sentence):** the head, not the ship. For example: "The sperm whale has the biggest brain of any animal on Earth, and most of its head isn't brain at all."
- **Twist by 0:45 (≤145 words):** the giant head is a weapon too. In 1820 one sank a whaling ship with it, twice.
- **Honesty line, one sentence:** the *Essex* was hunting them. No cannibalism, no crew death toll beyond one plain number if the writer needs it, and no harpoon imagery with blood.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug rows a small rowboat out "to apologise on behalf of the whalers". "Doug did not agree to this." (here; the narrator volunteered him).
  - The head rises behind the boat. Cut to black on the splash. Death 288, flat. The cap floats on a calm sea. Field guide: NO.
  - Re-hook: "That's what it does with its head. Wait until you hear what it does with its nose."

**2. The Nap** (about 15 m; Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - Sperm whales sleep in short bouts, hanging vertically, motionless, just under the surface, often in groups. It's about 7% of their time, possibly the least sleep of any mammal studied
    (Miller et al. 2008, *Current Biology*, "Stereotypical resting behavior of the sperm whale"; verify the % and the bout length).
  - The 2008 research team drifted into a sleeping group, and the whales didn't respond until the boat touched one (verify the anecdote in the paper or press release; cut it if unsourced).
- Analogy: a group of 15-metre whales standing upright like a forest of tree trunks, about the height of a five-storey building each (check the conversion).
- Twist: the biggest predator with teeth falls asleep in the open ocean, standing up, and nothing dares touch it.
- Doug beat: Doug floats upright among them with his eyes closed, copying them. They wake up all at once and dive. Doug sleeps on. "Doug is fine." Field guide: "MAYBE".
  - Re-hook: "When they wake up, they go down. This time, Doug went with them."

### Going down.

**3. The Click** (about 200 m; death 289)
- Facts:
  - The echolocation click is the loudest sound made by any animal, measured up to about 236 dB re 1 µPa at 1 m (Møhl et al. 2003, *JASA*; verify).
  - **Do not compare it to a jet engine in air.** Underwater and in-air decibels use different references, so the comparison would be wrong. Use a distance analogy instead: how far away hydrophones can pick it up (source it).
  - Whales start clicking a little way into the dive and click steadily all the way down (Watwood et al. 2006, *J. Anim. Ecol.*; verify the depth where clicking starts and use it for this item's gauge value).
  - The idea that the click stuns prey (Norris & Møhl 1983) has **never been shown**. Say so once.
- Analogy: a click that hydrophones hear many kilometres away (the number from the source), like a sound heard from one end of a city to the other.
- Twist: the giant nose from item 1 is a sound gun. Every click lights up the dark like a flash photo, but with sound.
- Doug beat: Doug puts on the scuba gear (the "rated to forty metres" line goes here), swims up to the whale's forehead and presses his ear to it "to listen".
  One click: Doug vibrates in cartoon rings, his eyes spin, then X eyes. Death 289. The field-guide MAYBE is rubbed out.
  - Honesty in the narration: nobody has measured what this does to a person up close, which is why Doug was sent. **No real injury described, no eardrum talk.**

**4. The Buzz** (about 500 m; costume item; death 290, played flat)
- Facts:
  - When a whale closes in on prey, its clicks speed up into a rapid "buzz" (or "creak"). Tagged whales make roughly 20 buzzes, or prey-capture attempts, per dive (Miller, Johnson & Tyack 2004, *Proc. R. Soc. B*; Watwood et al. 2006; verify the per-dive figure).
  - Their diet is mostly deep-sea squid, eaten whole. A big whale may eat hundreds a day (Clarke 1980s stomach studies; verify; give a weight per day only if a source gives one).
- **Costume gag:** sperm whales eat squid, "which is why Doug is dressed as a squid". **One 008 callback:** "He has worn this costume before. It went badly then too."
  The red cap sits on top of the costume. It comes off at the end of the item.
- Analogy: about 20 hunts in one dive, like going to the supermarket 20 times on one breath (check the dive length so the line is true).
- Twist: the clicks speed up, and the closer they come, the faster they get. Doug can hear it coming.
- Doug beat: the click rate speeds up on screen (a crude sound-wave strip that gets denser). Doug-squid tries to look like a rock. Cut to black at the buzz. Death 290, flat. The flat squid costume drifts down, cap on top.

**5. The Jaw** (about 700 m; Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - Sperm whales only have functional teeth in the lower jaw. They fit into sockets in the upper jaw, and they may not be needed for feeding: whales with badly deformed or broken lower jaws have been found well-fed
    (Spaul 1964 or another sourced report on jaw deformities; **verify, or cut this fact**).
  - **The famous "man swallowed by a sperm whale" story (James Bartley, 1891) is a hoax.** It has been traced and debunked (Edward B. Davis 1991, *Perspectives on Science and Christian Faith*; verify).
    There is no verified case of a sperm whale eating a person.
- Analogy: each tooth compared with something everyday (verify the length and weight of a large tooth).
- Twist: the most famous story about a sperm whale and a person is made up, and its teeth may not even be for eating.
- Doug beat: Doug, still in scuba gear, swims up to the open lower jaw with a dentist's mirror "to check". The whale closes its mouth, leaves him outside, and swims on. "Doug is fine."
  The narrator: one dry line, hinting he had a different ending ready.
  - Re-hook: "It doesn't need its teeth for squid. It does need them for something. The scars are the clue."

### The dark.

**6. The Scars** (about 900 m; midpoint, a strong item; death 291)
- Facts:
  - Sperm whales carry circular sucker scars and scratches from squid, including giant squid (Clarke; Roper; verify).
  - **Myth to kill:** stories of scars "the size of dinner plates" from enormous squid. Scars stretch as the whale grows, so big scars don't prove giant squid (verify a source that says this; otherwise just give the measured sucker size).
  - Giant squid beaks are found in their stomachs, sometimes thousands of beaks of many species in one whale (Clarke 1980; verify the number).
  - **Nobody has ever watched a sperm whale and a giant squid fight.** Everything we know is from scars and stomachs (verify; say it once).
- One callback allowed: 001's giant squid (the eyes that may have evolved to spot sperm whales). One line.
- Analogy: a giant squid's eye, about the size of a dinner plate (only if the source holds; 001 used this, so pick a different object or skip it).
- Twist: the whale's skin is a record of fights nobody has ever seen.
- Doug beat: Doug's dive light finds a giant squid. "hi friend?" (the only one this episode). The whale's click stops (it has found the squid). Doug is in between. Cut to black. Death 291, flat.
  - Show it as cartoon only: no tentacles wrapped on Doug, no bite. Afterwards Doug's ghost floats up beside one sucker-ring mark drawn on the whale, as an "evidence" label.

**7. The Cable** (1,134 m, exact; death 292)
- Facts:
  - Heezen 1957 (*Deep-Sea Research*, "Whales entangled in deep sea cables") documented whales caught in submarine telegraph cables. Almost all were sperm whales (14 of 16? verify), and the deepest was at 1,134 m (620 fathoms).
  - The cable was usually wrapped around the jaw, which suggests the whales were swimming along the seafloor with their mouths open, feeding at the bottom (Heezen's interpretation; say it as his interpretation).
- Analogy: a whale tangled more than a kilometre down, about three Empire State Buildings (check: about 443 m to the tip, so about 2.5) below the surface.
- Twist: they don't just hunt in the dark water. They hunt along the seafloor, mouths open, and the ocean floor has cables on it.
- Doug beat: the narrator sends Doug to "inspect the cable for damage". Doug volunteered (the narrator corrects himself: Doug was volunteered). The cable loops, and the whale sweeps past with its jaw open.
  Doug is tangled in a cartoon knot. Death 292. The cap snags on the cable and stays there (the cap survives).

**8. The Bones** (about 1,500 m; death 293)
- Facts:
  - Sperm whale lungs collapse during deep dives, which limits how much nitrogen gets into the blood (verify the mechanism wording).
  - Even so, a study of sperm whale skeletons found bone damage consistent with decompression sickness, and it built up with age (Moore & Early 2004, *Science*, "Cumulative sperm whale bone damage and the bends").
  - Dives last about 45-60 minutes, and some go far longer (Watwood et al. 2006; verify the longest recorded dive).
- **Medical-adjacent:** say "bone damage", not symptoms. No human medical detail, no advice about diving, ascending or decompression.
- Analogy: the pressure at 1,500 m is about 150 atmospheres, like the weight of a car on every postage stamp of skin (check the arithmetic and pick an honest everyday object).
- Twist: even the whale pays for diving this deep. Its own bones keep the receipts.
- Doug beat: at the gauge reading, Doug's tank crumples like a soda can (cartoon, the tank only). Cut to black. Death 293, flat. The narrator, deadpan, on the tank's forty-metre rating.
  - Re-hook: "And the whale isn't even at the bottom of its dive yet."

### Two kilometres.

**9. The Deepest Dive** (boss, the most extreme item; about 2,000 m; death 294, played flat)
- Facts:
  - Tagged sperm whales have dived to about 2,000 m (Watkins et al. 1993, *Marine Mammal Science*, or a later tag record; verify the exact depth and the source).
  - The whale found with a bottom-dwelling shark in its stomach, in water about 3,000 m deep, suggests they go deeper still (Clarke 1976 or the original report; give it as an inference, or cut it).
  - Down here there is no light from the surface. The whale finds everything by sound, and nobody has ever watched it hunt at this depth (verify the wording).
- Analogy: two kilometres is about five Empire State Buildings (or another sourced landmark) stacked under Doug, on one breath.
- Twist (the title's payoff): this is the deadliest the whale gets, a blind hunter that sees with sound in total dark. When it finds Doug, it clicks at him, buzzes, and decides he is **not food**.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug at 2,000 m, in the dark, his dive light failing. The click starts, then speeds up into the buzz. "wait" (Doug's only bubble here).
  - The whale's head stops right in front of him. It turns away and swims up for air. The narrator: the whale has to go back up to breathe, and so does Doug. Only one of them is going to.
  - Death 294, flat, quiet. No milestone bit.
- Closing (≤30 words of outro, no subscribe pitch): the cap floats up the whole two kilometres (the gauge counts back up) and surfaces in a group of sleeping, vertical whales (the item 2 callback).
  It settles on one whale's head. Field guide sticky note: "NOT FOOD." The narrator's last line is about the cap, not a recap.
