| Colleen Begg spent years following wild honey badgers (the bite is not placed anywhere, and nobody is said to have watched it) | Begg 2001 DPhil thesis, University of Pretoria, Fetched: https://repository.up.ac.za/handle/2263/29895?show=full ; 42-month study, Begg et al. 2022 (item 3) |
| She came across the bitten badger with its cheek badly swollen | Begg, Discover Wildlife (above): "I encountered one individual that was bitten on the face by a puff adder", "a large swelling on his cheek" |
| Cobra-type nerve toxins lock onto the muscle nicotinic acetylcholine receptor; in honey badgers a change at the binding site adds a positive charge, which impedes binding | Drabeck, Dean & Jansa 2015, *Toxicon* 99: 68-72, doi:10.1016/j.toxicon.2015.03.007, Abstract: https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/why-the-honey-badger-dont-care-convergent-evolution-of-venom-targ/ |
| A puff adder is not a cobra; its venom attacks tissue ("cytotoxic", "severe swelling and tissue damage") | SANBI puff adder (above) |
| So the receptor trick is no help against it | Inference from the two rows above (cytotoxins do not act on the nicotinic receptor); Londolozi notes resistance to cytotoxic venoms is "less well understood" (above) |
| Resistant is not the same as immune | Drabeck et al. 2015 is about resistance to one toxin class; the five-hour swelling shows the bite still lands (Begg) |
| Hedgehogs and pigs evolved almost the same change on their own; mongooses blocked the same spot by adding sugar groups (glycosylation); at least four independent origins | Drabeck et al. 2015 abstract (above) |
| Not repeated from 045: 045 said only that "a receptor in their muscles has changed shape". The positive charge, the convergent species and the cytotoxic exception are new. | 045 script line 125 |
