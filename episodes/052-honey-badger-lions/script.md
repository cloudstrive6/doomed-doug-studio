## The Puff Adder

The Puff Adder.

[A puff adder coiled in the sand beside a crude baseball bat for length. WEIGH-IN attacker side: "PUFF ADDER: A FEW KG".]

A puff adder is a heavy African viper that sits and waits in ambush, about as long as a baseball bat, and honey badgers dig them out and eat them.

[Field guide HONEY BADGER page. FRIEND? box empty.]

That bite was watched by Colleen Begg, who spent years following wild honey badgers in the southern Kalahari, and the badger's cheek swelled up badly before it went back to work.

[Crude cutaway of a muscle with a nerve ending. A keyhole-shaped receptor. A toxin blob tries to fit. A red plus sign sits in the keyhole. Label: "+".]

The usual explanation is in its muscles. Cobra-type nerve toxins work by locking onto a receptor that muscles use to take orders from nerves. In the honey badger, one small part of that receptor carries a positive charge, so the toxin struggles to get a grip.

Here's the problem.

[The puff adder's venom drop is a different colour, labelled "TISSUE". It ignores the keyhole entirely.]

A puff adder is not a cobra, and its venom attacks tissue instead, so that receptor is not what it aims at and the famous trick is no help here.

The badger just takes the swelling and waits it out, because resistant is not the same thing as immune.

[Three small portraits with the same keyhole: hedgehog, pig, mongoose. The mongoose's keyhole is stuffed with little sugar cubes.]

Hedgehogs and pigs evolved almost the same change in that receptor on their own, while mongooses blocked the same spot in a different way, by coating it in sugar molecules. Mammals have jammed that one lock at least four separate times.

[Doug holding the tail end of the same puff adder, the badger at the head end. Sign: "FOR SCIENCE".]

Doug was handed the other end of the same snake, for science, while the badger held the end with the teeth. Doug did not agree to this.

[A crude two-row timetable. Row one: "BADGER: SWELLS UP. 5 HOURS. BACK TO WORK." Row two: "DOUG:" with nothing after it. Doug lies flat, X eyes, the cap on the snake's head. DOUG DEATHS: 387. The narrator's hand writes "NO" in the FRIEND? box.]

Doug has died. Again. Only one of them had the right muscles, and neither of them had read the paper.

Next, the badger goes after something with no fangs at all, just a whole colony of stingers.

## The Hive

The Hive.

[A greater honeyguide on a branch, a honey badger below it, a dotted line from bird to badger to a bees' nest in a tree. A big red "?" stamps the dotted line. WEIGH-IN attacker side: "COLONY".]

For more than two hundred years, people have told a story about a bird that leads honey badgers to bees' nests, and scientists still have no solid record of it ever happening.

[The badger halfway up a tree trunk, paws in a wild bees' nest. A small bee cloud. A thought bubble shows larvae, not honey.]

Honey badgers really do raid bees' nests and will climb trees to reach them, and they want the larvae inside as much as the honey.

[The honeyguide beside a crude egg for size.]

The bird is the greater honeyguide, a small brown bird that weighs about as much as an egg. In the story, it finds a nest, calls a badger over and leads it there, and once the badger has broken in, the bird eats the wax.

Here is the strange part.

[An old-looking book page: "SPARRMAN, 1785. HEARD IT FROM SOMEONE."]

The story goes back to a traveller called Anders Sparrman in seventeen eighty-five, and he never saw it himself, only heard it from local people. In nineteen ninety, three researchers went looking for real evidence and found almost none.

[A crude map of Africa with nine countries shaded. Label: "394 HONEY-HUNTERS".]

In two thousand twenty-three, a team interviewed three hundred and ninety-four honey-hunters across nine African countries. Most had never seen it, although many in some Tanzanian communities said they had, and the team's best guess is that it happens but is rare, very local, or both.

[A Yao honey-hunter cupping his hands to call. Speech text: "brrr-hm". The honeyguide flies toward him.]

The bird does guide one animal for certain. In Mozambique, Yao honey-hunters call it with a loud trill and a short grunt, and in an experiment that call doubled their chance of being guided by a honeyguide.

[Doug in the beekeeper suit, cap on top, following the honeyguide through the bush. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug followed a honeyguide, expecting a badger to meet him at the nest and do the dangerous part. The bird did exactly what it does. It guides people.

[The bird leaves Doug at the nest. No badger. A huge bee cloud. The suit lies flat, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 388.]

No badger came, and the bees did. Doug has died. Again.

The bird got its wax. The next animal on the list doesn't want honey at all, only whatever the badger digs up.

## The Jackal

The Jackal.

[Night, Kalahari sand. The badger digging, nose in a hole, dirt flying. Behind it, at a polite distance, a black-backed jackal watching the hole. On a dead branch, a pale chanting goshawk watching too. WEIGH-IN attacker side: "JACKAL: ~8 kg".]

In the Kalahari, a digging honey badger often has a jackal right behind it, waiting for something to run out of the hole.

Black-backed jackals weigh about eight kilograms, a little less than the badger, and they are not here to fight it. A badger hunts with its nose in the ground, so anything that bolts from the dig gets grabbed by whoever happens to be standing closest.

[A crude tally board: "FOLLOWERS: GOSHAWK, JACKAL, +5 MORE".]

Colleen and Keith Begg followed nine wild badgers for years. Seven other species tagged along to feed, and the most common were black-backed jackals and pale chanting goshawks, a hawk that will perch near a badger's den and wait for it to come out.

[A dotted line across a map: "UP TO 27 KM A DAY". Beside it, a crude finish banner: "HALF MARATHON: 21 KM".]

A badger can cover up to twenty-seven kilometers in a day, which is further than a half marathon, mostly with its face in a hole.

The odd part is how little the badger does about it.

[A pie chart, crude: a thin slice labelled "5%".]

With the hawks, the badger lost nothing anyone could measure, and the jackals cost it only about five percent of the prey it catches above ground, so it mostly just keeps digging.

[Doug crouched behind the badger with a small bowl, cap on. A gerbil bolts out of the hole. The jackal catches it. A spray of dirt hits Doug in the face.]

Doug joined the freeloaders with a small bowl and waited by the hole. The jackal got the gerbil, and Doug got a face full of dirt.

[Doug wipes his face, cap on. The narrator's hand pencils "MAYBE" in the FRIEND? box.]

Doug is fine. The narrator had built this whole stop around the jackal, and the jackal was not interested in Doug at all.

The badger lets small things follow it around, but anything that tries to hold on to it meets a very different animal.

Its size.

## The Skin

The Skin.

[A honey badger in profile. A crude cutaway of its neck shows a thick skin layer. A red arrow: "6 MM". Beside it, a stack of eight bank cards. WEIGH-IN attacker side: "ITS OWN SIZE: ~9 kg".]

Grab a honey badger by the scruff, and it can turn round inside its own skin to bite you.

The skin is thick and loose. Near the throat it is about six millimeters thick, roughly a stack of eight bank cards, and the only thin skin is on the belly.

[A crude oversized hoodie on a stick figure. A hand grabs the hood. The figure inside turns round to face the hand.]

It hangs on the body like an oversized hoodie, so if you grab the hood, the person inside still has room to turn round and face you.

[The badger, grabbed by an unseen hand at the scruff, already rotated inside the skin, teeth toward the hand. No bite shown.]

That is the badger's whole plan for being caught. Begg puts it simply: even when a predator gets hold of one, the loose skin lets it twist round and bite back. Zoologists also describe that skin as keeping out some bee stings, some snake fangs and some teeth.

[The Kalahari males and females as two crude silhouettes: "MALE ~9.4 KG", "FEMALE ~6.2 KG".]

An average Kalahari male weighs about nine kilograms, and males are about a third bigger than females, which makes this a small animal that is built to be a bad thing to hold.

Here's the catch.

[A crude card in Doug's hands: "HOW TO HOLD A BADGER: 1. SCRUFF."]

Most handles are meant to give you control, but on a honey badger the scruff is just more loose skin, and the badger inside it is already turning round.

[Doug grabs the scruff. The badger turns inside its skin. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 389. The cap lands on the card.]

Doug read a card called How to Hold a Badger, and the card said scruff. Doug has died. Again.

Big cats have to deal with the same skin, and some of them have found ways around it.

## The Leopard

The Leopard.

[A leopard silhouette with a red glow on a tree branch, then the full reveal. WEIGH-IN attacker side: "LEOPARD: ~58 kg". The needle jumps.]

Leopards kill honey badgers, and so do lions, so the idea that nothing hunts this animal is simply wrong.

[Field guide page. A crude list: "EATS BADGERS: LEOPARD, LION, PROBABLY HYENA. EATS CUBS: JACKAL."]

In the southern Kalahari, the Beggs recorded lions, leopards and probably spotted hyenas killing both adult badgers and cubs, and the black-backed jackals from earlier killed cubs too.

[A crude bucket of water labelled "10 L = 10 KG" beside the badger, slightly bigger than it. A bracket: "LEOPARD'S FAVOURITE: 10 TO 40 KG".]

A male leopard in Kruger National Park averages about fifty-eight kilograms, six times a badger. Leopards prefer prey between ten and forty kilograms. A nine kilogram badger weighs a little less than a full ten-litre bucket of water, just under the size they like best.

[A bar chart: "WHO WINS? BIGGER ONE." The bars line up neatly by body size.]

When the Beggs looked at the badgers' fights with other carnivores, the winner could mostly be predicted from body size, and on this scale the badger has stopped being the big one.

The uncomfortable part is what the badger does about it.

[The badger sniffing a big paw print, then a speed line toward a hole.]

Begg has watched badgers sniff fresh lion or leopard tracks and bolt for a hole, which means the famous fearless animal checks the ground for cats and then leaves.

[Doug, cap on, sighs and crosses out two words on the HONEY BADGER page: "FEARLESS" and "UNKILLABLE".]

Doug crossed out two words in his field guide with a sigh, and looked around for the leopard.

[A leopard drops from the branch above, onto Doug, not the badger. Flat cut. The cap hangs on the branch. DOUG DEATHS: 390.]

The leopard was in the tree above him, and it was not there for the badger. Doug has died. Again.

From here the scale only goes up, and the next attacker on it is Doug himself.

## The Beekeeper

The Beekeeper.

[A row of white commercial beehive boxes on the ground, Western Cape mountains behind. Two boxes knocked over. WEIGH-IN attacker side: "DOUG: 80 kg".]

In one survey of beekeepers in South Africa's Western Cape, about half admitted to killing honey badgers.

[A crude survey card: "BADGER TROUBLE: 4 IN 5". A sober grey tint, no badgers shown.]

About four in five said badgers had caused them problems, because badgers climb, they break hives open, and an unprotected apiary is a row of easy meals.

[Doug in the beekeeper suit beside eight honey badger outlines on a balance.]

A beekeeper is the heaviest attacker yet, since Doug weighs eighty kilograms, about eight honey badgers, and the badgers were still winning.

Here is the twist.

[A hive up on sturdy legs; a badger looks up at it. Another hive with a strap around it. Label: "BADGER-FRIENDLY, 2002".]

The fix was not a weapon. In two thousand two, the Endangered Wildlife Trust and the bee industry launched a badger-friendly programme. Hives went up on stands a badger could not climb, or were secured so they could not be pushed over or opened.

[A honey jar with a crude sticker: "BADGER FRIENDLY".]

It grew into a national accreditation, and Woolworths, a big South African retailer, now requires its honey suppliers to meet badger-friendly standards that are checked independently. Raising and securing hives can cut losses significantly, and nobody has to fight anything.

[Doug, in the beekeeper suit, building a hive stand with crude MS Paint legs, wobbling. He sets the hive on top.]

Doug built a hive stand with legs that only an MS Paint artist could love, and the badger walked under it, sniffed once, and left.

[Doug gives a thumbs up under the stand, cap on top of the suit.]

Doug is fine.

So far, the only thing in this video to beat a honey badger is furniture, and lions don't get furniture.

Lions.

## The Lion

The Lion.

[Dawn on a private reserve. A crude open safari vehicle. A young lioness lunges at an adult honey badger; the badger is a blur of teeth and fur; the lioness backs off. WEIGH-IN attacker side: "LION: ~126 TO 190 kg". The needle swings hard.]

At Kapama, a private reserve in South Africa, a ranger watched a young lioness go for an adult honey badger, get nowhere, and quickly give up.

[Thirteen to nineteen crude buckets lined up beside one lion.]

A lioness averages about one hundred and twenty-six kilograms and a male about one hundred and ninety, which is thirteen to twenty badgers, or a long line of full buckets.

[The rangers' vehicle. A green cartoon cloud drifts across the grass toward it. A young male lion walks away from the bushes, nose wrinkled. Nothing behind the bushes is shown.]

The same morning showed why a lion might not bother. Her brother had already caught a young badger, and then a stench reached the rangers' vehicle, and the male lion stopped eating and walked away from his own meal.

[A crude honey badger tail, a small green cloud. A dotted line to a stick figure holding its nose, far away. Beside the line, an Olympic swimming pool for length: "45 M".]

Honey badgers have two large scent glands under the tail. People have smelled them from more than forty-five meters away, nearly the length of an Olympic pool.

The worst part, for the lion, is the maths.

[A crude lion's price list: "SKIN: CAN'T GRIP. BITES BACK. SMELLS." A red "?" over the badger.]

There is skin it can't get a grip on, an animal that turns round and bites, and a smell bad enough to put a male lion off a meal it had already won. Lions do kill honey badgers. They just have to want it.

[Doug crouched behind the badger, cap just visible. A male lion walks up, sniffs them both, leans back, considers, and pads away.]

Doug hid behind a honey badger, and the lion sniffed them both, thought about it for a while, and left.

Doug is fine. The narrator had a lion booked for this slot.

It works on one lion, but the next stop has six of them.

## The Pride

The Pride.

[A dirt road on the reserve, morning. Six lions spread across the road. One honey badger running straight at them, teeth bared. WEIGH-IN attacker side: "THE PRIDE: 6 LIONS". The needle slams into the end stop. The pride's weight is stamped red.]

In two thousand twelve, a ranger at Kapama watched one honey badger run straight at six lions.

[Six crude lions, each with a bucket tally above it; together they dwarf a single bucket beside the badger.]

Six lionesses alone would weigh more than seven hundred kilograms, about seventy-five full buckets against one.

[From the ranger's vehicle, the lions look like they are playing. Then the badger, mid-charge.]

He had gone out looking for lions and found them on the road, apparently playing with each other. They were not playing. A male honey badger was charging straight at them, teeth showing, growling the whole way.

[The badger runs through the middle of the pride. At a young male lion, it growls and swings a front paw at his face. The lions look at each other.]

The lions did not seem to know what to do. The badger ran right through the middle of the pride, and when it reached a young male, it growled and swung a front paw at his face.

[The badger dives into a hole in the ground. The lions sniff the hole, then wander off.]

Then it did the thing that actually saved it, which was to go down a hole in the ground, and after a while the lions lost interest and wandered off.

Here is the real secret of the fearless honey badger.

[Field guide note: "PLAN A: LEAVE. PLAN B: FIGHT."]

Its first plan is to leave. Begg describes badgers that avoid trouble wherever they can, and usually only attack when a predator surprises them, often while they are digging with their noses in the ground. Caught out in the open, they stand their ground, but given a hole, they take the hole.

[Doug and the badger in front of the whole pride. The badger disappears down the burrow. Doug tries to follow. Only his head fits. Hold.]

Doug stood behind the badger in front of six lions, and the badger went down the hole. Doug did not fit.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 391. Field guide: "NO" again, underlined twice.]

Doug has died. Again. The badger was never planning to share the hole.

[Closing: morning. The badger climbs out of the burrow. The red cap lies on the sand beside the hole. The badger sniffs the cap, then walks off. Sticky note on the field guide: "IT LEFT. DOUG DIDN'T."]

In the morning, the badger came out of the hole and found the cap lying on the sand. This animal has eaten puff adders, robbed bees' nests and run through six lions, and the cap, it let off with a sniff.
