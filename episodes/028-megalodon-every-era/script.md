## The Nursery

The Nursery.

[Caption bar: THE NURSERY. Era card: ABOUT 10 MILLION YEARS AGO. PANAMA.]

Doug did not agree to this.

One rule: nobody can run this experiment, so these are educated guesses from the fossils, and the megalodon gets one verdict at every stop.

[The time machine door opens onto a shallow, sunny bay. The cage is lowered into clear green water.]

The first stop is a warm, shallow bay on the coast of what is now Panama, about ten million years ago.

[A small shark shape glides toward the cage. Doug waves. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

In the rocks of this bay, scientists sorted through hundreds of fossil shark teeth and found that most of the megalodon teeth were small. Not small sharks. Young ones.

They argued this was a nursery, a sheltered shallow place where newborn megalodons could grow up with plenty of food and fewer big predators around.

[Evidence card: a spread of small fossil teeth labelled "GATÚN".]

Here's the twist: the newest study of megalodon babies thinks pups that big may not have needed a nursery at all.

One older study counted the growth rings in a fossil backbone and worked back to a birth length of about two meters. The newer study looked at the same kind of evidence and got closer to four.

A standard single bed is one meter ninety long. Even the smaller estimate hangs off the end.

[A newborn megalodon slides alongside the cage. It is longer than the cage. It is much longer than Doug.]

The thing Doug just waved at is a baby, and on the newer estimate, it is about twice as long as he is.

[The adult megalodon drifts past, calm. A tick appears on the chalkboard: MEG SURVIVED: 1 / 11.]

Warm water, shallow cover and plenty to eat. Needed or not, for the megalodon this was probably home.

## The Mosasaur

The Mosasaur.

[Caption bar: THE MOSASAUR. Era card: ABOUT 66 TO 70 MILLION YEARS AGO. MAASTRICHT SEA.]

Five stops on this list come after the megalodon's time. This one is long before it, in a warm, shallow sea that once covered what is now the Netherlands.

[The cage is lowered into pale, chalky water. A long reptile shape moves below.]

The biggest hunter in this sea was Mosasaurus, a giant marine lizard and the very first mosasaur to be given a name.

[Evidence card: MOSASAURUS HOFFMANNII.]

Its story starts in a limestone quarry near Maastricht in the seventeen hundreds, where miners kept digging up strange teeth and bones. One huge skull started a long argument about whether it came from a fish, a whale or a crocodile.

The animal was later named Mosasaurus, the lizard of the Meuse, after the river that runs through Maastricht. In seventeen ninety-five, the French army seized that skull and took it to Paris. It is still there.

[Evidence card: an old engraving-style drawing of a skull in a quarry. A red arrow points from Maastricht to Paris.]

The skull alone could be about two meters long, and that is where the agreement ends. Some scientists think the whole animal reached seventeen or eighteen meters, while others favour eleven or twelve.

That is roughly one to one and a half city buses. The cautious estimates for the biggest megalodons start at about fourteen meters.

[Doug holds up a scorecard between the two animals, like a referee. It reads: "TIE?"]

The strange part is that the size contest is basically a draw. The real difference is breathing.

The mosasaur is a reptile with lungs, so sooner or later the biggest predator in this sea has to swim up to the surface for a breath. The shark never has to stop.

[The mosasaur rises for air directly beneath the cage. The cage tips. Cut away. The cap floats beside the scorecard. DOUG DEATHS: 228.]

Doug was holding his scorecard in the one place the mosasaur had to go.

[Tick: MEG SURVIVED: 2 / 11.]

On size alone, it is even. On never needing air, the megalodon probably wins.

## The Giant Ichthyosaur

The Giant Ichthyosaur.

[Caption bar: THE GIANT ICHTHYOSAUR. Era card: ABOUT 202 MILLION YEARS AGO. SOMERSET, ENGLAND.]

At the very end of the Triassic, the sea over Somerset held something the megalodon has never had to deal with. Something bigger.

[The cage is lowered into deep blue water. The light goes dim. Something enormous blocks the sun.]

In May twenty twenty, an eleven-year-old called Ruby Reynolds was fossil hunting with her father, Justin, on the beach at Blue Anchor in Somerset, when she picked up a piece of bone.

They kept finding pieces for more than two years. Put back together, they made part of a lower jaw that would have been over two meters long.

[Evidence card: a long curved jawbone, with a small girl silhouette and a spade beside it for scale.]

In twenty twenty-four, scientists named the animal Ichthyotitan severnensis, the giant fish lizard of the Severn, and Ruby and Justin are co-authors on the paper.

The team estimates it was more than twenty-five meters long, about the size of a blue whale. That is more than two city buses parked end to end, and the inside of the bone suggests this one was still growing.

The researchers are careful about this. They only have jaw bones, and they would need a skull or a skeleton to be sure. Not long after this animal lived, giant ichthyosaurs like it disappeared from the record for good.

But that's not the disturbing part. For the first time on this list, the megalodon is the small one, and the proof was picked up by a child on a beach.

[The ichthyosaur glides over the cage like a ceiling, slowly, for a long time. Doug looks up. The megalodon looks up.]

It passes over the cage, it passes over the megalodon, and it does not slow down for either of them.

[Doug, untouched, gives a small thumbs up. DOUG DEATHS stays at 228.]

Doug is fine, and we had honestly expected more from a reptile the size of a blue whale.

[Tick: MEG SURVIVED: 3 / 11.]

The megalodon survives, because nothing here was trying.

Coin toss.

## The Supernova

The Supernova.

[Caption bar: THE SUPERNOVA. Era card: ABOUT 2.6 MILLION YEARS AGO.]

This stop sounds like the end of the world, and it might be one of the gentlest on the list.

[The time machine door opens on a calm sea under a daytime sky. A new, very bright star hangs above the horizon.]

Crusts and mud from the floor of every major ocean contain radioactive iron-sixty. It is made in exploding stars, and any that formed with the Earth decayed long ago, so fresh iron-sixty means a star blew up somewhere nearby.

[Evidence card: a cross-section of a dark seafloor crust with a thin glowing band.]

The iron says this happened more than once in the last ten million years, with one wave of that dust arriving between about one and a half and three million years ago.

One estimate puts the explosion roughly a hundred and fifty light-years away. Light leaving a star that far away when HMS Challenger was still at sea would only just be reaching us now.

[Doug puts on sunglasses and a sun hat and stares up at the new star.]

Here's the problem. A group of physicists proposed that radiation from a nearby supernova reached deep into the sea, and that big animals would have taken the biggest dose. They named the megalodon as a possible victim.

That idea is a hypothesis, and it is debated. The same researchers say a star at that distance should affect Earth without setting off a mass extinction on its own.

There is also a timing issue. The latest dating of the fossils says megalodon was already gone by about three point six million years ago, so this is a visit after its time.

[The megalodon circles the cage slowly. Doug adjusts his sun hat. DOUG DEATHS stays at 228.]

Doug is fine. Probably. Nobody really knows what a day under that star does to a person.

[Tick, drawn shakily: MEG SURVIVED: 4 / 11.]

It is the scariest-sounding era on the list, and the megalodon survives it, probably.

## The Rival

The Rival.

[Caption bar: THE RIVAL. Era card: ABOUT 9 TO 10 MILLION YEARS AGO. PERU.]

This is the megalodon's own time, off the coast of what is now Peru, and it was not the only giant hunting whales.

[The cage is lowered into murky green coastal water. A huge, blunt head looms out of the gloom.]

In November two thousand eight, on the last day of a field trip, a fossil hunter in the Peruvian desert found a skull about three meters long. The skull alone was longer than a single bed, with more than a meter to spare.

It belonged to a sperm whale, but not the kind alive today. The rocks it came from, the Pisco Formation, are famous for fossil whales, and they have since been dated to roughly nine to ten million years ago.

[Evidence card: a long skull lying in desert sand next to a single bed. Label: "3 M".]

This one was built to hunt big animals, and it probably went after baleen whales seven to ten meters long. Estimates for its full length run from about thirteen and a half to seventeen and a half meters, which puts it right in the megalodon's size class.

Scientists named it after Herman Melville, who wrote Moby-Dick, and they called it Leviathan.

The embarrassing part came a few weeks later. The name Leviathan had already been given to a fossil mastodon back in eighteen forty-one, so they had to change it. It is now Livyatan, the Hebrew spelling of the same sea monster.

Livyatan and the megalodon lived in the same waters at the same time, and they probably wanted the same whales.

[Doug sits on the cage roof between the two of them, holding a scorecard. He writes "?" on it.]

Nobody has a fossil of the two of them fighting, and we are not going to pretend we do.

[The whale's head swings toward the cage. Cut away. A deep thud. The scorecard floats up, with the cap beside it. DOUG DEATHS: 229.]

Doug was the only thing here small enough to be an easy decision.

[A red "?" stamps the chalkboard. MEG SURVIVED stays at 4 / 11.]

We are not calling this one. It goes on the board as a question mark.

## The Orcas

The Orcas.

[Caption bar: THE ORCAS. Era card: TODAY. NORTH PACIFIC.]

Off the coast of British Columbia, there is a group of killer whales that most people never see, because they live far out at sea and eat something unusual.

[The cage is lowered into grey open ocean. Tall black fins appear in the distance.]

Researchers spent years trying to work out what these offshore orcas were eating. When they finally caught them feeding, they scooped scraps of prey out of the water and tested the DNA, and it was shark, including at least sixteen Pacific sleeper sharks.

[Evidence card: a worn orca tooth next to a sharp one, drawn as a simple cartoon comparison.]

Then the researchers looked at the orcas' teeth, and in older animals, many of them were worn flat, right down to the gums.

They think shark skin is at least part of the reason. It is covered in tiny tooth-like scales called dermal denticles, it feels like sandpaper, and every meal grinds the teeth a little lower.

The worst part is that it doesn't stop them. They keep eating sharks anyway.

[The pod surrounds the cage. Five fins circle in a tight ring.]

Last time Doug met orcas, they treated him like he wasn't there. Today he is next to a shark, which is the one thing these orcas never ignore.

[A city bus outline appears beside the biggest orca. The orca is only a little shorter.]

The biggest killer whale ever recorded was about nine point eight meters long, nearly the length of a city bus, so a big megalodon would outsize any one of them. But orcas don't fight one on one. They live in family groups of anywhere from a few whales to around twenty, and they hunt as a coordinated team, a lot like wolves.

[Doug holds a toothbrush out through the bars toward the nearest orca. The orca opens its mouth. Cut away. The toothbrush floats up alone. DOUG DEATHS: 230.]

Doug was only trying to help with the teeth.

[A red X stamps the chalkboard. The megalodon swims off into the grey. MEG SURVIVED stays at 4 / 11.]

One on one, the megalodon is bigger. Against a family that does this for a living, probably not.

Wrong water.

## The Hothouse

The Hothouse.

[Caption bar: THE HOTHOUSE. Era card: ABOUT 56 MILLION YEARS AGO. TROPICS.]

About fifty-six million years ago, the planet heated up fast. Scientists call it the Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum, and the tropics took the worst of it.

[The time machine door opens on a flat, glaring sea. Heat shimmer. The water is a sickly green.]

In what is now Nigeria, chemical traces in tiny sea fossils show the surface water going from about thirty-three degrees Celsius to more than thirty-six. The plankton there crashed, and the researchers blame the heat.

[Thermometer graphic rising to 36. Label: "36 °C".]

Safety guidance says a hot tub should never go above about forty degrees, and this was the open sea.

Elsewhere, the warming brought a second problem. Parts of the ocean lost oxygen, and in some places the water below the surface had almost none left.

[The cage is lowered in. Doug wears a towel round his neck and holds a rubber duck.]

Now look at the megalodon. In twenty twenty-three, scientists read the chemical signals locked in its fossil teeth and estimated its body ran about seven degrees warmer than the water around it, roughly twenty-seven degrees Celsius.

Most sharks run at the same temperature as the water they swim in, which makes the megalodon one of the rare exceptions. That warm body was a weapon. It probably helped the shark swim faster and hunt in cooler seas than most sharks could.

Here's the catch. This water is about nine degrees hotter than the megalodon itself, and as far as we can tell, its body was built to hold heat in, not to get rid of it.

[The megalodon slows, sluggish in the shimmer. Doug sets the rubber duck on the water and leans back.]

Doug treated it like a spa. It was not a spa.

[Cut away. A short hiss. The cap floats beside the rubber duck. DOUG DEATHS: 231.]

[A red X stamps the chalkboard. The megalodon sinks away into darker green.]

Water hotter than its own body, and not enough oxygen to work with. That is our reasoning, not a study's, and our verdict is probably not.

## The Ice Age

The Ice Age.

[Caption bar: THE ICE AGE. Era card: ABOUT 20,000 YEARS AGO.]

About twenty thousand years ago, so much water was locked up in ice sheets that the sea was roughly a hundred and twenty meters lower than it is today.

[The time machine door opens on a wide, empty plain of sand and mud. The cage sits on dry ground. The sea is a thin line far away.]

Ice sheets buried much of North America and northern Europe. The missing water is taller than the Statue of Liberty, from the ground to the tip of the torch, and huge areas of the world's shallow continental shelves were simply dry land.

Between Siberia and Alaska, the sea floor turned into a land bridge, and woolly mammoths walked across ground that is now under the Bering Strait.

[Map graphic: a coastline with a pale band showing where the shelf was exposed. A small mammoth walks across the band.]

For the megalodon, the ice should have been good news. Baleen whales only became true giants in the last few million years, around the time the ice ages began.

Researchers think the ice is part of the reason. Growing ice sheets changed how food was spread around the ocean. Instead of being scattered thinly, it turned up in rich, dense patches at certain times of year, and the biggest filter feeders were the best at making the most of them.

So the whales got bigger than ever, and a bigger whale is a bigger meal.

What nobody mentions is what the ice took away. At this sea level, warm shallow shelves like the nursery at our very first stop were high and dry, and the nearest coast could be a very long walk away.

[Doug climbs out of the cage and starts walking toward the distant sea, dragging the cage behind him. Wind. He shivers.]

Doug decided the sea was a short walk away. He brought the cage with him, for safety. It was not a short walk away.

[Cut to Doug as a cartoon ice block, mid-step, cap frozen on top. DOUG DEATHS: 232.]

[A red X stamps the chalkboard. Far out, a fin turns away into grey water.]

Bigger food and less home. The fossils can't settle it, but we think the megalodon probably loses this one.

## The Canal

The Canal.

[Caption bar: THE CANAL. Era card: TODAY. PANAMA.]

This is the bay from the first stop, ten million years on, and the water is nowhere to be seen.

[The time machine door opens on a muddy bank in hot sun. Jungle. Dry land in every direction.]

The bay is gone, because over millions of years the land here rose until North and South America were joined. A review led by Smithsonian scientists puts the final closing at about two point eight million years ago.

Some researchers argue for a much earlier land bridge. The review found that evidence unconvincing.

Ten million years ago, the sea at our first stop was part of a seaway that still linked the Pacific to the Caribbean. When the land joined, that link was cut, and the Atlantic was split from the Pacific.

[Map graphic: two oceans with a thin strip of land between them. A red arrow labels the strip.]

The rocks from that nursery bay are still here. Geologists call them the Gatún Formation, and they study them in outcrops near the city of Colón and in fresh material dug out during the expansion of the Panama Canal.

[A container ship glides along the canal behind Doug.]

The cruel part is simple. Ships now cross the land where the pups were born.

The canal runs about eighty kilometers from ocean to ocean, a little under two marathons, and ships using it pass the very rocks where megalodon pups once grew up.

[Doug kneels at the bank and digs with his fingers. He pulls out a small, dark fossil tooth and holds it up to the sun.]

Doug digs a small tooth out of the bank. Small teeth like this one are the reason scientists called this place a nursery in the first place.

[DOUG DEATHS stays at 231. Doug sits on the bank, cap on, tooth in hand.]

Doug is fine. There is nothing here big enough to reach him, and he seems a little disappointed about it.

[A red X stamps the chalkboard. The cage hangs empty over dry mud.]

No sea, no nursery, no pups. The megalodon has nowhere left to be born.

## The Trench

The Trench.

[Caption bar: THE TRENCH. Era card: TODAY. DEEP PACIFIC.]

There is a popular idea that megalodon is still alive, hiding somewhere deep in the ocean, and this stop takes that idea seriously for about a minute.

[A period sailing steamship, HMS CHALLENGER, on a grey sea. A dredge rope runs over the side.]

In the eighteen-seventies, the British research ship HMS Challenger dragged dredges across the deep floor of the world's oceans.

From the Pacific, it hauled up shark teeth wrapped in black lumps of manganese, mostly from the red clay that covers the deepest parts of the open ocean floor. One came from about five kilometers down, which is six Burj Khalifas stacked on top of each other.

[Evidence card: a cut lump of manganese with a shark tooth in the middle.]

Two of the megalodon teeth from that voyage became famous. In nineteen fifty-nine, a scientist measured their manganese coatings, assumed how fast manganese builds up, and announced they were only about eleven thousand and twenty-four thousand years old.

That would make megalodon practically modern, and it didn't hold up.

Manganese crusts on the deep sea floor grow far more slowly than that. They take millions of years to form.

But that's not the twist. Re-checked, the teeth that started the rumour turned out to be as old as every other megalodon tooth.

[The cage is lowered into blackness. Doug holds a sign on a rope: "MEG?"]

Down here, the water is about two degrees Celsius, it is completely dark, and there is very little to eat. Today, no sharks live much below three thousand meters, because there simply isn't enough food for them.

Nobody has ever found a megalodon tooth younger than about three point six million years.

[Doug lowers the "MEG?" sign further into the dark. Silence. The rope goes slack. Only the sign comes back up, swinging. DOUG DEATHS: 233.]

The sign came back. Doug went down to check.

[A red X stamps the chalkboard. Nothing swims past the cage. Only dark.]

Megalodon is extinct. It is not down there.

The real one.

## The Last Megalodon

The Last Megalodon.

[Caption bar: THE LAST MEGALODON. Era card: ABOUT 3.6 MILLION YEARS AGO.]

Every verdict so far has been a guess. This one is written in the rocks.

[The time machine door opens onto an ordinary blue sea under an ordinary sky. Nothing is on fire. Nothing is frozen.]

This is the early Pliocene, about three point six million years ago. No asteroid, no supervolcano, no exploding star. Just an ocean, slowly changing.

[The cage is lowered. The megalodon swims beside it, huge and quiet.]

For a long time, scientists thought megalodon died out about two point six million years ago. Then, in twenty nineteen, a team went back through the megalodon fossils from California and Baja California and re-checked every date, one by one.

Many of the dates turned out to be too vague, some of the fossils had been misidentified, and some of the rocks were older than anyone had thought.

When they were done, the genuine megalodon fossils stopped at the end of the early Pliocene, about three point six million years ago. That is a million years earlier than the old answer.

The oldest stone tools ever found are about three point three million years old. Megalodon was gone before anyone on Earth had made one.

[Evidence card: a timeline. A red line marks 3.6. A small stone tool icon sits at 3.3.]

It did not go alone: a study of the Pliocene found that about a third of the ocean's big animals disappeared around this time. Many of them depended on the coasts, and the coasts kept shrinking as sea levels swung up and down. Marine mammals lost more than half of their variety, and sea turtles and seabirds were hit hard as well. Warm-blooded animals were more likely to be lost.

A warm body needs a lot of fuel, and less coast meant less fuel.

[A small early great white shark swims past the cage. The megalodon turns its head to watch it.]

Last time, Doug heard that early great whites were feeding at nearly the same level as the megalodon. They may have been chasing the same food. That makes competition a possible factor, but it is not proven.

The strangest part is how quiet it was. The greatest predator of its time disappeared from an ordinary ocean, and nothing dramatic happened at all.

[The megalodon turns slowly and swims away from the cage, into the dark blue, until it is gone.]

[Doug waves after it. Speech bubble: "bye friend?"]

[The cage cable snaps. The cage sinks out of frame. DOUG DEATHS: 234.]

Doug has died. Again.

[A red X stamps the chalkboard. MEG SURVIVED: 4 / 11.]

[Cut to a modern beach, bright and ordinary. Doug, alive, kneels in the sand and digs out a dark megalodon tooth. He places the red cap on top of it.]

Megalodon got through four of these eleven eras. It won at home ten million years ago, and lost the last era it actually lived in. The tooth is now wearing the cap.
