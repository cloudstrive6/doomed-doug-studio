## TITLE DELIVERY: "30 hornets / 30,000 bees / an afternoon" (brief section 7, highest risk)

**Verdict: the title is supported, but the sources do not agree on one figure. "30" is the top of the best-sourced range, not a fixed number.**

**Where the sources differ (flag for the creative director and the titler):**
1. **Hornet count.** The 1995 Tamagawa figure is 20 to 30, and the 2020 WSU figure is 15 to 30. The current 2022 WSU revision dropped that range in
   favour of "2 to 50" (Barth 2013). So "30" is the upper end of the classic range and the middle of the newer one. "30 Giant Hornets" is defensible,
   but "20 or 30" is what the sources actually say. **The script says "Twenty or thirty", never "thirty" alone.**
2. **Bee count.** The NYT 1995 and WSU sources use 30,000 as the colony (WSU: 30,000 to 50,000). The American Bee Journal's "Japanese estimates" give
   5,000 to 25,000 bees *killed*, which is lower. "30,000 Bees" is sourced as the colony size that gets wiped out, not as a body count.
3. **Time.** Every source says hours (3 hours, a few hours, as little as 90 minutes, 1 to 6 hours). "In an Afternoon" fits all of them.
4. **Recommendation:** keep the title shape. The safest exact wording is the 1995 one ("20 to 30" / "30,000" / "three hours"). If the titler wants a
   single hornet number, "30" is the top of a sourced range. Nothing in the title rests on a viral-only figure.

## The Mark
- Rubs a gland at the tip of the abdomen on the hive, which recruits nestmates: Ono et al. 1995, *Nature* 377:334-336, doi:10.1038/377334a0. The abstract, via search
  snippet, says the hornet "rubs the basal tuft of the terminal gastral sternite around a prey food resource ... the hornet nestmates then congregate and attack the
  marked site en masse" (Search; the Nature page is paywalled). WSU FS347E: "The hornets mark a colony by rubbing the basal gland at the tip of the abdomen";
  "A food-site marking pheromone is produced in a gland located on the last abdominal segment ... The pheromone recruits additional hornet nest-mates." (Fetched)
  The chemistry was dropped as the brief allowed. No chemical is named. It is not the 010 alarm pheromone.
- After several trips; nestmates congregate: NYT 1995: "After several return trips ... the hornet smears a messenger chemical called a pheromone on the hive,
  which signals other hornets to join in an attack." (Fetched)
- Three or more switch to mass attack: NYT 1995: "If three or more giant hornets ... gather at a beehive, they switch from individual hunting behavior to an
  attack en masse." (Fetched)
- Distance, 0.5 to 1.5 miles (about 1 to 2.5 km); up to 5 miles, hunting can continue indefinitely: WSU FS347E: "within 0.5 to 1.5 miles ... If a greater
  distance, up to five miles, the hunting phase can continue indefinitely without ever shifting to a slaughter phase (Matsuura and Sakagami 1973)." (Fetched)
  The "walk to the corner shop" is narrator framing.
- Japanese honeybees detect the mark: Ono 1995 abstract (Search): "The Japanese honeybee can detect the hornet marking pheromone, and responds by increasing
  the number of defenders". WSU: "The A. cerana recognizes this pheromone and alerts the colony." (Fetched)
- European honeybees don't seem to react: WSU FS347E: "EHBs do not react and apparently do not recognize the hornet's marking pheromone." (Fetched)

## The Occupation

  next ten to fourteen days, until it becomes rancid". (Fetched)
- Brood rich in protein and fat: WSU FS347E: "Honey bee pupae and larvae are rich in protein and fat". (Fetched)
- Chew the brood into a paste and fly it home: WSU FS347E. (Fetched)
- Defensive during the occupation, attacking animals and people: WSU FS347E: "becomes very defensive, attacking nearby animals and humans." (Fetched)
- Almost 100 km a day: RIKEN Research news, 13 Nov 2015: "Adult hornets fly almost 100 kilometers a day in pursuit of insects to feed their developing
  young." https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/rr/8130 (Fetched). It is attributed in the script ("According to Riken"). A marathon is 42.195 km, so 100 km is
  more than two marathons (World Athletics standard distance; widely documented, e.g. https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Marathon, Search).
- A Riken scientist found 17 amino acids in larval saliva and made it into a sports drink consumed by millions a year: RIKEN, same page: "found they contained
  17 different amino acids"; "a sports drink that is now consumed by millions of people a year". (Fetched) No product name is spoken or shown.
- The drink contains no real saliva, only a copied amino-acid mix: MDPI *Foods* 9(7):885 (2020): "the beverage products do not contain the actual larval saliva
  of V. mandarinia, but an amino acid mixture composed of the same amino acid composition." https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/9/7/885 (Search; fetch failed on an expired certificate)

## The Heat Ball
- Japanese honeybees (*Apis cerana japonica*) coevolved with the hornet: WSU FS347E. (Fetched)
- The bees retreat, let the scout follow, and jump it: NYT 1995: "When a hornet approaches, the bees at the opening flee into the nest, luring their enemy inside
  where it is jumped by a crowd of waiting defenders." (Fetched)
- More than 500 bees vibrate: NYT 1995: "more than 500 bees engulf each hornet in a ball"; "the bees vibrate". Gu et al. 2021, *Biology* 10:484
  (doi:10.3390/biology10060484), citing Ono 1995: ">500 A. cerana japonica workers can quickly engulf the giant hornet". https://labs.biology.ucsd.edu/nieh/papers/Beestinghornet.pdf (Fetched)
- About 46 °C: NYT 1995 gives 116 °F (46.7 °C). Sugahara & Sakamoto 2009, *Naturwissenschaften* 96:1133-1136 (doi:10.1007/s00114-009-0575-0; the brief's journal
  question is resolved), abstract: "the temperature inside the bee balls does not rise higher than 45.9 degrees C". https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19551367 (Fetched via the Europe PMC API).
  WSU FS347E gives 47 °C, citing Sugahara 2009 and Yamaguchi 2018. Sources range from 45.9 to 47, so the script says "about forty-six".
- CO2 helps, killing within minutes: Sugahara & Sakamoto 2009: hornets "are killed in less than 10 min"; "CO2 produced inside the bee ball by honeybees is a
  major factor together with the temperature". NYT 1995 says about 20 minutes, so the script says "within minutes".
- Hornet dies around 46 °C, bees survive to about 50 °C, a gap of about 4 degrees: Sugahara 2009: "The lethal temperature of the hornet (45-46 degrees C) under
  conditions of CO2 ... The lethal temperature of the honeybee is 50-51 degrees C". NYT 1995: hornet lethal range "111 to 114.8 degrees" F (44 to 46 °C), and bees "can
  survive heat of up to 122 degrees" F (50 °C). WSU: bees "are tolerant of temperatures a few degrees higher". (Fetched)
- Hot tubs should never exceed 104 °F (40 °C): US Consumer Product Safety Commission, "CPSC Warns of Hot Tub Temperatures": "hot tub water temperatures should
  never exceed 104 degrees Fahrenheit." https://www.cpsc.gov/content/cpsc-warns-of-hot-tub-temperatures (Search). 46 minus 40 is 6 degrees.
- European bees have no defence like it: WSU FS347E: "EHBs lack the defense mechanisms developed by Asian honey bees ... which coevolved with the hornets." (Fetched)
  Gu 2021 notes that *A. mellifera* can heat-ball smaller hornets, but ineffectively, so the script avoids "never learned".

## The Sting

- 8 credit cards: ISO/IEC 7810 ID-1 card nominal thickness 0.76 mm, and 8 x 0.76 = 6.08 mm. https://www.iso.org/standard/31432.html (Search)

- Stings repeatedly, unlike honeybees, which lose the stinger: WSU FS347E: "individual hornets can sting repeatedly, unlike honey bees that lose their stinger
  in the process." (Fetched). The script says a honeybee "usually" loses it.

  (Fetched). This is attributed to his book, as the brief required.
- Not aggressive toward people unless the nest or food is threatened (draft 2 drops "at all", per the screener): WSU FS347E: "The NGH tends not to be aggressive toward people, though they will attack when
  their nest or food source is threatened." (Fetched)
