## The Hunter

The Hunter.

[A huge hornet beside a honeybee. Label: "UP TO 5 CM". An AA battery drawn beside it, same length.]

This is the northern giant hornet, the largest hornet in the world. Its queens can reach about five centimeters, the length of an AA battery.

[A wooden beehive. One hornet waits at the entrance.]

The raid starts with just one of them, waiting by the entrance of a honeybee hive and grabbing a single bee as it comes past. Then it flies off with it, and comes back for the next one.

Here's the strange part. The hornet doesn't eat the bee at all, and instead it chews it down to the thorax, the chunky middle section, and rolls that into a meat ball to carry home.

[A round brown bundle in the hornet's legs. No legs, wings or heads visible on it.]

The meat ball goes to the hornet larvae waiting back in the nest, because the adults don't eat any of it. The larvae eat it, then drool out a high-energy liquid, and that liquid is what the grown hornets actually live on.

So the babies are fed by the hunters, and the hunters are fed by the babies.

[Field guide NORTHERN GIANT HORNET page. FRIEND? box empty.]

The giant hornet hunts insects, not people, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a bee and standing guard at the hive entrance with his arms folded.

## The Mark

The Mark.

[The hornet backs up against the side of the hive and rubs the tip of its abdomen on the wood. A faint orange smudge appears.]

After a few trips, the hornet leaves a note, rubbing a gland at the very tip of its abdomen on and around the hive, and the scent it leaves behind works like an invitation.

[Map: a hornet nest icon, a dotted line, the hive. Label: "1 TO 2.5 KM".]

Other hornets from the same nest pick up the scent and start gathering at the hive. Once three or more of them are there together, the hunt stops being a hunt, and they switch to a mass attack.

Distance matters here. The switch usually happens when the hornet nest is close, somewhere between about one and two and a half kilometers from the hive, which is roughly a twenty-minute walk. Further out, the hornets can keep picking off single bees indefinitely and never bother with the rest.

But that's not the disturbing part. Japanese honeybees can smell the mark and start getting ready, while the European honeybees that beekeepers keep in North America don't seem to react to it at all.

[Doug in the bee costume. A hornet rubs its abdomen on his shoulder. An orange smudge appears on the costume.]

The hornet marked Doug too, and Doug said the only thing he ever says to things that are about to kill him.

## The Slaughter

The Slaughter.

[THE TARGET label changes to HIVE. Thirty hornets land on the hive at once.]

In a mass attack, a single giant hornet can kill up to forty bees a minute, and it does it with nothing but its jaws.

[Bees stream out of the hive one at a time. Each one gets a red X and drops as a whole, round cartoon bee with X eyes.]

The researchers who reported that rate said a group of twenty or thirty hornets can wipe out a colony of thirty thousand bees in about three hours. Washington State University puts a healthy colony at thirty thousand to fifty thousand bees, gone within a few hours, and Washington's agriculture department says a whole hive can fall in as little as ninety minutes.

That works out to one hornet for every thousand bees, like one usher against a sold-out concert hall, and the usher wins.

European honeybees don't fight back as a group. They rush out to defend the hive one by one, and they are killed one by one.

Here's the part that makes it worse. The hornets aren't even after the adult bees, which are just in the way, so once the defenders are dead, the last few survivors get ignored, because what the hornets really want is still inside.

[Doug in the bee costume, being placed in the middle of the hive by the narrator's hand.]

The last time Doug met this hornet, he brought a fly swatter. This time, the narrator zipped him back into the bee costume and put him in the middle of the hive, to even up the numbers.

Doug did not agree to this.

[Thirty hornets turn toward him. Cut. The costume lies flat among the cartoon bees, X eyes, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 370.]

The numbers did not even out, and the hornets were only getting started.

## The Occupation

The Occupation.

[Hornets standing guard on top of the empty hive. One faces the camera.]

After the slaughter, the hornets move in, taking over the empty hive and guarding its entrance for ten days or more.

[Inside the hive: rows of white capped cells. A hornet carries off a pale round bundle.]

What they came for was the brood, the honeybee larvae and pupae, which are packed with protein and fat. The hornets chew the brood into a paste and fly it back to their own nest, trip after trip, until what's left goes bad. While they hold the hive, they turn very defensive, and they will attack animals and people who come too close.

Back at the nest, it's the same arrangement as before, with the larvae eating the paste and drooling the liquid that the adults drink. According to Riken, a Japanese research institute, adult hornets fly almost a hundred kilometers a day hunting for their young, which is more than two marathons, every day, fuelled by baby drool.

And this is where it gets strange. A scientist at Riken analyzed that larval drool and found seventeen different amino acids in it, then turned the mix into a sports drink that millions of people now drink every year. It doesn't contain any real hornet spit, just a recipe copied from it.

[Doug in the bee costume, sitting next to a hornet larva in its cell, holding a tiny cup up to it.]

Doug skipped the recipe and went straight to the source, for the energy.

[Doug drinks. His eyes go wide. Thumbs up.]

Doug is fine. He has never felt better in his life, and it is not going to help him with what happens next.

## The Heat Ball

The Heat Ball.

[A Japanese honeybee hive. A single hornet scout approaches the entrance.]

Japanese honeybees can cook a giant hornet alive.

They evolved alongside this hornet, so when a scout shows up, the bees at the entrance retreat inside, let it follow them in, and then jump it.

[A ball of bees forms around the hornet. A red thermometer next to it climbs.]

More than five hundred bees pile onto the hornet in a tight ball and vibrate their flight muscles, until the temperature inside climbs to about forty-six degrees Celsius. To put that in context, American safety advice for hot tubs says the water should never go above forty, so the ball runs six degrees past it.

The heat isn't working alone, either. Inside the ball, the bees also push up the carbon dioxide, and the two together kill the hornet within minutes.

The insane part is how close it is. The hornet dies at around forty-six degrees, while the bees can survive up to about fifty, so the whole defence depends on a gap of about four degrees.

The European bees in that slaughtered hive have no defence like it.

[Doug in a hornet costume, cap on top, stepping through the hive entrance. Bubble: "hi"]

Doug went in a hornet costume this time, to see what it's like from the other side, and the bees were happy to show him.

Doug has died. Again. Four degrees was plenty.

## The Sting

The Sting.

[THE TARGET label changes to DOUG. A giant hornet stinger, drawn huge, next to a stack of eight credit cards of the same height. Label: "ABOUT 6 MM".]

A giant hornet's stinger is about six millimeters long, and it can go straight through a standard beekeeper's suit.

That's about as long as a stack of eight credit cards is thick, which is not much, but it is more than enough. Washington State University says professionals who remove nests wear two layers of smooth, slippery fabric instead, and they wear face masks too, because these hornets can spray venom.

A honeybee stings once and usually loses its stinger in the process, but a giant hornet doesn't. It can sting again and again, and it delivers a lot of venom each time. In his book on stinging insects, the entomologist Justin Schmidt called it the most intimidating insect on earth.

[A wide shot of a forest path. The hornet flies past a hiker without stopping.]

But here's what the scary reputation leaves out. The northern giant hornet isn't looking for people, and it tends not to be aggressive toward them. It attacks when its nest or its food is threatened, and when that happens, it does not hold back.

[Doug, no costume now, in a standard white beekeeping suit, cap on top of the hood. He gives a thumbs up.]

Doug is not a bee anymore. He is a beekeeper now, in a perfectly standard suit, standing right next to a hive that the hornets have just taken over.

[One hornet lands on his sleeve. Cut. Doug flat in the suit, X eyes, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 372.]

The suit was standard. So was the result.

## The Toll

The Toll.

[A plain white card. Text only: "JAPAN, DEATHS FROM BEE, WASP AND HORNET STINGS: ABOUT 20 A YEAR". No drawings of people.]

In Japan, about twenty people a year have died from stings in recent years, according to the national health ministry's population statistics.

That is more than one person every month, year after year, in a country where people live and work alongside these insects every summer.

The hard part is what these numbers can't tell you. They don't separate giant hornets from smaller hornets, wasps and bees, because every stinging insect goes into the same column, so nobody can say from these figures how many were this hornet.

[A second card: "SHAANXI, CHINA, 2013: 42 DEAD. ABOUT 1,600 INJURED."]

In twenty thirteen, China's state news agency reported that forty-two people had died after hornet stings in southern Shaanxi Province since the summer. Around sixteen hundred more people were hurt, according to the regional health authority. Local officials there blamed the giant hornet for the attacks, and experts at the time said they were not sure why that year had been so much worse than usual.

[Doug stands beside the card with his cap in his hands. He does not move.]

Doug has nothing to add. He holds his cap in both hands, says nothing, and waits quietly for the card to go.

## The Hitchhiker

The Hitchhiker.

[THE TARGET label changes to NORTH AMERICA. Map of the Pacific. A red dot appears on Vancouver Island.]

In August twenty nineteen, three giant hornets were found near Nanaimo, on Vancouver Island in British Columbia, the first time the species had ever been detected in North America.

[A small nest in the ground in a park. A red X stamps over it.]

The following month, local beekeepers and provincial officials found the nest in a park and destroyed it. Then in December, Washington's agriculture department confirmed giant hornets just across the border, near the town of Blaine.

Here's the unsettling part. DNA from the hornets suggests these were two separate arrivals, from two different countries.

Nobody knows how they got here. One idea is that a mated queen rode over hidden in cargo, by ship or by plane, but that's still a guess and not a finding. A standard forty-foot shipping container is about two hundred and forty times longer than she is.

[Newspaper headline: "MURDER HORNET". A red line crosses it out. Below: "NORTHERN GIANT HORNET, 2022".]

In twenty twenty, newspapers made it famous as the murder hornet. Entomologists didn't like that name, and in twenty twenty-two, the Entomological Society of America gave the old Asian giant hornet the name it has now, the northern giant hornet.

[Doug at the border in a booth, holding a sign: "NO HORNETS". He checks a passport.]

Doug volunteered for border control, made his own sign, and checked every single passport that came through, very slowly, one at a time.

Hornets don't carry passports. And one of them had already built a nest.

## The Nest

The Nest.

[October 2020. A gloved hand ties a tiny radio tag around a hornet's waist with a piece of dental floss.]

In October twenty twenty, Washington's agriculture department caught live giant hornets near Blaine and tied tiny radio trackers onto them with dental floss.

One tagged hornet flew home, to a hollow inside a tree, with the entrance about three meters off the ground.

[Before dawn. Workers in thick suits wrap the tree in plastic. A vacuum hose goes into the hole.]

Before dawn on October the twenty-fourth, a crew in protective suits wrapped the tree, pushed a vacuum hose into the hole, and knocked on the trunk until eighty-five hornets had been sucked out. Later, they cut the tree open and found a nest about the size of a basketball.

The worst part is what was growing in it. There were seventy-six queens that had already emerged, and another hundred and eight capped cells holding pupae that were mostly thought to be queens too. Altogether, the nest could have produced nearly two hundred queens. A new queen mates, leaves to find somewhere to spend the winter, and starts a colony of her own the next year, although only a small share ever manage it.
