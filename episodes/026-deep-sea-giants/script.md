## Giant Oarfish

Giant Oarfish.

[CABLE OUT: 250 M. Blue fading to navy. A silver line hangs straight up and down in the water. Doug, hanging from the cable, squints at it.]

At two hundred and fifty meters, most of the sunlight is gone, and something long and silver is hanging in the water, straight up and down, like a dropped ribbon.

This is the giant oarfish, the longest bony fish alive. Measured ones reach about eight meters, about as long as two cars parked end to end. It has a red crest on its head and one red fin running the length of its back, and it moves by rippling that fin while its body stays almost still.

In Japan, its name is usually translated as messenger from the sea god's palace, and a popular belief says it turns up before earthquakes.

Here's the problem. In twenty nineteen, Japanese researchers checked more than three hundred deep-sea fish sightings against more than two hundred earthquakes, going back to nineteen twenty-eight. They found one possible match. The earthquake fish predicts nothing.

[Red X stamp over a small drawing of a cracked map of Japan.]

It also hides well. Almost everything we know about oarfish comes from ones that floated to the surface or washed up on a beach, already dead or dying, which is an unusual way to study anything. The best early video of a healthy one, upright in its own habitat, came from an oil industry robot submarine in the Gulf of Mexico in twenty eleven. For the longest bony fish on the planet, that's only about fifteen years ago.

[Doug holds up a hand-drawn sign beside the fish: "EARTHQUAKE?". The fish hovers. Nothing happens. A long beat.]

Doug holds up a sign next to the fish and waits for the earthquake, the way the legend says he should. The fish keeps hovering, and nothing happens for a long time.

Doug is fine.

## Japanese Spider Crab

Japanese Spider Crab.

[CABLE OUT: 350 M. A slope off the coast of Japan. Pale shapes on the mud like folded umbrellas. One of them stands up.]

At three hundred and fifty meters off the coast of Japan, the seafloor looks like it's covered in broken umbrellas, until one of the umbrellas stands up.

The Japanese spider crab has the longest legs of any arthropod alive, the group that includes insects, spiders and crabs. From the tip of one claw to the other, it can stretch close to four meters, which is about as wide as a small car is long.

It lives on the cold slopes on the Pacific side of Japan, anywhere from about fifty to five hundred meters down. It can weigh around nineteen kilograms, and it may live as long as a hundred years.

[A crude clipboard appears in the corner, titled "WHY SO BIG?". The narrator's hand writes the first line: "COLD".]

This is where the collection of reasons begins. One idea about why deep animals get so big is cold, because cold water slows an animal's whole life down. Growing takes longer, adulthood arrives later, and a body that keeps growing for longer can end up larger than its relatives in warm, shallow water.

The surprising part is that all that reach isn't for hunting. Most of the time, the spider crab walks over the mud and picks at dead and decaying material with its claws. It is roughly as wide as a car, and it spends most of its time tidying the seafloor.

[Doug stretches his arms out wide beside the crab. A dotted line shows the gap: his arms end long before the legs do.]

Doug stretches his arms out to compare, and he loses by about two meters. The crab reaches over, picks the cap off his head, and walks off downhill.

[The crab strolls down the slope, the red cap held up in one claw. Doug follows. The slope keeps going. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 211.]

Doug follows his cap down the slope, considerably further than his equipment allows.

Doug has died. The crab carried the cap for about a minute, then lost interest.

## Giant Phantom Jelly

Giant Phantom Jelly.

[CABLE OUT: 990 M. Navy, nearly black. A deep red bell drifts in, trailing four long red curtains.]

At nine hundred and ninety meters, there is almost no light left, and a dark red shape the size of a dinner table is drifting toward Doug.

This is the giant phantom jelly, and its bell can be more than a meter across, and below it hang four ribbon-like arms that can grow longer than ten meters. A single arm can be about as long as a tennis court is wide.

Scientists first collected one in eighteen ninety-nine, and in twenty twenty-one a robot submarine filmed one at this exact depth in Monterey Bay, California. In all that time, it has been seen only about a hundred times. One deep-sea research institute in California has met it just nine times in thousands of robot dives. It has turned up in every ocean except the Arctic, from near the surface down to about six thousand seven hundred meters.

Its diet is mostly a guess: probably plankton and small fish. Scientists suspect it wraps prey in those long curtains and pulls it up toward its mouth.

The unsettling part is that it isn't always alone. Robot cameras have filmed a small fish called a pelagic brotula living beside it, hovering over the bell and slipping in and out of the arms. Scientists now think the two live together on purpose.

[Doug looks at the little fish, then at the curtains. Speech bubble: "roommates?"]

Doug has seen that the jelly takes in lodgers, so he offers to be the next one.

[Doug swims between the curtains. They fold slowly around him. Cut on the wrap. The cap drifts out alone. DOUG DEATHS: 212.]

Doug has died. Again. The fish is still living there, rent free, which was always the arrangement.

Bigger.

## Giant Isopod

Giant Isopod.

[CABLE OUT: 1,100 M. Black water, a grey mud floor in the cable's lamp. A huge armoured bug walks into the light.]

At one thousand one hundred meters, something walks into Doug's lamp. It looks exactly like the woodlouse under your garden pot, except that this one is about the length of a house cat.

The giant isopod is a close relative of the pill bug, the grey roly-poly that curls into a ball when you touch it, and this one can do that too. It can reach about fifty centimeters, and it lives on muddy seafloor from about one hundred and seventy to more than two thousand meters down.

It eats whatever sinks to the bottom, from dead fish and squid to the occasional whale. When a big meal lands, it eats until it can barely walk.

[The narrator's hand writes the next line on WHY SO BIG?: "FOOD IS RARE".]

That's the next reason on the clipboard. Down here, food mostly arrives as whatever falls from above, and it arrives rarely. One idea is that a bigger body burns less energy for its size and stores more, so it can wait much longer between meals.

Here's the part nobody expected. In January two thousand nine, a giant isopod at the Toba Aquarium in Japan ate one small fish of about fifty grams. Then it stopped. Its keepers kept offering food for years, and it kept refusing, sometimes nosing at the meal and then leaving it alone. It was found dead in February twenty fourteen, more than five years after its last meal. In twenty twenty-six, scientists studying two related giant isopods found a huge stomach and an extremely slow metabolism, which helps explain the waiting.

[Doug sets a fish on a plate in front of the isopod. Speech bubble: "hi friend?". Calendar pages fly off a wall calendar beside them: 2009, 2010, 2011, 2012, 2013. The isopod does not move.]

Doug offers the isopod a fish on a plate, says hello, and waits politely. The isopod ignores him. For five years.

Doug is fine.

The isopod outlasted the narrator's patience, and it wasn't close.

## Colossal Squid

Colossal Squid.

[CABLE OUT: 1,500 M. Near-freezing black water in the Southern Ocean. A huge eye opens in the dark, then the rest of the squid.]

At one thousand five hundred meters, in the freezing water around Antarctica, a huge eye opens in the dark. Doug met the giant squid once. This is its heavier cousin.

The colossal squid is the heaviest invertebrate known. In February two thousand seven, a fishing boat in the Ross Sea hauled one up that weighed about four hundred and ninety-five kilograms. That's about as much as a concert grand piano. The squid is now at Te Papa, the national museum in Wellington, New Zealand, where it's still the most massive invertebrate anyone has ever examined.

[Inset: the squid's eye with a ruler beside it, "27 CM".]

Its eye measured about twenty-seven centimeters across, among the largest eyes in the animal kingdom. And its arms and tentacles carry hooks that can swivel, so whatever they catch has a hard time getting loose.

Adults are thought to live deeper than a thousand meters, in water close to freezing, and almost nothing is known about how they live. Sperm whales are thought to be the main animal that hunts them, and some whales carry scars from those hooks.

The strangest part is how long it took to see one alive. Scientists described the species in nineteen twenty-five, from pieces of arms found in the stomach of a sperm whale. For the next hundred years, nobody filmed a living one in the wild. Then, in March twenty twenty-five, a robot camera near the South Sandwich Islands finally caught one on video. It was a juvenile, about thirty centimeters long, the size of a school ruler.

[Doug waves at the eye. A hooked tentacle lifts the cap off his head first. Cut on the moment. Then the cap alone, hanging from one swivelling hook. DOUG DEATHS: 213.]

Doug waves at the eye. The hooks find the cap first, and they find Doug second.

Doug has died. A century of searching, and the cap got closer than anyone.

## Greenland Shark

Greenland Shark.

[CABLE OUT: 2,000 M. Arctic water, a thin crust of frost on the cable. A huge grey shark drifts past, very slowly.]

At two thousand meters in the North Atlantic and Arctic, the water sits just above freezing. A grey shark the length of a small boat glides past, so slowly that it barely seems to be moving.

The Greenland shark can grow to about five meters, and it may be the longest-living vertebrate known. In twenty sixteen, scientists dated twenty-eight of them using the lens of the eye, which keeps the same core from before the shark is born. They timed it against a pulse of radioactive carbon that nuclear bomb tests spread through the oceans after the nineteen fifties. The biggest shark was about three hundred and ninety-two years old.

It grows about one centimeter a year, and females don't seem to be ready to breed until they're about a hundred and fifty years old or more. Take the middle of that estimate, and the shark was born around the time the Mayflower crossed the Atlantic under sail.

[The narrator's hand writes on WHY SO BIG?: "LIVE LONGER".]

That's the next reason. In cold water, some animals grow slowly but keep going for a very long time, and if you keep growing for centuries, you eventually become enormous.

Here's the uncomfortable part. That age comes with an error bar of plus or minus a hundred and twenty years. So the shark could be under three hundred, or a lot older than four hundred, and nobody knows exactly.

It's also one of the slowest-swimming fish ever measured for its size, cruising at about a third of a meter per second, which is slower than most people walk.

[Doug, in the harness, kicks hard and paddles away from the shark. The shark doesn't change speed. Frost spreads over Doug from the cap down. Frosty X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 214.]

Doug decides he can outswim it, and he's right, because the shark doesn't even try. It doesn't need to.

The cold got him first. Doug has died, and the shark will remember none of this in three hundred years.

## Giant Tube Worm

Giant Tube Worm.

[CABLE OUT: 2,400 M. A hydrothermal vent on the East Pacific Rise: black chimneys, shimmering water, and a thicket of white tubes topped with bright red plumes.]

At two thousand four hundred meters, on the volcanic seafloor of the eastern Pacific, there's a field of white tubes taller than a person. Each one has a bright red plume on top, like a lipstick-red garden hose as tall as a doorway.

These are giant tube worms, and nobody knew they existed until nineteen seventy-seven, when a submarine diving near the Galapagos discovered them living around hydrothermal vents, the hot springs of the seafloor. Their tubes can stand about two meters tall.

The red comes from blood, rich in the same molecule that makes your blood red. The plume soaks up oxygen and chemicals from the vent water and passes them down to billions of bacteria packed into a spongy organ inside the worm's body.

But that's not the weird part. The giant tube worm has no mouth and no gut, and it never eats anything. The bacteria inside it turn the vent chemicals into food, and the worm lives entirely on what they produce.

[The narrator's hand writes on WHY SO BIG?: "BRING YOUR OWN FOOD".]

That's the next reason on the clipboard. Most of the deep sea is starving, but here, the food is manufactured on location. With its own kitchen inside, it can grow incredibly fast: more than eighty-five centimeters of tube in a year, the fastest growth ever measured in an ocean invertebrate.

[Doug holds out a sandwich to the nearest plume. Nothing happens.]

Doug offers lunch to something with no mouth, and it turns out it brought its own.

[Doug leans back against the warm black chimney. A puff of cartoon steam. The cap drifts up and away through the shimmering water. DOUG DEATHS: 215.]

Doug leans on the nice warm rock to wait, and the vent water handles the rest.

Doug has died. The worms never noticed.

Too deep.

## Bigfin Squid

Bigfin Squid.

[CABLE OUT: 4,000 M. Pure black. The lamp picks out a pale, small body near the seafloor, its arms held straight out and then bent sharply downward, like a giant drawing compass.]

At four thousand meters, close to the bottom, something pale is suspended in the darkness, and its arms are bent at the elbows like it's waiting for a bus.

The bigfin squid has a small body and two big fins, and its arms and tentacles stick straight out to the sides, then bend sharply down at a right angle, like elbows. From there, they trail down in thin filaments that can be up to twenty times the length of its body. The filaments are sticky and covered in tiny suckers, and scientists suspect they catch prey, although nobody has ever watched it happen.

The biggest one ever estimated from video was more than six meters long in total, which is taller than a giraffe. Adults apparently spend their time hovering just above the seafloor, while the young start life near the surface. It lives down to more than six thousand meters, and one filmed in the Philippine Trench is the deepest squid ever recorded.

Here's what makes it worse. Only about seventy have ever been seen or caught, and the caught ones were damaged youngsters. Scientists have never collected an adult, so everything we know about the grown-up version comes from cameras.

[Doug hangs beside it, arms held out at right angles, copying the elbows. The squid does not react. A long hold.]

Doug hangs beside it with his arms bent at the elbows, attempting to blend in. The squid ignores him completely.

Doug is fine.

Nobody knows what it eats, but it has clearly decided that it isn't Doug.

## Giant Sea Spider

Giant Sea Spider.

[CABLE OUT: 5,000 M. Pure black, soft grey ooze. Eight long, thin, pale legs step into the lamp, carrying a body barely longer than a thumb.]

At five thousand meters, on the soft mud of the abyss, eight long, thin legs walk into the lamp, and they don't seem to be attached to much.

This is a sea spider. Despite the name, it isn't a spider, or even an arachnid. It belongs to its own separate group of sea animals with jointed legs, and most of them are small, often only a few millimeters across.

This one, the giant sea spider, can reach a leg span of about seventy centimeters, about as wide as a bicycle wheel, on a body only about seven centimeters long. It walks the deep slopes of most of the world's oceans, from about four hundred to more than five thousand meters down.

It gets stranger. There's so little body that the legs have to carry everything else. Long branches of its gut run down inside the legs, and so do most of its reproductive organs.

It has no gills and no lungs, so oxygen simply soaks in through its outer shell. And in twenty seventeen, scientists found that in sea spiders, the weak heart barely moves blood beyond the middle of the body. Out in the legs, the gut does the pumping instead, squeezing in slow, regular waves.

It's a giant made almost entirely of legs, and the legs are doing everyone's job.

[Doug puffs out his cheeks and holds his breath, arms spread out stiffly like legs. He slowly turns blue. X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 216. The cap drops onto one long leg, and the spider walks off with it.]

Doug decides to blend in. He stops breathing and waits for the oxygen to soak in through his skin.

It doesn't. Doug has died. The sea spider walks off with the cap, and to be fair, it has the legs for it.

## Supergiant Amphipod

Supergiant Amphipod.

[CABLE OUT: 6,500 M. The Kermadec Trench, northeast of New Zealand. A trench wall in the lamp. Pale, shrimp-like animals drift in from the dark.]

At six thousand five hundred meters, in the Kermadec Trench northeast of New Zealand, a pale animal that looks like a giant shrimp swims into the light.

It's an amphipod, a relative of the tiny sand hoppers that jump around on beaches, and most amphipods are about the size of a grain of rice. This one, the supergiant amphipod, can reach about thirty-four centimeters, longer than a school ruler. Scientists reported it from this trench in twenty thirteen.

[The narrator's hand writes on WHY SO BIG?: "MORE OXYGEN".]

That's the next reason on the list. Cold water holds more oxygen than warm water. In nineteen ninety-nine, two researchers compared the sizes of nearly two thousand species of amphipods from around the world and found that the biggest possible size tracked how much oxygen was in the water. Not every animal fits that idea, and scientists still test it. Doug already met it once, in the air of an old Earth, where the bugs got big.

The surprising part is that this animal isn't rare. In twenty twenty-five, researchers pulled together about two hundred records from all over the world, from about three thousand nine hundred to almost nine thousand meters down. Their model suggests it may live across more than half of the world's oceans, and its genes suggest the populations are connected. We just almost never go where it lives.

It's a scavenger, and when food reaches the bottom, it comes fast and it comes in numbers.

[Doug lowers a sandwich on a string. A few pale shapes appear. Then dozens. Cut on the cloud. The cap floats up alone, past the cable counter. DOUG DEATHS: 217.]

Doug lowers a sandwich as bait. The swarm arrives.

Doug has died. The sandwich, for the record, also did not make it.

## The Giant Cell

The Giant Cell.

[CABLE OUT spins past 8,336 M. A tiny pale snailfish flicks past the lamp, going the other way. The narrator's hand writes "PRESSURE?" on WHY SO BIG?, then crosses it out with one red line.]

On the way down, the cable passes the deepest fish ever filmed, the snailfish Doug met on his first trip, and nobody has filmed a fish any deeper. The narrator writes pressure on the clipboard, then crosses it out, because none of the main ideas say it makes animals bigger. Pressure doesn't build giants. It builds limits.

[CABLE OUT: 10,641 M. The Sirena Deep, Mariana Trench. Near-black water, fine grey mud. A lumpy, fragile, sponge-like object sits on the mud in the lamp, about the size of a fist.]

At ten thousand six hundred and forty-one meters, in the Sirena Deep of the Mariana Trench, something lumpy and fragile is sitting on the mud, and it looks like a dirty sponge.

It isn't a sponge. It isn't even an animal. It's a xenophyophore, and it's one single cell.

It's a giant kind of foram, a group of single-celled organisms that build shells, and this one builds its fragile outer shell by gluing together grains of seafloor mud. A single one often grows more than ten centimeters across, about the size of a fist, and scientists call them the largest single cells in existence. The biggest kind, about twenty centimeters across, lives much shallower, around a thousand meters down, off Scotland.

[Inset: a map of the Pacific with the Mariana Trench highlighted, a dotted line down to "10,641 M".]

In July twenty eleven, scientists from Scripps, working with National Geographic engineers, dropped cameras into the Sirena Deep and found these cells right here. Nobody had ever recorded them anywhere near that depth before.

They're also crowded: one researcher found more than fifteen major groups of animals living inside them, sometimes more than a hundred animals in a single cell. Worms, crustaceans, sponges and snails all treat them like apartment blocks.

To be fair to the ocean, not everything down here is a giant. Plenty of deep animals are ordinary-sized, the tiniest seafloor animals actually get smaller with depth, and scientists still argue about the reasons on this clipboard.

But here's the real answer to this trip. At the very bottom of the deepest trench, the giant isn't an animal at all. The biggest thing on the seafloor is one cell.

[Doug kneels beside the cell, very carefully. Speech bubble: "hi friend?". A flat, quiet beat. X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 218.]

Doug kneels down and says hello to a single cell.

Doug has died. Again.

[Cut to the research ship at night. Inside, a monitor shows the camera feed from the bottom, with a red "LIVE" tag in the corner. The red cap drifts down into frame and settles on top of the cell. A small worm pokes out of the shell beside it.]

Up on the ship, the live feed shows the cap sinking slowly into frame from above. It settles on top of the cell, which already had a hundred tenants, and now it has a hat.

[Final frame: the monitor, "LIVE", the cap on the cell. DOUG DEATHS: 218.]
