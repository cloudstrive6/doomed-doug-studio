## 2. Escalation axis and items
**Axis: depth, the depth at which Doug meets each giant (a value inside that animal's sourced depth range), about 250 m to 10,641 m,
monotonic.** The deeper Doug goes, the darker, colder and higher-pressure it gets and the stranger the giants become, ending where
the biggest thing on the seafloor is one cell.

**Spoken bands (two words or fewer):** **"Big."** (items 1-3, under 1,000 m) / **"Bigger."** (items 4-7, 1,000-3,000 m) / **"Too deep."**
(items 8-11, abyss and trenches; the zone shift at about 70%).

**Vehicle:** Doug rides a winch cable from the surface research ship (`research_ship.json`, `diver.json` / `submersible.json` exist; the
director and art director choose). Ends with the ocean-standard **LIVE feed** closing (series bible).

**Episode-only device, proposed: WHY SO BIG?** A crude clipboard in the corner. The narrator's hand adds one handwritten reason at the
item that demonstrates it: COLD (item 2 or 4), MORE OXYGEN (item 2/3, the one 025 callback), FOOD IS RARE (item 4), LIVE LONGER (item 6),
BRING YOUR OWN FOOD (item 7), and at item 10 "PRESSURE?" is written and **crossed out** (pressure sets limits, it doesn't make giants).
Every line is a hypothesis stated as one ("scientists think", "one idea is"), sourced in facts.md. The death counter always stays.

### Big.
**1. Giant Oarfish** (*Regalecus glesne*; about 250 m; the hook item; **Doug survives**)
- Facts: longest bony fish, reliably about 8 m (longer claims unconfirmed; use the conservative figure); filmed swimming vertically
  (2010s ROV footage); in Japan, "messenger from the sea god's palace" earthquake omen; Orihara et al. 2019 (*BSSA*) found no link between
  sightings and earthquakes. Most specimens ever seen were dying at the surface.
- Analogy: about as long as two cars parked end to end.
- Twist (by 0:45): "the earthquake fish" predicts nothing; almost everything we know comes from ones that were already dying.
- Doug beat: Doug holds up a hand-drawn "EARTHQUAKE?" sign beside the vertical fish; nothing happens. "Doug is fine." The narrator,
  quietly disappointed, starts the cable again.

**2. Japanese Spider Crab** (*Macrocheira kaempferi*; about 300-400 m; death 211)
- Facts: biggest leg span of any living arthropod, up to about 3.7 m; lives roughly 50-600 m off Japan; lifespan claims up to 100 years
  (hedge unless a strong source); reason COLD.
- Analogy: wider than a small car is long.
- Doug beat: Doug stretches his arms out to compare and loses by a lot; the crab picks up his cap and walks off downslope, Doug follows it
  too deep. Cartoon death, cap recovered from a claw. (The 009 spider-crab line is not repeated.)

**3. Giant Phantom Jelly** (*Stygiomedusa gigantea*; about 1,000 m on MBARI's ROV footage; death 212)
- Facts: bell about 1 m, four curtain-like arms up to about 10 m; no stinging tentacles; seen only about 100-odd times since 1899;
  recorded down to about 6,665 m; a small fish (*Thalassobathia*) is sometimes seen living alongside it.
- Analogy: arms as long as a school bus is (verify against the arm figure; else a different analogy).
- Twist: "It doesn't sting. It doesn't need to." Doug beat: Doug offers to be its little fish companion ("roommates?"), gets folded into
  the curtains. Cartoon only, cut on the wrap.

### Bigger.
**4. Giant Isopod** (*Bathynomus giganteus*; about 1,000 m; **Doug survives**)
- Facts: up to about 50 cm, a roly-poly relative; 170-2,140 m; the Toba Aquarium individual refused food for about five years (2009-2014);
  reason FOOD IS RARE (big bodies store more and can wait).
- Analogy: a woodlouse the length of a house cat.
- Doug beat: "hi friend?" plus a fish on a plate; the isopod ignores it for five years (calendar pages fly). "Doug is fine." Narrator:
  the isopod outlasted the narrator's patience. No out-waiting folding-chair gag (spent in 022).

**5. Colossal Squid** (*Mesonychoteuthis hamiltoni*; about 1,500 m; death 213)
- Facts: heaviest invertebrate, the 2007 Ross Sea specimen about 495 kg (Te Papa, Wellington; NZ is our #2 country); eyes about 27 cm,
  among the largest in the animal kingdom; swivelling hooks on arms and clubs; first described in 1925 from pieces in a sperm whale
  stomach; first confirmed footage of a live one in the wild in 2025 (Schmidt Ocean Institute, a juvenile about 30 cm, about 600 m, South
  Sandwich Islands). Adults are thought to live to 2,000 m or more.
- Analogy: the weight of a grand piano (verify, about 400-500 kg).
- Twist: a century after discovery, the first live one anyone filmed was a baby the length of a ruler.
- One callback to 001: "Doug met the giant squid. This is its heavier cousin." Doug beat: the hooks catch the cap first; cartoon death.

**6. Greenland Shark** (*Somniosus microcephalus*; about 1,800-2,000 m, Arctic; death 214)
- Facts: Nielsen et al. 2016 (*Science*, eye-lens radiocarbon): the largest female about 392 years old, ±120; matures at about 150;
  grows about 1 cm a year; about 5 m (larger claims hedge); among the slowest-swimming fish for its size (Watanabe et al. 2012);
  water near 0 °C; stomachs have held scavenged land animals (say "scavenged", never that it hunts them). Reason LIVE LONGER.
- Analogy: alive when ships still crossed the Atlantic under sail (the writer picks one sourced historical anchor inside the ±120 range).
- Doug beat: the shark is so slow Doug thinks he can outswim it; the shark doesn't have to try. The cold takes him first. Cartoon frost
  X eyes (no ice-block return, spent in 007). No eye-parasite material.

**7. Giant Tube Worm** (*Riftia pachyptila*; about 2,500 m, East Pacific Rise / Galápagos Rift; death 215)
- Facts: up to about 2 m (verify the max); no mouth, no gut; bacteria inside it make its food from vent chemicals; Lutz et al. 1994
  (*Nature*): among the fastest-growing marine invertebrates (more than 80 cm a year); reason BRING YOUR OWN FOOD.
- Analogy: a lipstick-red garden hose as tall as a doorway.
- Doug beat: Doug offers lunch to something with no mouth. "It brought its own." He leans on the warm rock; the vent water does the rest
  (puff of cartoon steam, cap drifts). `tubeworm_cluster.json` exists. Not a repeat of 006's vent items (no Champagne Vent, scaly-foot snail).

### Too deep.
**8. Bigfin Squid** (*Magnapinna*; about 4,000 m; **Doug survives**)
- Facts: elbowed arms held at right angles, estimated up to about 8 m including the filaments (Vecchione et al. 2001, *Science*);
  seen on only a few dozen videos; no adult ever caught; records from about 1,940 m to over 6,000 m (writer: confirm the deepest record).
- Analogy: arms longer than a giraffe is tall.
- Twist: we've only ever seen it on camera, never held one. Doug beat: it hangs motionless beside him, ignores him entirely.
  "Doug is fine." Narrator: "Nobody knows what it eats. It has decided it isn't Doug."

**9. Supergiant Amphipod** (*Alicella gigantea*; about 6,000-7,000 m, Kermadec Trench, NZ; death 216)
- Facts: up to about 34 cm when most amphipods are a few millimetres to a couple of centimetres; found at about 4,000-7,000+ m; a 2025
  study (verify authors, *Royal Society Open Science*) found it across about 59% of the world's ocean and genetically one species. Reason COLD
  plus MORE OXYGEN (Chapelle & Peck 1999, *Nature*: maximum amphipod size tracks oxygen). **The 025 callback lives here, one line.**
- Analogy: a shrimp-like scavenger the length of a school ruler (verify 30 cm vs 34 cm).
- Doug beat: Doug lowers a sandwich as bait; the swarm arrives. Cut on the cloud; the cap floats up alone. **Never describe eating Doug.**
  001 already did the pollutants-in-amphipods twist; do not repeat it.

**10. The Fish Line** (about 8,200-8,400 m; death 217)
- Facts: Yancey et al. 2014 (*PNAS*): fish need more of a protective molecule (TMAO) the deeper they go, and around 8,200-8,400 m their
  bodies would hit a limit; the deepest fish ever filmed was a snailfish at 8,336 m (Izu-Ogasawara Trench, 2022, announced 2023,
  Jamieson et al.). Below this, no fish has ever been seen. The narrator crosses out "PRESSURE?" on WHY SO BIG?: pressure doesn't build
  giants, it builds limits.
- Analogy: stack Mount Everest on the line and its summit would still be under water (8,849 m vs about 8,300 m; check the wording).
- One callback to 001 (Doug met a hadal snailfish). Doug beat: Doug crosses the line with a "FISH?" sign. "Doug is not a fish. The ocean
  noticed." Cartoon pancake or "the pressure does the rest, quietly". No body detail.

**11. The Giant Cell** (xenophyophores; Sirena Deep, Mariana Trench, about 10,641 m; the boss; death 218)
- Facts: single-celled organisms (giant foraminifera) that build fragile shells; some species reach about 10-20 cm (the largest,
  *Syringammina fragilissima*, lives far shallower: say so); 2011 Scripps/NatGeo drop-cameras found xenophyophores at about 10,641 m in
  the Sirena Deep, the deepest ever recorded; they host many smaller animals. Writer: confirm size of the trench specimens.
- Analogy: one cell as big as your fist (if the trench specimens are smaller, use them and reserve the fist for the record species).
- Twist (the payoff of the why-thread): at the very bottom, the giants aren't animals at all. The biggest thing down here is one cell.
- Doug beat: "hi friend?" to a single cell. Death 218 played flat. **LIVE feed closing:** the ship monitor, red "LIVE" tag, the cap drifts
  down and settles on the xenophyophore, which, per the facts, is home to other things already. One dry kicker (writer's own).

## 7. Risks
- **Gore:** cartoon only (X eyes, ghost Doug, floating cap, cut to black). No descriptions of hooks in flesh, crushing, eating,
  freezing injuries, scalding or decompression. The amphipod swarm and the colossal squid are cut on the moment, never shown.
- **Kid appeal:** giant sea creatures are popular with children. Keep the narration adult-coded and dry; no "wow, kids", no cute voices,
  no nursery adjectives ("cute", "little buddy"). The thumbnail must not look like a children's ocean poster: dark depth colours, no
  smiley creatures.
- **No safety or medical advice**, no diving tips.
- **Honesty:** gigantism is real for some groups (amphipods, isopods, sea spiders, some squid and sharks) and not others (many deep fish are
  small); say so once. Every WHY SO BIG? reason is a hypothesis with a source.
- **Facts that need strong sources (primary paper or institution page in facts.md):** oarfish length (use 8 m, not 11 m) and Orihara
  2019; spider crab span and any lifespan claim; phantom jelly arm length and sighting count; Toba isopod fast dates; colossal squid mass,
  eye size, 1925 description and the 2025 footage details; Greenland shark age with its ±120 range (never "512 years" as fact); Riftia
  max length and growth rate; bigfin squid arm estimate and deepest record; *Alicella* size and the 2025 distribution study; Yancey 2014
  and the 8,336 m fish; xenophyophore depth (10,641 m) and size of trench specimens.
- **Repetition:** do not reuse 001's zone headers ("Sunlight zone", "Twilight zone"), 006's "at Every Depth" phrasing or the depth gauge
  styling unchanged; the axis meter should read as a cable counter, not 006's gauge.
