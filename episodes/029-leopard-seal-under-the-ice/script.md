The Chase.

The Ice Edge.

The Visitor.

The Head.

[A tourist's photo pinned to a board: a leopard seal on the ice with its mouth wide open, looking like a huge grin. A sticky note on it: "WARNING SIGN".]

[A page from a rulebook: "SIGNS A SEAL IS DISTURBED". A checklist: head turning, sitting up, moving away, "OPEN MOUTH THREAT DISPLAY". The last line is circled in red.]

The wildlife guidelines that Antarctic tour operators follow list the signs that a seal is disturbed. ## The Gift

The Gift.

[Underwater, pale blue light under broken ice. A diver with a big camera. A huge seal coming straight at him.]

In Antarctica, a leopard seal once spent four days trying to feed a human.

[Caption: "2006". The seal's open mouth around the camera and the diver's head, drawn simply, no teeth detail.]

The human was the wildlife photographer Paul Nicklen, diving there on assignment for National Geographic in two thousand six. A large female came straight at him, and her head, the magazine reported, was bigger than a grizzly bear's. She took his camera, and his head, into her mouth.

[The seal swims past the diver with a live penguin and lets it go in front of him. The penguin darts away.]

## The Sieve

The Sieve.

[The seal's open mouth, drawn as a diagram, side on. The back teeth, each with three sharp points, interlocking into a grid. Label: "SIEVE".]

The leopard seal, the reptile-headed penguin hunter, can also feed like a whale.

[A pink cloud of tiny krill. Label: "KRILL".]

Much of its diet isn't penguins or seals at all, but krill, small shrimp-like animals that live in dense swarms. To eat them, it uses its back teeth, which each have several sharp points, and when the jaws close, those points lock together into a sieve.

[A kitchen pasta strainer drawn beside the closed teeth. Same shape of gaps.]

It works like a pasta strainer, made of teeth.

[A zoo pool with a sign: "TARONGA ZOO, SYDNEY". A seal and a small fish. Arrows: water in, then water squirting out sideways between the teeth. A stopwatch: "UNDER 1/3 SECOND".]

A study published in two thousand thirteen filmed leopard seals feeding at Taronga Zoo in Sydney. With small prey, they didn't bite, they sucked it in, then pushed the seawater back out sideways through the sieve, and each suction lasted, on average, less than a third of a second.

Here's the part nobody had seen.

[An old textbook page with a question mark next to the back teeth. Then the question mark is crossed out.]

Scientists had long suspected what those back teeth were for, but this was the first time anyone documented it happening. The animal famous for its fangs is also, quietly, a vacuum cleaner.

[Doug floating in the middle of the krill cloud, cap on, holding a hand-lettered sign: "KRILL".]

Doug is hiding in a swarm of krill, carrying a sign that says krill, in case anybody has doubts.

[A huge whoosh. The krill cloud is gone. Cut to black. The red cap pops out through the gaps in the teeth and floats. DOUG DEATHS: 238.]

The seal kept the krill and spat out the cap. Doug counted as krill.

Krill is the everyday meal, but on some beaches, the leopard seal specializes in other seals.

## The Seal Eater

The Seal Eater.

[A rocky beach crowded with small fur seal pups. A card: "ABOUT 7 IN 10 PUPS, ON AVERAGE, EACH YEAR".]

On one Antarctic beach, leopard seals have eaten, on average, about seven in every ten fur seal pups born there each year.

[A map: Livingston Island, a red dot on Cape Shirreff. A field hut with a flag.]

The beach is Cape Shirreff, on Livingston Island, where American government scientists have studied the fur seals for twenty years. Since two thousand ten, they estimate leopard seals have eaten an average of sixty-nine percent of the pups born there each year. Pup numbers have fallen by eighty-six percent since two thousand seven.

[A row of ten cinema seats. At the end of the shot, seven are empty.]

Picture ten seats in a row at the cinema, and by the end of the summer, seven of them are empty.

[About twenty seal shapes along the shore. Then a few larger ones are circled: "BIG FEMALES".]

It doesn't even require many seals, since on a typical peak day only about twenty were seen hunting there simultaneously. A two thousand twenty-five study of their whiskers concluded that a few large females, specializing in this prey, have likely helped drive the collapse.

The disturbing part is the survivors.

[Pack ice. A group of crabeater seals hauled out. Each one has three simple pale lines on its side.]

Out in the pack ice, crabeater seals live alongside them. The majority of adult crabeaters carry long scars, often in pairs, that are thought to come from leopard seal attacks they escaped when they were young. Most of the crabeaters you'll ever see have already survived this animal once.

[Doug arrives in the seal costume, cap on top. Speech bubble: "hi friends?" Every crabeater has stripes. Doug's costume is perfectly clean.]

Doug has joined the crabeaters in his seal costume, and he's the only one without stripes, which tells the leopard seal exactly who hasn't been tried yet.

[A splash. Cut to black. The seal costume floats flat, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 239.]

So far, it has hunted seals, penguins and Doug, and once, on record, it killed a person.

## The Snorkeller

The Snorkeller.

[Sober card: white text on black. "JULY 2003. ROTHERA RESEARCH STATION, ANTARCTICA. ONE PERSON DIED." Below, smaller: "Sources: British Antarctic Survey; Muir, Barnes and Reid, Antarctic Science, 2006." No Doug. No music sting.]

In July two thousand three, a marine biologist with the British Antarctic Survey was snorkelling near Rothera Research Station when a leopard seal attacked her, and she died.

It is the first human death known to have been caused by a leopard seal, and snorkelling at the station was prohibited afterwards.

This is the part that matters.

[Second card, same style: "1970 TO 2003: 4 CONTACTS IN BRITISH ANTARCTIC SURVEY DIVE LOGS".]

Attacks like this are extraordinarily rare. In more than thirty years of the Survey's diving and snorkelling records, a leopard seal made physical contact with someone four times. Most of the seals that approach people in the water are simply curious, and in those records, none of the curious ones went on to attack.

[Doug, bareheaded, sets the red cap down on the ice and stands beside it. Two seconds. No joke.]

The leopard seal earns its reputation from penguins and seals, not from people.

Under the ice.

## The Song

The Song.

[Under the pack ice: a blue-grey ceiling of broken floes. A single leopard seal below it. Music notes drift away from it through the water.]

Under the Antarctic pack ice, in the breeding season, a male leopard seal can sing for up to thirteen hours a day.

[A wide map card: a ring of broken ice around the continent. Label: "PACK ICE: HOME".]

This is where the leopard seal actually lives, in the broken sea ice that surrounds the continent. From November to January, the males dive under it and sing in bursts of about two minutes, then surface for a breath and dive again.

[A clock. A full workday shaded in, then five more hours shaded in another colour. Label: "13 HOURS".]

Thirteen hours is an average full-time workday, then five more hours.

[A figure on a quad bike crossing the ice. Sleeping seals with bright dye marks. Caption: "1990s".]

In the nineteen nineties, the biologist Tracey Rogers rode a quad bike out across the ice and marked twenty-six sleeping males with dye, so she would know exactly who was singing.

[Five simple symbols on a card, each a different call. Then the same five in different orders, one row per seal.]

Every song is built from the same five calls, and each male puts them in his own order. In two thousand twenty-five, researchers at the University of New South Wales measured how predictable those songs are. They came out about as predictable as simple human songs, and more predictable than classical or modern music, a structure that helps the song reach distant listeners accurately. Rogers has suggested that, to rival males, the song could mean this patch is taken.

And here's the twist.

[Pull back: the whole video's path drawn as a dotted line, from the floe, to the edge, to the boat, to the water, and down to here.]

Doug has spent this whole video being hunted from below. Down here, where the leopard seal actually lives, it isn't hunting at all. It's singing the same five notes, over and over, to anyone who'll listen.

[Doug under the ice in a diving mask, cap over it, hanging upside down from the ice ceiling beside the singing seal. His eyes are closed. He is enjoying it.]

Doug listened. That was the problem.

[Doug starts humming along. Small music notes from Doug. The seal's notes stop. The seal turns its head.]

To a rival male, that song could mean this patch is taken, and Doug had just sung one back.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 240. The field guide LEOPARD SEAL page: "MAYBE" is rubbed out and a red "NO" is stamped in the FRIEND? box.]

[Back on the surface: a bright white floe. The red cap blows across the ice on its own. Under the ice, a dark shape follows the cap's small shadow. The cap slides to a crack and drops in.]

The first thing it followed was Doug's shadow, and now it's following the cap's.

[End card: "DOUG DEATHS: 240".]

Doug deaths: two hundred and forty. The cap is under the ice now. The next place Doug really shouldn't go is right there on screen.
