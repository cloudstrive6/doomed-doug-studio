### On the ice.

**1. The Chase** (death 235)
- Facts:
  - On Shackleton's Endurance expedition (1914-17, on the drifting sea ice in 1916), a leopard seal chased crew member Thomas Orde-Lees
    across the ice.
  - It disappeared under the floe and came up in front of him. It had **tracked his shadow from beneath the ice**.
  - Frank Wild shot it.
  - Sources: Shackleton's *South* (1919, public domain, Project Gutenberg) and the PBS NOVA "Shackleton's Voyage" wildlife page.
    **Quote neither. Verify the shadow detail against *South* or Lansing.** If only PBS has it, word it as "according to the expedition's
    accounts".
  - Optional extra, verify it or cut it: the men then ate the seal, so the hunter fed the expedition.
- Size facts:
  - Leopard seals are about 3 m long, and up to about 3.5-3.8 m. Females are larger than males. Weight is up to about 500 kg.
  - Use the Australian Antarctic Division / BAS species pages, and give the range.
- Analogy: a 3.5 m seal is longer than a small car (source both).
- **Twist by 0:45:** it hunted him **from underneath**. The ice was not between them; it was a window.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug skis across a floe with the cap on. A dark shape follows his shadow under the ice.
  - "Doug did not agree to this." (once, here).
  - Death 235. Cut to black, and the cap is left on the snow.

**2. The Ice Edge** (death 236; costume gag 1 of 2)
- Facts:
  - Leopard seals patrol the ice edge and the water off penguin colonies, and take penguins as they enter or leave the water
    (Australian Antarctic Division; a peer-reviewed predation study such as Ainley et al. 2005 at Cape Crozier or Rogers & Bryden 1995;
    verify).
  - Optional, verify it or cut it: a 1985 expedition member was grabbed by the leg at the ice edge and pulled toward the water, and his
    companions kicked the seal off. **No name and no injury detail.**
- Analogy: one sourced figure, such as the time a penguin is in the "danger strip" or the strip's width, against an everyday distance.
  If neither is sourced, use body length against a doorway.
- Twist: the safest-looking place in Antarctica, the edge where the ice meets the water, is where it waits.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug is zipped into a penguin costume ("which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a penguin"). The cap is on top.
  - The other penguins make him go first.
  - Death 236. **Abstract:** a splash, cut to black, and the costume floats flat. No penguin is ever shown being caught.

### In a boat.

**3. The Visitor** (Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine."; narrator quietly disappointed)
- Facts:
  - Leopard seals are regular visitors to New Zealand, not lost strays. A citizen-science study found hundreds of NZ records
    (Hupman et al. 2020, *Marine Mammal Science* or similar; verify the journal, number and wording).
  - Owha, a leopard seal living in Auckland's Waitematā Harbour, punctured, flooded and capsized dinghies at Westhaven Marina and caused
    thousands of dollars of damage (1News 2019/2020; Wikipedia "Owha" for leads only, with a primary news or NIWA source in facts.md).
  - She is protected under NZ's Marine Mammal Protection Act 1978 (one clause).
- Analogy: the trip from Antarctica to Auckland (about 3,000-4,000 km, sourced) against a familiar route.
- Twist: one of them moved into a city marina and started sinking boats.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug rows an inflatable dinghy into the marina. A huge head rests on the side and the dinghy fills with water.
  - "Doug is fine." He is standing waist-deep in a marina, in his cap. The narrator is quietly disappointed.
  - Counter stays at 236.
- **Policy:** no "she was shot" detail (an unverified 2019 report) and no tone that mocks real boat owners.

**4. The Head** (death 237)
- Facts:
  - The skull is long and reptile-like.
  - The front canines are about 2.5 cm, verify it or cut it.
  - The jaw opens unusually wide: verify the gape figure (often quoted as about 160°). If there is no primary or museum source, cut the
    number and say "unusually wide".
  - The seals put their heads over the side of inflatable boats in Antarctica, and expedition guidelines set approach distances
    (IAATO guidance; verify).
- Analogy: the head is the size of a [sourced object]. Pick one and source it.
- Twist: the "smile" people photograph is the hunting tool, and it is pointed at the boat on purpose. Word it to the source: curiosity
  or investigation, not "attack".
- Doug beat:
  - Doug leans out of a Zodiac for a selfie with the head. Death 237. The cap is left on the pontoon.

### In the water.

**5. The Gift** (Doug SURVIVES; FRIEND? "MAYBE")
- Facts:
  - In Antarctica, a large female leopard seal took photographer Paul Nicklen's head and camera into her mouth and did not harm him.
  - She then brought him penguins over about four days, first live and then dead, apparently because he was a hopeless hunter
    (National Geographic, "How a Leopard Seal Fed Me Penguins", 2014, about his 2006 assignment; NPR *Fresh Air* interview).
  - **Say "apparently".** Nobody knows why she did it.
- Analogy: four days against something everyday (one sentence).
- Twist: the most feared predator in Antarctica decided the human was too useless to survive and tried to feed him.
- Doug beat:
  - The seal pushes a **live** penguin toward Doug. Doug misses it, and it swims off. The seal looks at him.
  - "Doug is fine." The narrator: the seal is also disappointed.
  - FRIEND? MAYBE. Counter stays at 237.
  - **Overlap:** 021's closing (an orca drops a fish into Doug's boat) was used once.
    - This item is a different, real event and gets **no** 021 callback line.
    - Its visual is the live penguin escaping, not food landing in a boat.
  - **Dead penguins are never drawn.**

**6. The Sieve** (death 238)
- Facts:
  - The back teeth have interlocking cusps that work as a sieve for straining krill (Hocking, Evans & Fitzgerald 2013, *Polar Biology*,
    "Leopard seals use suction and filter feeding when hunting small prey underwater").
  - The same paper shows they **suck prey in** (suction feeding).
  - Krill can make up a large share of the diet in some places and seasons (Hall-Aspland & Rogers 2004 or a similar source; give the
    figure only as the source states it).
- Analogy: the sieve is like a pasta strainer. The writer keeps this as an explanatory comparison, not a fact claim.
- Twist: the hunter with the reptile grin spends much of its time eating like a whale, and it can suck food in like a vacuum.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug floats in a cloud of krill, holding a "KRILL" sign so he blends in.
  - Whoosh. Death 238. The cap is strained out through the teeth. Cartoon only, no inside-the-mouth view.

**7. The Seal Eater** (death 239; costume gag 2 of 2, using the existing `seal_costume.json`)
- Facts:
  - Many adult crabeater seals carry leopard-seal scars from attacks they survived as pups.
    - The figure is often given as "up to 78% of crabeaters that survive their first year" (Siniff & Bengtson; **verify the year,
      1977 or 1997**).
    - If the primary paper can't be reached, use "most" with the source that states it.
  - At Cape Shirreff (Livingston Island), leopard seals eat a large share of Antarctic fur seal pups, and a few large females do most
    of it.
    - Sources: NOAA AMLR reports, Krause et al. 2015/2020, and the 2025 whisker-isotope study.
    - **The "70% of pups" figure is from media only.** Use a peer-reviewed figure or none.
- Analogy: the scar share as "about three in four", against an everyday group (a classroom of 20, so 15 marked). Pick a neutral
  group; no children's framing.
- Twist: most of the crabeaters you'll ever see are survivors of this animal, and they still carry the stripes.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug in the seal costume, cap on top, among the crabeaters ("hi friends?"). They all have scars, and he doesn't.
  - Death 239. The costume floats flat. No wound detail on any seal: draw the scars as three simple pale lines.

**8. The Snorkeller** (sober card; **no Doug death and no Doug on this item**; counter stays at 239)
- Facts:
  - In 2003, a British Antarctic Survey marine biologist was killed by a leopard seal while snorkelling near Rothera Research Station.
  - It is the only recorded human death caused by a leopard seal.
  - Source: BAS statement; Muir, Barnes & Reid 2006, *Polar Record*, or the official inquest coverage. Verify the citation.
  - Afterwards, rules for in-water work near the seals were tightened (verify with BAS).
- **Decision 7 conditions:**
  - No name, no depth or injury detail, no joke.
  - A plain sober card in white text on black: the year, the station, "one person died", and the source line.
  - The narrator says it in 2-3 flat sentences.
- **Honesty line, required:** attacks on people are very rare. This is the only recorded death, and most encounters end with nothing
  happening. This keeps the title honest and the tone adult.
- Doug is shown only as **the cap set down on the ice, Doug standing beside it, bareheaded**. It is a respect beat, not a joke, and
  lasts 2 seconds.
- About 100-130 words. It is the shortest item.

### Under the ice.

**9. The Song** (the boss; death 240)
- Facts:
  - In the breeding season, male leopard seals hang upside down under the ice and call for hours.
  - The calls are built from five sound types, are very repetitive, and carry a long way across and through the ice.
  - The 2025 UNSW study (Chambers & Rogers) found the songs are about as predictable as simple human songs.
    - **Say "about as predictable as simple songs"; do not say "nursery rhymes".** It is a kid-content trigger for us.
    - Rogers recorded the songs in the 1990s by marking sleeping males with dye. Verify, then pick one of these details.
  - Leopard seals live in the pack ice, so this is their home ground (one line).
- Analogy: one sourced figure for how far the song carries or how loud it is, against an everyday sound. Cut the analogy if it isn't
  sourced.
- **Twist (the payoff of the promise):** Doug is finally where the leopard seal lives. He has spent the whole video being hunted from
  below, and down here it isn't hunting at all. It is singing the same few notes, over and over, to anyone who'll listen. The
  narrator: "Doug listened. That was the problem." Death 240 comes from the female that turns up, or from the ice closing over his
  hole. Play it flat.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug is under the ice in a diving mask, cap over it, upside down to copy the seal.
  - He hums along. The song stops.
  - Death 240, cut to black. FRIEND? stamped "NO".
- **Closing image:** back on the surface. A dark shape follows the **cap's** shadow across the ice, mirroring item 1, and the cap slides
  into a crack.
  - Kicker, which the writer rewords: "It followed Doug's shadow first. It has now followed the cap."
- **Outro:** 30 words or fewer. The final counter (240) and one dry line, re-hooked to the end slate. No recap and no subscribe pitch.

  - Penguin kills stay off-screen.
