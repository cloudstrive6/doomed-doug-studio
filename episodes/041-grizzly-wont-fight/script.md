## The Wolf Pack

The Wolf Pack.

[Six wolves circle a mother bear and two cubs in the snow. Red curved arrows show the wolves in back rushing in. Label: "FROM BEHIND".]

In June two thousand one, researchers watched six wolves surround a mother grizzly and her two cubs on the way to a carcass. While the bears faced the wolves in front, the others rushed in and bit at them from behind.

[A tape measure stretches one hundred meters to the carcass. A stopwatch beside it ticks to "20 MIN".]

The bears needed about twenty minutes to cover the last hundred meters to the food. That pack had twenty more grown wolves that were not even there.

[A calendar flips from 1995 to 2002. A tally board fills up to "96".]

It was one of ninety-six run-ins between wolves and grizzlies recorded in the park's first eight years with wolves back. Wherever the two meet, most fights are over food or over young.

Here's the strange part.

## The Wolverine

The Wolverine.

[The wolverine stands on the elk carcass, teeth bared, facing a grizzly that is far bigger. Label: "THE LEGEND".]

The internet says a wolverine can chase a grizzly off its dinner, and on paper that makes no sense at all.

[A side-by-side: a wolverine skull next to a dog skull, the wolverine's jaw much thicker. Label: "THICKEST JAWS".]

A big male wolverine weighs up to about eighteen kilograms, and a big male grizzly can weigh more than fifteen times that. But the wolverine has the heaviest skull, the thickest jaws and the biggest teeth of any weasel on land, and it can crack bones and frozen meat. It is the biggest weasel on land, and it is still only about the size of a medium dog.

[A crude sandwich on a plate next to a running chainsaw. A stick hand reaches for the sandwich. Label: "NO".]

Taking food from one is like stealing a sandwich from a running chainsaw.

Here's the problem.

The stories of wolverines driving bears off carcasses are mostly unverified reports. Wildlife biologists in Alaska say wolverines usually keep well away from bears and wolves, because a meeting with either one can kill the wolverine, and wolves are the main animal that does.

In the Yellowstone area, researchers found a radio-collared wolverine that had been killed by a black bear, next to an elk it had tried to drag away. So a bear can win. One did, once. Even so, a fight with an animal that does not stop is a lot of trouble for one dinner.

## The Porcupine

The Porcupine.

[The porcupine, tight on its back. A label counts up: "10,000... 20,000... 30,000".]

A North American porcupine carries about thirty thousand quills, and it cannot throw or shoot a single one of them.

[A bear paw comes near. A few quills stay in the air where the porcupine was. Label: "COME OFF EASILY".]

It doesn't need to. The quills are only loosely attached, so they come away the moment something touches them, and the porcupine itself weighs about nine kilograms and would mostly rather leave.

[Zoom on one quill tip. Several dozen tiny black barbs, all pointing back. Label: "BARBS".]

Each quill tapers to a point covered in several dozen tiny barbs, and when the barbs get wet, they swell and help work the quill in. A quill that has lodged keeps moving inward, about a millimeter an hour.

[A pincushion with a tiny smug face.]

A porcupine is a pincushion that bites back by holding still.

The worst part is how far that goes.

[A row of animal outlines: coyote, cougar, bear, fisher. Each gets a small red "!" on the nose. No detail.]

Quills have been found stuck in coyotes, cougars, bears and fishers. In the southern Yellowstone area, researchers who tracked fifty-nine cougar deaths over fifteen years found two young cats that had hunted porcupines and died from the quills afterwards.

[A fisher flips a porcupine onto its back. The bear watches from a distance.]

The best-known porcupine hunter is the fisher, and even it has to go for the face or flip the porcupine over. Other predators simply learn to leave them alone. Some learn once.

[Doug zipped into a porcupine costume, deadpan, the red cap on top. The bear sniffs, sees the quills, and leaves.]

Doug is wearing a porcupine costume, so the bear will leave him alone, and it works, because the bear leaves him alone.

## The Bull Moose

The Bull Moose.

[A cow moose rears up and strikes with both front hooves. A grizzly tumbles backwards in a full somersault. Label: "ONE BIOLOGIST SAW THIS".]

In the nineteen eighties, an Alaska biologist watched a cow moose box a male grizzly so hard that the bear went somersaulting backwards. The bear had come for her calf. That was a cow.

[A bull moose with a huge rack. Label: "UP TO 700 KG".]

A large bull weighs up to about seven hundred kilograms. In the fall rut, it shoves other bulls with a rack it grew in a single summer.

[Diagram of a moose front leg with red arrows: up, forward, sideways, down.]

Moose mostly kick with their front legs, and the hoof can come straight up, swing forward, kick out to the side, or come down on top of whatever is there.

[A vending machine tips off a shelf at shoulder height.]

A bull moose kick is a vending machine falling on you from shoulder height.

But that's not the whole picture.

[A tally board: "3 TO 4 ADULT MOOSE A YEAR".]

Grizzlies do kill moose. In one Alaska study, each adult male grizzly killed three or four adult moose a year, and each bear killed at least five calves. The highest kill rates came from males killing cows at calving time.

[A pie chart, almost all one colour: "OLDER KILLS: 88% COWS". Beside it, a row of collared bull moose, each with a green tick.]

In Sweden, researchers checked thirty-two adult moose killed by brown bears. Among the older moose, almost nine in ten were female, and not one of the male moose the researchers had radio-collared was killed by a bear.

The bear eats moose. It mostly leaves the bulls alone.

[Doug walks up to the bull holding out a moose-calf costume on a hanger, hopeful smile. The bull looks at it.]

Doug has read the numbers, so he brings the bull a moose-calf costume, so it can see how the calves feel.

We have met this moose before. It has not changed.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 318. The red cap hangs from one point of the antlers as the bull walks away.]

That's three hundred and eighteen.

The next animal is heavier still, and in Yellowstone the bears mostly wait for it to die on its own.

## The Bull Bison

The Bull Bison.

[A grizzly eating at a bison carcass half buried in snow. Label: "ALREADY DEAD".]

Yellowstone's grizzlies eat a lot of bison, and almost all of that meat comes from bison that were already dead when the bear arrived.

[A bull bison. Label: "UP TO 900 KG". Beside it, eleven Doug outlines in a row.]

A bull weighs up to about nine hundred kilograms, more than eleven Dougs. Wolves and grizzlies are the only large predators of adult bison in the park, and bison usually turn and face an attacker together, as a group. A bull can run about fifty kilometers an hour, spin around fast, and jump about one and a half meters.

[A small car with horns and a beard, facing the viewer.]

A healthy bull bison is a small car that has decided you are the road.

[The herd turns in a line toward a grizzly. The grizzly sits down.]

So the bears wait. Bison die of hunger and disease in winter and spring, and from injuries in the rut. In spring, when grizzlies come out of their dens, elk, bison and other hoofed animals make up much of what they eat, and a lot of it was killed by the winter.

The uncomfortable part is how patient the bear is.

[A calendar beside the bear flips: MARCH, APRIL, MAY. The bear keeps sitting.]

Grizzlies can take a bison calf, but one bear researcher's verdict is that a grown bison is too big, too well armed and too far from cover for a bear to kill outright.

[Doug sits beside a big bull in the snow, a folding chair, a thermos, waiting. A calendar flips. The bull keeps chewing.]

So Doug waits beside a bull for it to weaken a bit, so the bear can help, and the bison outlasts Doug.

[Calendar flips one last page. DOUG DEATHS: 319. The red cap sits on the bison's back as it walks off.]

Bison still have right of way.

[A sedge meadow by a river. A mother bear with two cubs eats grass near a road with parked cars. She keeps glancing over her shoulder.]

So far, the bear has only risked a meal, a face full of quills or a broken bone. The next animal is what a mother grizzly fears most.

Its cubs.

[BEAR'S COST tag flips to CUBS.]

## The Big Male

The Big Male.

[A huge male grizzly silhouette on a ridge. The mother bear below looks up.]

In one Swedish population of brown bears, the same species as the grizzly, about a third of cubs die every year. At least nine in ten of those deaths are caused by adult males that are not their father.

[The male, side-on. Label: "UP TO ABOUT 320 KG". A tall upright piano beside him for scale.]

A big male grizzly can weigh more than three hundred kilograms, which is about a tall upright piano that wants your kids gone.

[A calendar: MAY to JULY, highlighted red. Label: "MATING SEASON".]

Biologists call it sexually selected infanticide, which means a male kills young that aren't his, to get a chance to mate with their mother. The mating season runs from early May to mid-July, and that's when most of the cubs die.

But here's the clever part.

[A map. A red dot for the mother, a cluster of houses, a dotted line between them. Label: "ABOUT 780 M".]

In the Swedish study, the mothers that kept their cubs stayed closer to people. Their typical distance from houses was about seven hundred and eighty meters, against about twelve hundred for the mothers that lost them. Adult males there generally avoid people, so a house nearby works on them like a locked door. In British Columbia, bear viewing pushed big males away from a river, and the mothers with cubs spent more of their time fishing.

[The mother bear sits right beside a line of tourists with cameras. The big male stops at the treeline.]

The animal a mother grizzly has most to fear is another grizzly, so she hides behind us.

[Doug in front of the mother bear, arms out wide, cap on. A small sign: "BABYSITTER". The male looks at Doug.]

Doug offers to babysit, and the mother uses Doug the way she uses people, as a shield.

It works on the male. It works less well on Doug.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 320. The red cap sits at the edge of the sedge meadow. The field guide page: "MAYBE" is rubbed out.]

[Snow. Huge striped paw prints cross the frame.]

Every danger so far has cost the bear its dinner, its face, its bones or its cubs, but the next one eats bears.

Its life.

## The Amur Tiger

The Amur Tiger.

[BEAR'S COST tag: LIFE, stamped in red. A Siberian forest in deep snow. A tiger stalks a brown bear.]

In the Russian Far East, tigers kill and eat brown bears, the same species as the grizzly.

[The tiger. Label: "180 TO 300 KG".]

A male Amur tiger weighs about one hundred and eighty to three hundred kilograms. At the top end, that is a big touring motorbike, and it is still a cat.

[A map of the Russian Far East with one area highlighted: "SIKHOTE-ALIN". A tally board counts up to "763 KILLS".]

Over more than twenty years, from nineteen ninety-two to two thousand thirteen, in and around the Sikhote-Alin reserve, researchers found seven hundred and sixty-three animals killed by tigers. About two in every hundred were bears, brown or black.

[A row of twelve tiger droppings, cartoon brown blobs. One gets a small bear-face icon.]

Most of the time, these tigers eat deer and wild pigs, so a bear is not on the menu every week. Even so, bear remains turned up in roughly one in every twelve tiger droppings the researchers checked, which is a lot of bear for a cat.

But it doesn't only go one way.

[A brown bear walks up to a tiger's kill. The tiger backs off. Label: "AT LEAST 8 TIMES".]

Bears there regularly feed at tiger kills, about one kill in every six in one study, and at least eight times a bear pushed the tiger off its own kill. The bear sometimes wins. It just doesn't always get to choose the day.

[Doug stands in the snow between the brown bear and the tiger, one arm out toward each, like a referee. Hopeful smile.]

Doug stands between them in the snow, one arm out toward each, to make sure that it stays a fair fight.

[The tiger and the bear both look at Doug. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 321. The red cap sits in a tiger print in the snow.]

It was a fair fight, and Doug lost it.

[A forest trail in Montana. A grizzly walks along it. Far off, a small figure with a red cap.]

The tiger is the one animal here that hunts the bear, but the animal grizzlies go out of their way to avoid walks on two legs.

## The Human

The Human.

[A tally board: "17,171 REPORTS". Pie chart, mostly green: "CALM OR LEFT".]

Researchers in Yellowstone collected more than seventeen thousand reports of how bears reacted to people, and in most of them, the bears stayed calm or ran away.

[A grizzly turns its back on a hiker and walks into the trees.]

Grizzlies usually avoid trouble with people, and in that study, attacks were uncommon everywhere in the park.

[A map of the lower forty-eight states, almost entirely shaded brown. Label: "1800: ABOUT 50,000".]

Before eighteen hundred, about fifty thousand grizzlies lived in what is now the lower forty-eight states.

[The brown shading shrinks to a few small patches. Label: "1975: 700 TO 800".]

People shot, poisoned and trapped them wherever they were found, sometimes for government bounties. By nineteen seventy-five, only seven or eight hundred were left, and that year they were listed as threatened.

[The patches grow slightly. Label: "TODAY: ABOUT 2,300 / 6% OF THE RANGE".]

Today there are a little over two thousand, on about six percent of their old range. About a thousand of them live in and around Yellowstone, and most of the rest live in the northern Rockies of Montana.

[A big parking lot with twenty-two marked spaces. Twenty-one are empty. One battered pickup truck sits in the last space.]

If the old grizzlies were twenty-two cars in a parking lot, today there would be one car left, and it would be parked at the far end.

Here's the honest part.

[The empty parking lot at dusk. A grizzly silhouette stands beside the last pickup, looking toward the gate. A red-capped figure stands in it.]

The animal grizzlies avoid most is the one that took nearly all of their land, and it is the only one on this list that ever came close to finishing them off. Attacks on people are rare, and for most of history the danger has run the other way.

[Back on the trail. The grizzly sees Doug, stops, and turns away.]

The last bear came for him on purpose. This one won't.

[Doug, hopeful smile, follows the bear into the trees. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

For the first time, Doug is the scariest animal in the forest, and he is offended, so he follows it to make friends.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 322.]

Doug has died. Again.

The cap has been avoided. It is taking it personally.
