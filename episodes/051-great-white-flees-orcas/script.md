## The Exit

The Exit.

[Map of California, a pin on Southeast Farallon Island. A crowd of tiny shark icons with tag dots. SHARKS GONE: ABOUT 5 MIN.]

Four times, orcas turned up at Southeast Farallon Island, and four times the tagged great whites started leaving within minutes, as if someone had pulled a fire alarm.

That is an animal that can reach about six meters, longer than a family car, and almost nothing in the ocean is willing to hunt one. Almost.

Off San Francisco, researchers tagged one hundred and sixty-five white sharks at the island between two thousand six and two thousand thirteen. Every fall the sharks gather there to hunt young elephant seals, and they usually stay for more than a month.

The strange part is that the orcas were not always staying. Some of their visits lasted less than an hour, and the scientists described them as only passing through. The sharks left anyway.

[Field guide GREAT WHITE SHARK page. FRIEND? box empty.]

[Doug in a shark cage, zipped into the seal costume, cap on top. A sign on the cage: "BAIT".]

Doug was lowered into a shark cage in a seal costume, to attract the sharks. Doug did not agree to this. The sharks did not agree to it either, so they left.

[The shark icons stream off the map. Doug is the only seal-shaped thing in the water. Two bent fins arrive. Splash. The cap floats alone. DOUG DEATHS: 381. The narrator's hand writes "NO" in the FRIEND? box.]

The sharks needed only a few minutes to leave, and Doug was still doing up the flippers.

Off South Africa, scientists would soon find out what the sharks were running from.

Hours.

## The Liver

The Liver.

[A beach at Gansbaai. Five clean shark outlines on the sand, X eyes, no marks. Four have a small liver icon beside them with a red X. Label: "FEB TO JUNE 2017".]

Between February and June two thousand seventeen, five white sharks washed up on the beaches of Gansbaai, in South Africa, and four of them were missing their livers.

Orcas had taken the liver and, in some cases, the heart as well, and left everything else behind on the sand.

[A crude cutaway shark. A huge yellow shape inside, labelled "LIVER: UP TO 1/4".]

There is a reason they want that one organ: a white shark's liver can make up a quarter of its whole body weight.

[SHARKS GONE: HOURS.]

The other sharks got the message fast. At the Farallones in two thousand nine, all seventeen tagged sharks around the island were gone within eight hours, and at Gansbaai the tags showed sharks leaving straight away.

[The cutaway shark again. A drop icon labelled "OIL" fills the liver. A fuel gauge beside it reads FULL.]

The weird part is what that liver is made of. In sharks that scientists have tested, between half and four fifths of the liver's weight was oil. Gram for gram, dried liver holds nearly twice the energy of the shark's own muscle. Picture a car where a quarter of the weight is the fuel tank, and the thief only ever takes the fuel.

[Doug in the shark costume, cap on top. Two bent fins circle him. A crude X-ray card pops up: "LIVER: NOT FOUND."]

This is exactly why Doug is now dressed as a great white. The orcas inspected him closely, found no liver of any kind inside the costume, and swam off.

Doug is fine. The narrator had already sharpened the pencil.

Two years earlier, in False Bay, the orcas had already gone after a shark nobody had seen them hunt there.

Weeks.

## The Cousins

The Cousins.

[An underwater kelp site, False Bay. A crowd of spotted grey sharks with seven gill lines each. Label: "UP TO 70 IN ONE DIVE".]

Divers in False Bay, near Cape Town, could once see up to seventy broadnose sevengill sharks in a single one-hour dive.

That dive site was the largest known gathering of sevengills anywhere on Earth, and in False Bay these serious hunters shared the top of the food chain with the great whites. A big one is about three meters long, longer than a ping-pong table, and it has seven gill slits where most sharks have five.

Until two thousand fifteen, the orcas in False Bay had only ever been recorded eating marine mammals. Then, in two thousand fifteen and again in two thousand sixteen, scientists documented orcas killing sevengill sharks, and each time they ate only the liver.

But that's not the part that worried the researchers. After the attacks, the sevengills abandoned the biggest gathering site their species had, and it stayed empty for up to a month.

[Doug in a sevengill costume, seven gill lines drawn on with marker, cap on top, hovering hopefully among the real sevengills. The real sevengills leave. SHARKS GONE: WEEKS.]

Doug drew seven gill lines on a shark costume with a marker and joined the crowd. The crowd left, but two bent fins did not.

[Cut to black. The cap drifts down onto the kelp. DOUG DEATHS: 382.]

Doug has died. Again. To be fair to him, he had exactly the right number of gills.

Back at Gansbaai, the tour boats were about to learn how long a few weeks can feel.

## The Empty Cages

The Empty Cages.

[Gansbaai harbour. A row of cage-diving boats with empty cages. A wall calendar with weeks crossed off.]

Before two thousand seventeen, Gansbaai almost never went a week without great whites.

The town sits about a hundred kilometers east of Cape Town, and it was South Africa's largest gathering of white sharks and one of the most famous cage-diving spots in the world. In the years of records before the attacks, the sharks had gone missing for a week or more only twice. One gap lasted a week in two thousand seven, and the other lasted three weeks in two thousand sixteen. That is a bakery running out of bread twice in a decade.

After the orca attacks began, sharks that had been regulars did not show up for weeks, and sometimes for months. Researchers followed fourteen tagged sharks as they left the area while the orcas were around, in a study that ran for five and a half years.

The worst part is that it builds on itself. As the study's lead author put it, the more often the orcas visit these sites, the longer the great whites stay away.

[Doug in the shark costume, cap on top, inside a tourist cage. Tourists on the boat photograph him. Speech bubble from a tourist: "GREAT WHITE!"]

For a whole week, Doug in his costume was the only great white anyone at Gansbaai managed to photograph, and the tourists were thrilled.

[The calendar flips. The tour boat leaves. Two bent fins rise beside the empty cage. Cut. The cap floats past the cage. DOUG DEATHS: 383.]

Then the tour boat went home, and Doug stayed in the cage. The cage was never the problem. Meanwhile, back at the Farallones, the seals were about to have a wonderful few months.

Months.

## The Seals

The Seals.

[Back at the Farallones. A beach crowded with fat elephant seal pups. A tally board: "SHARK KILLS PER SEASON: ABOUT 40". SHARKS GONE: MONTHS.]

In the years when the sharks fled the Farallones, they made four to seven times fewer kills on elephant seals.

In a normal season, the sharks around the island take about forty elephant seals, most of them young ones, but after an orca visit the island simply went quiet. One of the researchers said that once the orcas showed up, they did not see a single shark, and the kills stopped.

The surprising part is that the orcas did not just chase the sharks away: by doing it, they protected the seals for months.

South Africa saw the same thing in False Bay, where seals living alongside great whites had high levels of stress hormones. When the sharks disappeared, those levels dropped sharply. The seals also began resting farther from their island, out over deep water, in places that a few years earlier would have been far too dangerous. It is a town relaxing the week the speed cameras come down.

[Doug in the seal costume, cap on top, lying on the rocks among the elephant seals. Speech bubble: "hi friends?"]

Doug went back to the Farallones in the seal costume, to enjoy the peace with his new neighbours.

Here's the catch: a bull elephant seal with nothing left to fear does not check what it is lying on.

[A huge bull elephant seal rolls over onto Doug. Flat costume, X eyes, cap on the rock. DOUG DEATHS: 384.]

Doug has died. Again. The sharks, meanwhile, were not planning to come back for a very long time.

A year.

## The Year

The Year.

[A crude calendar wheel, one full turn. SHARKS GONE: ABOUT 1 YEAR.]

After an orca visit, the Farallon sharks would not return for up to a year.

That is the researchers' own figure, and it needs one honest line. White sharks already leave the Farallones each winter and head far out into the Pacific, so the claim is not that they left for good. The claim is that after one short orca visit, they gave up the rest of their hunting season and did not come back until the next one.

What nobody expected was where they went instead. They did not stop eating, because tagged sharks turned up at Año Nuevo Island, farther down the California coast, and the number of white sharks there went up. The sharks did not starve: they just moved to the other restaurant.

To put the year in context, that is a whole season of seal hunting thrown away, a full trip around the sun cancelled because something swam past and kept going.

[Doug in the shark costume, cap on top, alone off the Farallones. Seasons change around him: leaves, snow, flowers, sun. Field guide: "MAYBE" pencilled in the FRIEND? box.]

Doug stayed behind in his great white costume to keep the spot warm. For a whole year nobody came, not the sharks, and not even the orcas.

Doug is fine. The narrator had drawn a death for every season.

In South Africa, a year turned out to be the short version.

Years.

## The Replacements

The Replacements.

[False Bay beaches. A tally board: "BRONZE WHALERS: 101". Label: "2019". No white shark count on the board; the zero is first shown at The Empty Bay.]

In two thousand nineteen, shark spotters in False Bay logged one hundred and one bronze whaler sightings.

Bronze whalers mostly hunt fish close to the shore. With the great whites gone, they moved in, both at Gansbaai and in False Bay.

They were not the only new tenants: sevengills started turning up at the boats off Seal Island, where great whites used to hunt, sometimes fifteen of them in a single day. One scientist described it as going from nothing to double digits, and the number of seals in the bay rose sharply too, while the small fish those seals eat began to decline. It is a new tenant moving into the flat before the old one's mail has even stopped.

And here's the uncomfortable part: in May two thousand nineteen, one of the shark-hunting orcas, known as Port, was photographed in False Bay with a bronze whaler in its mouth. The replacements are not safe either.

[Doug in the shark costume, cap on top, in a cage among bronze whalers, holding a bucket of fish labelled "BAIT".]

Doug was still dressed as a great white, so he expected a little respect from the newcomers. The bronze whalers did not care what he was dressed as, only about the bucket.

[Bronze whalers swarm the bucket. Cut to black. The cap hangs on the cage bar. DOUG DEATHS: 385.]

Doug has died. Again. The great whites, meanwhile, had not vanished, and some of them had simply moved.

## The Move

The Move.

[A map of South Africa's south coast. Arrows from Gansbaai and False Bay pointing east toward the Eastern Cape.]

After the attacks, some of the tagged white sharks from Gansbaai started heading east, along South Africa's southern coast.

A two thousand twenty-three study went further, finding that across the whole country, white shark numbers had stayed roughly stable since the species was protected in nineteen ninety-one. Reports of incidents between sharks and people had shifted from the Western Cape toward the Eastern Cape, so the authors suggested the population had not crashed at all. It had moved, most likely away from the orcas, like regulars abandoning their usual café for one further down the road.

The odd part is that other scientists strongly disagree. In two thousand twenty-four, they argued that the data cannot show either a stable population or a move to the east. They pointed out that the declines began before the shark-eating orcas were seen at those sites, and that fishing and shark nets also kill white sharks. Both sides agree that the orcas changed things, and they disagree about how much.

Moving east did not solve the problem either, because in two thousand twenty-three, further east at Mossel Bay, Starboard took a young white shark completely on his own.

[Doug drives east along the coast in a crude car, a "SHARKS?" sign on the roof, shark costume on the back seat. He finds a white shark in a new bay. It swims straight past him.]

Doug followed the sharks east, found one, and waved, and it ignored him completely.

Doug is fine.

The sharks found new bays to live in. One famous bay never got its sharks back.

## The Empty Bay

The Empty Bay.

[False Bay. Seal Island in the middle. A tally board: "2011: 300+". It wipes to "2019: 0". SHARKS GONE: YEARS, stamped red.]

In two thousand eleven, shark spotters at eight Cape Town beaches logged more than three hundred white shark sightings. In two thousand nineteen, they logged none.

False Bay used to be one of the most famous white shark sites on Earth, and Seal Island, in the middle of the bay, was where scientists went to watch great whites hunt. One of them called it the greatest place on Earth to see them.

The sightings started sliding around two thousand ten and fell faster from two thousand fifteen, and by the middle of two thousand eighteen the great whites were gone from the bay. Three hundred sightings is almost one a day, and zero is a stadium where the regulars stopped coming, and nobody ever resold the seats.

The bleak part is how little has changed since. In late two thousand twenty-three, there were four confirmed white shark sightings in False Bay in a single week. The head of the shark spotters called it exciting, and then admitted it was nowhere near the old numbers.

[Doug in the shark costume, cap on top, floating alone near Seal Island. The seals ignore him.]

The last time Doug was at Seal Island, the sharks were still there, and now Doug is the last great white in False Bay.

[Two bent fins rise on the horizon and turn toward him. The field guide's MAYBE is rubbed out.]

Two bent fins came back across the bay to check on him.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 386.]

Doug has died. Again. This time, nobody left the bay first.

[Closing: the red cap drifts past Seal Island. One small white shark passes, nudges the cap with its nose, and swims away. Field guide sticky note: "AFRAID OF ONE THING."]

One shark came back, checked the cap, and left. The cap stayed. It is the only thing in False Bay that nobody has told about the orcas.
