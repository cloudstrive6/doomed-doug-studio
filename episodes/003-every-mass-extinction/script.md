## Killer Moss

Killer Moss.

Four hundred and forty-three million years ago, animals had barely set foot on land. Almost all complex life lived in the sea, and the continents were bare rock.

That smear is one of the first plants on land, related to the ancestors of today's mosses. It has no wood, no flowers and no real roots, and it barely rises as high as your thumbnail.

Here's the twist. Some scientists think this little carpet helped start the first mass extinction.

[Red arrow from the moss into the rock, then down to the ocean. Label: "Weathering".]

As plants like this grow, they pull minerals out of the rock. When scientists grew a modern moss on rocks in incubators for three months, it sped up the rock's chemical breakdown. ## Gondwana Ice

Gondwana Ice.

Gondwana was a supercontinent that held what would become Africa, South America, Antarctica, Australia and India, and late in the Ordovician it drifted toward the South Pole.

[Map: a huge landmass sliding down to the bottom of the globe. White spreads across it.]

That was a problem, because land sitting at a pole is a perfect place to grow an ice sheet. An ice cap formed over what is now Africa and eastern South America, and it may have reached around thirty million square kilometers. That is roughly three times the size of the United States, all of it ice.

Every drop of water in that ice came out of the ocean, so sea level fell by up to roughly a hundred meters, the height of a building about thirty floors tall.

Here's the problem. In the Ordovician, huge shallow seas covered the middle of the continents, and many species lived nowhere else. When the water drained away, their homes simply vanished. The survivors tried to move to the edges of the continents, but those spots were already taken by animals that had held them for a long time.

[Before and after: a lush shallow sea, then the same spot as a dry, cracked plain with stranded shells.]

## Dead Reefs

Dead Reefs.

About three hundred and seventy-two million years ago, the seas of the Devonian held something spectacular, because this period saw the largest development of animal-built reefs in the history of the planet.

[A huge reef in cross-section: corals and lumpy sponge mounds, fish everywhere.]

The main builders were corals and a group of reef-building sponges called stromatoporoids. Then came two pulses of ocean anoxia, which is the scientific way of saying the water ran out of oxygen, and they are named the Lower and Upper Kellwasser events. Marine life suffered massive losses in both.

The evidence is a pair of black stripes in the rock, because water with no oxygen leaves behind a dark rock called black shale. Rhythms in these layers suggest the first took about ninety thousand years to form, and the second about a hundred and ten thousand. The rock in that section built up at roughly half a centimeter every thousand years, about the thickness of three stacked coins.

[Rock column. Two black bands. Red arrows: "Lower", "Upper".]

But that's not the disturbing part. One proposed cause is stronger weathering on land, which would have washed nutrients into the sea and fed huge blooms of algae, and the rotting blooms would have used up the oxygen. The seas were also hot, and by the end of this stretch, surface water may have passed thirty-two degrees Celsius. The reef builders, the creatures that made the biggest structures in the sea, were among the hardest hit.

Overall, the Late Devonian crisis took out about three-quarters of the world's species.

## Ozone Hole

Ozone Hole.

Thirteen million years later, at the very end of the Devonian, the next threat arrived from above, in a crisis called the Hangenberg event.

[A sun with an angry face. A thin, torn blue band in the sky labelled "Ozone".]

In the mountains of East Greenland and high in the Bolivian Andes, scientists found fossil plant spores from this exact moment. Many of them were malformed, with strange, twisted spines on their surface. The researchers linked that damage to a jump in ultraviolet radiation, which means the ozone layer had thinned.

Their own explanation was rapid warming at the end of an ice age, which could have weakened the ozone layer from the inside. Another team proposed a more dramatic suspect: one or more nearby supernovas, about sixty-five light-years away.

That's like a campfire in the next town burning off your eyebrows.

[Dotted distance line from Earth to a bursting star. Label: "65 light-years".]

Here's the strange part: it didn't matter where you lived. A study of more than twelve hundred kinds of early vertebrates found the losses were the same in the sea and in fresh water, so there was nowhere to hide. Major groups lost more than half their diversity.

Among the victims was the family of armored fish called placoderms. Their most famous member, Dunkleosteus, swam these seas around three hundred and sixty million years ago, with heavy bony plates over its head and sharp bony blades instead of teeth. After this event, the placoderms never really recovered.

## Siberian Traps

Siberian Traps.

About two hundred and fifty-two million years ago, life on Earth had its worst moment ever. The classic estimate says more than ninety-five percent of species were lost, while a careful recount in two thousand sixteen brought the marine figure down to about eighty-one percent. Either way, it is the worst on record.

[Map of Siberia. A red stain spreads across it.]

The culprit was in Siberia, where volcanic eruptions over a long stretch of time poured out enough magma to cover a region the size of the United States a full kilometer deep.

Here's the problem. The lava on the surface was not the main killer.

[Cross-section: lava on top. Below, flat orange sheets of magma slide sideways through dark rock layers labelled "Coal".]

At a certain point, the magma stopped erupting so much and started spreading sideways underground in flat sheets. Those sheets cooked the rock around them, and that rock was full of gas-rich sediment, including coal in some places. Field evidence shows the magma burned large amounts of coal and plant matter, and all that heat released huge volumes of greenhouse gas.

The killing itself was fast for a geologist, since dating of volcanic ash beds in China suggests the main extinction took about sixty thousand years, give or take. On a planet that is billions of years old, that's a blink. The volcanism itself went on for much longer, but the deadliest stretch seems to have been the first big pulse of those underground sheets.

## Hot Tub Ocean

Hot Tub Ocean.

After the Great Dying began, the planet kept getting hotter. Scientists measured that heat using the tiny fossil teeth of eel-like animals called conodonts, and they checked about fifteen thousand of them from rocks in South China.

[A microscope view of a tiny jagged tooth. Label: "Conodont".]

The oxygen inside each tooth records the temperature of the water it grew in, and the answer was brutal. Near the equator, the sea surface approached forty degrees Celsius, and the land may have reached fifty to sixty.

Forty degrees is the exact temperature that safety regulators in the United States say a hot tub should never go above. For a while, the whole tropical ocean was the top setting on a hot tub, with no button to turn it off.

[Thermometer climbing into the red. Label: "Forty degrees".]

Near the equator, fish almost disappeared, the algae that build hard chalky skeletons were lost, and the invertebrates that remained were mostly small ones. On land, the heat pushed most plants and animals out of the tropics entirely. A separate model of the Great Dying found the oceans warmed by around eleven degrees Celsius, and that warm water lost about three-quarters of its oxygen.

The worst part is how long it lasted. This dead zone did not fade in a few thousand years, because it held on for about five million years, which helps explain why the recovery from the Great Dying was so slow.

## Purple Oceans

Purple Oceans.

Heat drives oxygen out of water, and a hot ocean also mixes poorly. In parts of the Permian and early Triassic sea, the water lost its oxygen completely and filled with hydrogen sulfide.

[Ocean cross-section: blue on top, a rising black-purple layer below it.]

Hydrogen sulfide is the gas that smells like rotten eggs, and your nose can pick it up at about half a part per billion. That's like noticing a quarter teaspoon of something in an Olympic swimming pool.

Chemical traces in rocks from Western Australia and South China show it didn't stay in the deep. They contain the remains of green sulfur bacteria, which need both sunlight and hydrogen sulfide to live. The poisoned water had reached the sunlit surface.

But that's not the disturbing part. One hypothesis says huge amounts of the gas may have bubbled out of the sea and into the air, poisoning life on land and attacking the ozone layer from below. One paleontologist went further and pictured purple seas, colored by sulfur bacteria, under a pale green sky.

[Wide shot: a flat purple sea, a sickly green sky. Label: "Hypothesis".]

Not everything died, and one of the few land animals with a backbone to make it through was Lystrosaurus. It was a stocky plant-eater with a beak and two tusks, and its skull was roughly the length of a ruler. It ate tough plants, may have burrowed, and handled hard conditions well, and afterwards it became remarkably common across the south of the supercontinent.

## Pangaea Splits

Pangaea Splits.

About two hundred and one million years ago, every continent was still joined in one supercontinent called Pangaea. Then North America started pulling away from Africa, and the Atlantic Ocean began to form.

[Map: Pangaea with a glowing red crack running down the middle.]

Splitting a supercontinent lets magma rise from below, and the result was the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province. Its lava flows and buried sheets of magma cover an estimated ten million square kilometers, about the size of the United States. Pieces of it are found today in North America, South America, Africa and Europe.

That's one spilled drink reaching four tables.

The eruptions came in four pulses spread over about six hundred thousand years, and they pumped carbon dioxide into the air, warmed the planet and changed the oceans. The earliest volcanism lines up with the extinction, and about eighty percent of Earth's species disappeared.

As in Siberia, some magma stayed underground. That activity began about a hundred thousand years before the first known eruptions. In Brazil, sheets of magma pushed into rock rich in oil and organic matter, right as the extinction began. Gas cooked out of that rock may have added to the climate change.

[Four red pulses on a timeline. Red "X" marks over crocodile-like reptiles.]

Here's the twist: somebody won. Before this extinction, dinosaurs shared the land with rival reptiles, including crocodile-like hunters called phytosaurs. ## Acid Seas

Acid Seas.

When carbon dioxide dissolves into the ocean, it makes the water more acidic, and at the end of the Triassic the volcanoes of Pangaea added a huge amount of it very quickly.

[A seashell next to a red "pH" thermometer dropping.]

That matters a lot for animals that build shells and skeletons out of calcium carbonate. Acidic water makes it harder to build those shells, and in strong enough conditions, it eats them away. It's the same chemistry as a stick of chalk left in a glass of vinegar.

Across this extinction, carbonate rock built up much more slowly on seafloors all over the world. That's the kind of slowdown you'd expect if the sea was too acidic for it to form. And the sea creatures that built chalky shells were more likely to die out than the ones that didn't.

On the casualty list, corals, sponges, brachiopods and shelled relatives of the octopus all suffered losses. Every major group of sea invertebrates did survive in the end, but most of them came out smaller.

[Red "X" over a coral, a brachiopod and a coiled ammonite.]

The worst part is what happened to reefs. Before the extinction, reefs had spread across the warm tropical seas. Afterwards, they nearly disappeared, and reef ecosystems did not fully recover until the middle of the Jurassic period. The animals that built some of the most solid structures in the sea found themselves living in water that was working against them.

[Doug zipped into a coiled ammonite costume, red cap on top, floating in yellow-green water. Flat, unimpressed face.]

## Deccan Traps

Deccan Traps.

Sixty-six million years ago, India was having its own disaster before the famous one arrived, because the Deccan Traps were erupting.

[Map of India. Lava layers stacked like a cake.]

These eruptions spilled lava flows at least five hundred kilometers across the Indian continent, and in some places the stacked layers are nearly two kilometers thick. That's about five Empire State Buildings piled on top of each other, all of it hardened lava.

Two research teams dated these eruptions in twenty nineteen. One team found the lava came out in four big pulses, and the biggest of them came before the impact. The other found a steadier flow and concluded that about three-quarters of the lava erupted after the asteroid hit. Both teams agree the eruptions were happening right around the time of the impact, and one of them matched the two to within about fifty thousand years. Some researchers even suspect the impact shook the planet hard enough to boost the eruptions, because India sat almost directly opposite the crash site.

Here's the problem. Volcanoes this size release carbon dioxide and sulfur gases that can change the climate, so scientists still argue about how much of the end of the dinosaurs was down to India.

[Two scientist stick heads arguing. One points at a volcano, the other points at the sky.]

Some think the eruptions weakened the planet's ecosystems before the final blow, while others think their role was small. The two teams even disagree about whether the biggest pulses came before or after the impact.

## Chicxulub

Chicxulub.

It's a morning in the late Cretaceous, sixty-six million years ago, and the dinosaurs have ruled the land for about a hundred and thirty-five million years.

[A peaceful scene: ferns, a long-necked dinosaur, a small bright dot in the sky.]

Then an asteroid about ten kilometers wide arrives, which is wider than Mount Everest is tall. It hits the shallow sea off what is now the Yucatan Peninsula in Mexico and leaves a crater about one hundred and eighty kilometers across.

[Silhouette of the asteroid with a red glow, then the reveal: a cratered rock next to Mount Everest for scale.]

Every other extinction on this list played out over thousands of years or more, but this one started on a single day.

You'd think the blast itself did most of the killing. Here's the strange part: it didn't.

The impact hit a thick shelf of carbonate rock and threw sulfate particles, dust and soot high into the atmosphere, where the soot circled the entire globe within a few hours. Together, they cooled and darkened the planet in what scientists call an impact winter.

[The sky goes from blue to brown to black. A plant wilts. Then a plant-eater lies down. Then a meat-eater.]

Darkness is what finished the job, and one climate model suggests photosynthesis shut down for almost two years. Plants failed, the animals that ate plants starved, and the animals that ate them followed.

The evidence is all over the world. In nineteen eighty, a team led by a father and son, Luis and Walter Alvarez, found a thin clay layer in rocks from Italy, Denmark and New Zealand. It held up to a hundred and sixty times the normal amount of iridium, a metal that is rare on Earth's surface but common in asteroids. They proposed that dust from an asteroid impact had blocked the sun, and that the darkness had shut down photosynthesis.

In the end, about three-quarters of Earth's species were lost, including every dinosaur that wasn't a bird.
