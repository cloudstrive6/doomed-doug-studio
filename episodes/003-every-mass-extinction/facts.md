# Facts: 003 Every Mass Extinction

Every factual claim in script.md is listed here with its source. Doug, his deaths, the time machine, the costume, the
narrator's asides and the jokes ("doormat causing a blizzard", "campfire in the next town", "spilled drink reaching four
tables", the pigeon bench) are fiction or framing. Everyday-object sizes in the analogies (thumbnail, ruler 30 cm,
building floor about 3.3 m, a teaspoon about 5 ml) are rough common-knowledge comparisons, as in 001 and 002. Where a
claim is a hypothesis, the script frames it once ("some scientists think", "one proposed cause", "another team proposed", "one
hypothesis", "some researchers suspect").

Checked 2026-10-01. "Verified" = I read the quoted text on the page or in the abstract (Europe PMC API for paywalled
papers). Britannica and Nature article pages returned 403 to WebFetch; where they are cited it is for common reference
facts only.

## Opener
- "Five times, most species on Earth died out at once, by a planet's standards": NHM defines a mass extinction as about
  75% of species lost "in a short period of geological time"; all five figures above are 75% or more. "Five": the Big Five
  (NHM page; Da Silva et al. 2020 call the Frasnian-Famennian "one of the five main extinction intervals").

## End-Ordovician
### 1. Killer Moss
- 443 million years ago: NHM ("443 million years ago"); Sam Noble Museum ("began about 443 million years ago"),
  https://samnoblemuseum.ou.edu/understanding-extinction/mass-extinctions/end-ordovician-extinction/ (verified)
- "Animals had barely set foot on land", complex life in the sea: Sam Noble, "Animals had not yet conquered land at this time so the
  extinction was confined to life in the seas." "Barely" (draft 2, screener A2) allows for trace-fossil evidence of brief
  arthropod excursions onto land; the script makes no stronger claim.
- First land plants related to the ancestors of mosses, non-vascular: University of Exeter / ScienceDaily release on
  Lenton et al. 2012, https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/02/120201094923.htm (verified): "Among the first plants to
  grow on land were the ancestors of mosses that grow today." Paper: Lenton, Crouch, Johnson, Pires and Dolan 2012,
  "First plants cooled the Ordovician", Nature Geoscience 5: 86-89, https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo1390 (abstract:
  "non-vascular land plants"). "No wood, no flowers and no real roots": non-vascular plants (bryophytes) lack true roots
  and vascular tissue, https://www.britannica.com/plant/bryophyte
- "Some scientists think" (draft 2; was "one leading idea", screener A1): the paper's own framing is "may have drawn down
  enough atmospheric carbon dioxide" (hypothesis). Other explanations for the cooling exist, so it is not called the leading one.
- Moss on rocks in incubators for three months sped up chemical weathering: ScienceDaily, "placed a number of rocks, with
  or without moss growing on them, into incubators. Over three months they were able to measure the effects the moss had
  on the chemical weathering".
- Weathering removes CO2 and forms carbonate rock in the ocean; phosphorus and iron feed ocean productivity and organic
  carbon burial: ScienceDaily ("removed carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, forming new carbonate rocks in the ocean";
  "increased the quantities of both these nutrients going into the oceans, fuelling productivity there and causing
  organic carbon burial").
- Cooling of about 5 °C plus another 2-3 °C: ScienceDaily (about five degrees through weathering, "another two to three
  degrees Celsius" from the nutrient effect).
- Ice spread over the South Pole: Sheehan 2001 (below): "the large Gondwana landmass approached the south pole in the
  Late Ordovician"; NHM: "huge glaciers forming".

### 2. Gondwana Ice
- Sheehan, P. M. 2001, "The Late Ordovician mass extinction", Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences 29: 331-364,
  https://usuarios.geofisica.unam.mx/cecilia/CT-ST/Sheehan2001OrdovicianExtintion.pdf (full text read):
  - Gondwana approached the South Pole (quoted above).
  - "the ice cap may have covered much of Africa and eastern South America"; "The ice cap may have been on the order of
    30,000,000 km2 in size". Script: "may have reached around thirty million square kilometers".
  - "recent estimates suggest a decline during the Ordovician of less than ∼100 m". Script: "Sea level fell by up to
    roughly a hundred meters".
  - "large areas of the continents were covered by epicontinental seas"; "In the endemic epicontinental sea provinces,
    glacio-eustatic sea-level decline drained the seaways and eliminated habitat"; species tried to move to marginal
    settings "but these habitats were already occupied by an incumbent fauna". Script: shallow seas, many species lived
    nowhere else, homes vanished, the edges were already taken.
  - "The glaciation ended suddenly, and as sea level rose, the climate moderated, and oceanic circulation stagnated,
    another pulse of extinction occurred."
- Second pulse about a million years after the first: Sam Noble, "two distinct extinctions roughly a million years apart".
  Draft 2 states the gap once, in the kicker (screener A8).
- Gondwana included Africa, South America, Antarctica, Australia and India: https://www.britannica.com/place/Gondwana
- Scale: 30 million km² is about three times the United States (total area about 9.8 million km²,
  https://www.census.gov/geographies/reference-files/2010/geo/state-area.html). 100 m at about 3.3 m per floor is about
  thirty floors (common knowledge).

## Late Devonian
### 3. Dead Reefs
- Date note (screener A9): NHM dates the Devonian event at 374 Ma and the Permian at 250 Ma. The script uses 372 Ma
  (Da Silva 2020, below) and 252 Ma (Burgess 2014, below); both are sourced, and the difference is deliberate.
- About 372 million years ago, Kellwasser events, two pulses of oceanic anoxia with massive biodiversity losses, black
  shale horizons, durations about 90 and 110 thousand years, sedimentation about 0.5 cm per thousand years: Da Silva et
  al. 2020, "Anchoring the Late Devonian mass extinction in absolute time by integrating climatic controls and
  radio-isotopic dating", Scientific Reports, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7395115/ (full text read via Europe
  PMC): "two pulses of oceanic anoxia, named the Lower and Upper Kellwasser events, during which massive marine biodiversity
  losses occurred"; "deposition of the black argillaceous Lower and Upper Kellwasser horizons over ~ 90 and ~ 110 kyr";
  "sedimentation rates around 0.5 cm/kyr". The F-F boundary is the Kellwasser termination at about 372 Ma.
- "Water with no oxygen leaves behind a dark rock called black shale": Da Silva et al. (anoxia "expressed in the
  sedimentary record by the deposition of distinct black shale horizons").
- "One proposed cause is stronger weathering on land ... algal blooms ... used up the oxygen": Da Silva et al., "Increase
  continental weathering in particular would have resulted in eutrophication, planktonic algal blooming and subsequent
  widespread marine anoxia."
- Largest animal reefs in Earth history; collapse in the Late Devonian; temperatures exceeding 32 °C at the Kellwasser
  thermal maximum: Bridge, Baird, Pandolfi, McWilliam and Zapalski 2022, "Functional consequences of Palaeozoic reef
  collapse", Scientific Reports, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8792005/ (full text read via Europe PMC): "The largest reef systems in
  Earth's history occurred in the Devonian period, but collapsed during the Late Devonian Mass Extinction"; "the largest
  development of metazoan reefs in the Earth's history"; "temperatures by the end of Frasnian ... exceeded 32°C".
- Main builders, corals and stromatoporoid sponges, hit hard: Sam Noble Museum,
  https://samnoblemuseum.ou.edu/understanding-extinction/mass-extinctions/late-devonian-extinctions/ (verified): "Reef
  building sponges called stromatoporoids and corals suffered losses"; Bridge et al. 2022 on reef collapse.
- About three-quarters of species: NHM (above).
- Scale: three US quarters are about 5.25 mm thick (1.75 mm each, https://www.usmint.gov/learn/coins-and-medals/circulating-coins).
- Doug beat: "less oxygen than the air in his lungs" is a joke built on "anoxia" (no oxygen); exhaled air still holds
  about 16% oxygen (common physiology). Not a claim about real Devonian water values.

### 4. Ozone Hole
- Thirteen million years later, Hangenberg event at the Devonian-Carboniferous boundary, 359 Ma: Sallan and Coates 2010
  (below): Frasnian-Famennian "374 Ma" and Hangenberg "some 15 million years later ... (359 Ma)". Using Da Silva's 372 Ma
  for the Kellwasser end, 372 minus 359 is 13. 
- Malformed spores from East Greenland and the Bolivian Andes, linked to elevated UV-B and ozone loss: Marshall et al.
  2020, "UV-B radiation was the Devonian-Carboniferous boundary terrestrial extinction kill mechanism", Science Advances 6:
  eaba0768, https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aba0768 (abstract verified via Europe PMC); University of
  Cambridge news, https://www.esc.cam.ac.uk/about-us/news/ozone-depletion-blamed-for-the-end-devonian-mass-extinction
  (verified: "Many of the spores were malformed"; samples from "the polar mountains of East Greenland and high in the
  Bolivian Andes"); spines: Sci.News summary, https://www.sci.news/paleontology/end-devonian-mass-extinction-erosion-ozone-layer-08477.html
  ("bizarrely formed spines on their surface").
- Their own explanation, rapid warming at the end of an ice age: Marshall abstract, "coincident with a major climatic
  warming that ended the intense final glacial cycle of the latest Devonian ice age. A mechanism for ozone layer reduction
  during rapid warming is increased convective transport of ClO."
- Supernova hypothesis, about 65 light-years: Fields et al. 2020, "Supernova triggers for end-Devonian extinctions", PNAS
  117: 21008, https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2013774117 (abstract verified: "we propose that the end-Devonian
  extinctions were triggered by supernova explosions at ~20 pc"; 20 parsecs is about 65 light-years, University of Illinois
  release via https://phys.org/news/2020-08-stars-mass-extinction-earth.html). "One or more": the paper says "supernova
  explosions" (plural possible). Framed once as "another team proposed ... may have done it".
- Losses the same in sea and fresh water, over 1,250 taxa, over 50% diversity loss, placoderms' minimal recovery: Sallan
  and Coates 2010, "End-Devonian extinction and a bottleneck in the early evolution of modern jawed vertebrates", PNAS,
  https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0914000107 (abstract verified): "over 1,250 taxa"; "long-term losses of over 50%
  of diversity"; "Marine and nonmarine faunas were equally affected, precluding the existence of environmental refugia";
  "The subsequent recovery of previously diverse groups (including placoderms ...) was minimal." Script says "more than
  twelve hundred kinds" and "never really recovered".
- Dunkleosteus, about 360 million years ago, armored head plates, bony blades instead of teeth: Ohio Department of
  Natural Resources, https://ohiodnr.gov/discover-and-learn/safety-conservation/about-ODNR/geologic-survey/educational-resources/dunkleosteus-fossil
  (Ohio's state fossil fish; "about 360 million years ago"); Cleveland Museum of Natural History coverage ("razor-sharp
  bone blades instead of teeth"; "thick bony plates that protected the head and thorax"). The script does not claim
  Dunkleosteus died exactly in the Hangenberg event; it says it lived "around three hundred and sixty million years ago"
  and that its family, the placoderms, never really recovered.

## The Great Dying
### 5. Siberian Traps
- About 252 million years ago: USGS release (below); Burgess et al. 2014: "between 251.941 ± 0.037 and 251.880 ± 0.031 Mya".
- "Enough magma to cover a region the size of the United States a full kilometer deep": MIT News 2015, "Siberian Traps
  likely culprit for end-Permian extinction", https://news.mit.edu/2015/siberian-traps-end-permian-extinction-0916
  (verified: "enough to cover a region the size of the United States in kilometer-deep magma").
- Shift from surface lava to sideways underground sheets (sills), heating gas-rich sediments, releasing greenhouse gas,
  the initial sill pulse as the trigger: Burgess, Muirhead and Bowring 2017, "Initial pulse of Siberian Traps sills as the
  trigger of the end-Permian mass extinction", Nature Communications 8: 164,
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-017-00083-9 (abstract verified: "an abrupt change in the emplacement style ...
  from dominantly flood lavas to sill intrusions"; "Heat from these sills exposed untapped volatile-fertile sediments to
  contact metamorphism, likely liberating the massive greenhouse gas volumes needed to drive extinction"); USGS release,
  https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/subsurface-magma-triggers-earths-most-severe-extinction (verified).
  Note: the brief cited this as Science Advances; it is Nature Communications.
- Coal in some places, magma burned coal and plant matter: Elkins-Tanton et al. 2020, "Field evidence for coal
  combustion links the 252 Ma Siberian Traps with global carbon disruption", Geology 48: 986,
  https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/gsa/geology/article/48/10/986/587319 ; ScienceDaily release,
  https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/06/200616135818.htm ("burned large volumes of a combination of vegetation and
  coal"). Burgess 2017 also notes "locally coal-bearing Permian host rocks" in the Tunguska Basin.
- About sixty thousand years, give or take, from ash beds in China: Burgess, Bowring and Shen 2014, "High-precision
  timeline for Earth's most severe extinction", PNAS 111: 3316, https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1317692111 (abstract
  verified: "an interval of 60 ± 48 ka"; ash beds at Meishan, China).
- "The volcanism went on for much longer": MIT News 2015 (eruptions before, during and for about 500,000 years after);
  "first big pulse of those underground sheets": Burgess 2017 title and abstract.

### 6. Hot Tub Ocean
- Sun et al. 2012, "Lethally hot temperatures during the Early Triassic greenhouse", Science 338: 366,
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1224126 (abstract verified via Europe PMC); University of Leeds release via
  ScienceDaily, https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/10/121018141844.htm (verified):
  - Conodonts, about 15,000 from South China, oxygen isotopes record temperature (release).
  - Sea surface near 40 °C, land about 50-60 °C (release: "around 50-60°C on land, and 40°C at the sea-surface").
  - "the loss of calcareous algae, the near-absence of fish in equatorial Tethys, and the dominance of small taxa of
    invertebrates"; "High temperatures drove most Early Triassic plants and animals out of equatorial terrestrial
    ecosystems" (abstract).
  - Dead zone "lasted for a perplexingly long period: five million years" (release); "suppressed ecosystem recovery"
    (abstract).
  - "Eel-like animals": the release describes conodonts as "tiny teeth of extinct eel-like fishes".
- Hot tub limit 40 °C: US Consumer Product Safety Commission, "CPSC Warns Of Hot Tub Temperatures",
  https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/1980/CPSC-Warns-Of-Hot-Tub-Temperatures ("Hot tub water temperatures should
  never exceed 104 degrees Fahrenheit"; 104 °F = 40 °C). No advice is given to the viewer; it is a comparison only.
- Oceans warmed about 11 °C, oxygen down about 76% (model): Penn et al. 2018, Science, via Smithsonian,
  https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/how-did-great-dying-kill-96-percent-earths-ocean-dwelling-creatures-180970992/

  percent"). Script: "A separate model ... warmed by around eleven degrees Celsius ... lost about three-quarters of its oxygen."

### 7. Purple Oceans
- Warm water holds less oxygen: USGS Water Science School, https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/dissolved-oxygen-and-water
  (cold water holds more dissolved oxygen than warm water). A hot ocean mixes poorly: Penn State release on Kump 2005,
  https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/global-warming-led-climatic-hydrogen-sulfide-and-permian-extinction (warmer
  water slowed ocean circulation and reduced oxygen, letting sulfur bacteria produce hydrogen sulfide).
- Oxygen-free, sulfide-rich water reaching the sunlit zone; green sulfur bacteria biomarkers; Western Australia and South
  China: Grice et al. 2005, "Photic zone euxinia during the Permian-Triassic superanoxic event", Science 307: 706,
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1104323 (abstract verified: Perth Basin, Western Australia; "Biomarkers
  diagnostic for anoxygenic photosynthesis by Chlorobiaceae"; "Similar conditions prevailed in the contemporaneous seas off
  South China"; "widespread photic-zone euxinic conditions"). Green sulfur bacteria need both light and hydrogen sulfide
  (photic zone euxinia definition, same paper).
- Hypothesis that hydrogen sulfide escaped to the air, poisoned land life and damaged the ozone layer: Kump, Pavlov and
  Arthur 2005, "Massive release of hydrogen sulfide to the surface ocean and atmosphere during intervals of oceanic
  anoxia", Geology 33: 397, https://doi.org/10.1130/G21295.1 ; Penn State release (verified: "The hydrogen sulfide
  dispersing in the atmosphere would kill most terrestrial life"; "hydrogen sulfide gas destroys the ozone layer").
  Framed once as "One hypothesis says".
- Purple seas under a pale green sky, one paleontologist's picture: Peter Ward, "Under a Green Sky" excerpt, Orion
  Magazine, https://orionmagazine.org/article/under-a-green-sky/ ("a vast flat oily purple" sea "under a pale green
  sky"); Ward 2006, "Impact from the Deep", Scientific American, https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/impact-from-the-deep/
  (verified: "photosynthetic purple sulfur bacteria"; "great bubbles of toxic H2S gas erupting into the atmosphere"). On
  screen labelled "Hypothesis".
- Rotten-egg smell, odor threshold about 0.5 parts per billion: ATSDR ToxFAQs for hydrogen sulfide,
  https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Tsp/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=388&toxid=67 and Public Health Statement,
  https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Tsp/PHS/PHS.aspx?phsid=387&toxid=67 ("as low as 0.5 parts of hydrogen sulfide per billion parts of
  air"). Scale: an Olympic pool holds at least 2,500 cubic meters (50 m x 25 m x 2 m minimum, World Aquatics facilities
  rules); a quarter teaspoon is about 1.2 ml, so about 0.5 parts per billion (illustrative ratio).
- Lystrosaurus: one of the few tetrapods to survive, remarkably abundant in southern Pangaea, stout dicynodont with a
  beak and tusk-like caniniform processes, skull about 16-39 cm, tough-plant diet, possible burrowing, broad tolerances:
  Botha 2021 (search title "Living fast in the Triassic"), PLOS ONE, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8570511/
  (verified: "one of the few tetrapods to survive the Permo-Triassic mass extinction"; "remarkable abundance in southern
  Pangean deposits"; "ranged in maximum skull size from approximately 16–39 cm"; "generalist diet of tough plant
  material", "burrowing lifestyle", "broad habitat tolerances"). Script: "skull roughly the length of a ruler" (30 cm,
  inside the range). Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/animal/Lystrosaurus

## End-Triassic
### 8. Pangaea Splits
- About 201 million years ago; Pangaea breaking up; North America separating from Africa; Atlantic beginning to form;
  volcanism adding carbon dioxide, warming and ocean change; phytosaurs hit: Sam Noble Museum,
  https://samnoblemuseum.ou.edu/understanding-extinction/mass-extinctions/end-triassic-extinction/ (verified: "about 201
  million years ago"; "As North America separated from Africa and the Atlantic Ocean began to form, volcanic activity on a
  massive scale introduced carbon dioxide into the atmosphere"; phytosaurs, "crocodile-like land animals", among the
  groups particularly impacted).
- Central Atlantic Magmatic Province, about 10 million km², flows, sills and dykes, on four continents: Davies et al. 2017,
  "End-Triassic mass extinction started by intrusive CAMP activity", Nature Communications,
  https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms15596 (CAMP "predominantly emplaced into the Triassic-Jurassic basins of North and
  South America, Europe and Africa"); areal extent "about 10^7 km^2": https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10073112/
  (New U-Pb geochronology for CAMP). Scale: US total area about 9.8 million km² (Census, above).
- Four pulses over about 600,000 years; earliest volcanism synchronous with extinction; "setting the stage for dinosaurs
  to dominate Earth for the next 136 million years": Blackburn et al. 2013, "Zircon U-Pb geochronology links the
  end-Triassic extinction with the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province", Science 340: 941,
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1234204 (abstract verified).
- About 80% of species: NHM (above).
- "Some of the magma never reached the surface. Underground activity began about a hundred thousand years before the
  first known eruptions. In Brazil, sheets of magma pushed into rock rich in oil and ancient remains, right as the
  extinction began. Gas cooked out of that rock may have added to the climate change." (draft 3): Davies, Marzoli,
  Bertrand, Youbi, Ernesto and Schaltegger 2017, "End-Triassic mass extinction started by intrusive CAMP activity",
  Nature Communications 8: 15596, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5460029/ (full text read): "magmatic activity
  was occurring ∼100 Kyr ago before the earliest known eruptions"; "sills in an organic rich sedimentary basin in Brazil
  that intrude synchronously with the extinction suggesting that degassing of these organics contributed to the climate
  change which drove the extinction"; the Amazonas basin holds "some of the country's largest oil reserves" and "organic
  and volatile rich sediments". "Ancient remains" = organic matter (shales, oil source rock). Hedged as "may have".
- "The crack ... you know it now as the Atlantic Ocean": Sam Noble (Atlantic began to form as North America separated from Africa).

### 9. Acid Seas
- CO2 dissolving in seawater makes it more acidic and makes calcium carbonate shells harder to build: NOAA PMEL, "What is
  Ocean Acidification?", https://www.pmel.noaa.gov/co2/story/What+is+Ocean+Acidification%3F
- Chalk in vinegar: chalk is calcium carbonate and dissolves in acetic acid (common chemistry; same carbonate reaction).
- Draft 2 re-sourcing (screener Fix 2). Greene, Martindale, Ritterbush, Bottjer, Corsetti and Berelson 2012, "Recognising
  ocean acidification in deep time: An evaluation of the evidence for acidification across the Triassic-Jurassic
  boundary", Earth-Science Reviews 113: 72-93, DOI 10.1016/j.earscirev.2012.03.009,
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0012825212000463 . The abstract could not be read (ScienceDirect,
  ResearchGate and Academia return 403; Crossref, Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex carry no abstract). Only its opening line
  was seen, in a search snippet: "While demonstrating ocean acidification in the modern is relatively straightforward ...,
  identifying palaeo-ocean acidification is problematic." Greene 2012 is therefore NOT used as a source for any script
  line. The draft 1 quotes ("global paucity of carbonate", "particularly selective against acid-sensitive organisms",
  "temporarily eliminated coral reefs") are withdrawn, and the script lines were reworded to match the sources below.
- "Across this extinction, carbonate rock built up much more slowly on seafloors all over the world" and "the sea
  creatures that built chalky shells were more likely to die out than the ones that didn't": Trudgill et al. 2025, "Pulses
  of ocean acidification at the Triassic-Jurassic boundary", Nature Communications, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12260068/
  (full text read: "carbonate sedimentation rates decreased worldwide"; "marine calcifiers preferentially became extinct";
  "the reduction in global carbonate sedimentation rates, paucity of preserved calcareous fossils, and preferential
  extinction of marine calcifiers"). The same paper links these to acidification, which supports "the kind of slowdown
  you'd expect if the sea was too acidic".
- "Reefs had spread across the warm tropical seas. Afterwards, they nearly disappeared, and reef ecosystems did not fully
  recover until the middle of the Jurassic period": Hodges and Stanley 2015, "North American coral recovery after the
  end-Triassic mass extinction, New York Canyon, Nevada, USA", GSA Today 25(10),
  https://www.geosociety.org/gsatoday/archive/25/10/article/i1052-5173-25-10-4.htm (verified: "Reef ecosystems nearly
  collapsed with a reduction in deposition of CaCO3"; "The geographic proliferation of Late Triassic reefs through the
  Tethys is in stark contrast to their Early Jurassic reduction"; "a biocalcification crisis accounts for the scarcity of
  corals and reefs in the Early Jurassic"; "It was not until the Middle Jurassic that the ecosystem was fully recovered").
  Draft 1's "wiped coral reefs out entirely" was softened to "nearly disappeared": an early Hettangian coral reef is known
  from southern France (Kiessling et al., "An early Hettangian coral reef in southern France: Implications for the
  end-Triassic reef crisis", https://www.researchgate.net/publication/250083086 ).
- Supporting (not quoted in the script): Martindale, Berelson, Corsetti, Bottjer and West 2012, "Constraining carbonate
  chemistry at a potential ocean acidification event (the Triassic-Jurassic boundary) using the presence of corals and
  coral reefs in the fossil record", Palaeogeography, Palaeoclimatology, Palaeoecology, DOI 10.1016/j.palaeo.2012.06.020,
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031018212003756 (abstract seen via search snippet only, page 403:
  pCO2 increases "would have depressed saturation state to the point where coral biomineralization would have been
  extremely challenging", "resulting in the observed coral and coral reef gap in the fossil record"; a short, extreme
  acidification event "would satisfactorily explain the significant extinction of calcareous organisms, the Early
  Hettangian coral gap, and the T-J carbonate crisis"). This is the paper the draft 1 reef/selectivity wording most
  likely came from.
- Corals, sponges, brachiopods and shelled cephalopods suffered losses; all major marine invertebrate groups survived:
  Sam Noble (verified: "All major groups of marine invertebrates survived the extinction, although most suffered losses";
  brachiopods, shelled cephalopods, sponges, corals named). "Shelled relatives of the octopus" = shelled cephalopods.
- Costume: ammonites were shelled cephalopods (Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/animal/ammonoid). No claim is made
  about ammonite survival rates.

## End-Cretaceous
### 10. Deccan Traps
- Erupting around 66 million years ago, around the time of the impact; lava flows "for distances of at least 500
  kilometers across the Indian continent", "in some places nearly 2 kilometers thick"; "three-quarters of the lava

### 11. Chicxulub
- 66 million years ago; non-bird dinosaurs died; "dinosaurs survived as birds"; soot reduced sunlight and plant growth;
  food chain collapse: NHM, https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/how-an-asteroid-caused-extinction-of-dinosaurs.html (verified:
  "All of the non-bird dinosaurs died out, but dinosaurs survived as birds").
- Dinosaurs dominant for about 135 million years before the impact: Blackburn et al. 2013 (dominance "for the next 136
  million years" from 201 Ma) and 201 minus 66 = 135.
- Asteroid about 10 km wide: Alvarez et al. 1980 ("10 +/- 4 kilometers"); NOAA Science On a Sphere, "6+ miles wide",
  https://sos.noaa.gov/catalog/datasets/tsunami-asteroid-impact-66-million-years-ago/ (verified). Mount Everest 8,849 m
  (8,848.86 m, 2020 Nepal-China survey), https://www.britannica.com/place/Mount-Everest ; so 10 km is "wider than Mount
  Everest is tall".
- Crater about 180 km across, Yucatan Peninsula, Mexico: NOAA Science On a Sphere (verified: "estimated to
  be 180 kilometers (110 miles) in diameter and 20 kilometers (12 miles) deep"). NHM gives 150 km; 180 km is the more
  common estimate and is what NOAA states. Draft 2 drops the 20 km depth (original crater, now buried; screener A3).
- "Started on a single day" versus thousands of years or more for the others: the impact is a single event; the others
  ran over about 60,000 years (Permian, Burgess 2014), about 600,000 years of CAMP pulses (Blackburn 2013), tens of
  thousands of years per Kellwasser black shale (Da Silva 2020), and about a million years between Ordovician pulses (Sam Noble).
- Hit a carbonate platform; sulfate aerosols, dust and soot; soot "circulated the globe within a few hours"; impact winter
  and global darkening that "curtailed photosynthesis"; 76% of species: Lyons et al. 2020, "Organic matter from the
  Chicxulub crater exacerbated the K-Pg impact winter", PNAS 117: 25327, https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2004596117
  (abstract verified via Europe PMC). The brief attributed this to Chiarenza et al. 2020; the PNAS paper cited is Lyons et al.
- Photosynthesis shut down for almost two years (one model): Senel et al. 2023, "Chicxulub impact winter sustained by
  fine silicate dust", Nature Geoscience, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-023-01290-4 ("a dust-induced
  photosynthetic shut-down for almost 2 years post-impact"). Framed as "one climate model suggests".
- Alvarez team 1980, iridium in Italy, Denmark and New Zealand at about 30, 160 and 20 times background, extraterrestrial
  origin, dust causing darkness that suppressed photosynthesis: Alvarez, Alvarez, Asaro and Michel 1980, "Extraterrestrial
  cause for the Cretaceous-Tertiary extinction", Science 208: 1095, https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.208.4448.1095
  (abstract verified). Father and son: Luis (physicist) and Walter (geologist) Alvarez, https://www.britannica.com/biography/Walter-Alvarez
  ; thin clay layer: UC Berkeley Understanding Science, https://undsci.berkeley.edu/lessons/pdfs/alvarez_wflow.pdf
  ("a thin 1/2-inch layer of clay"). Iridium rare at Earth's surface, more common in asteroids: Alvarez abstract
  ("Platinum metals are depleted in the earth's crust relative to their cosmic abundance").
- Pigeons are birds, and birds are the surviving dinosaurs: NHM (above). The bench is a joke.
