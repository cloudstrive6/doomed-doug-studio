# 050: Every Reason the Octopus Is the Most Alien Animal in the Ocean (draft 2)

[Thumbnail held 1.5 s: one big red octopus fills the frame, one arm curled into a fist; tiny Doug floats in front of it in a goatfish costume, cap on top. Then cut. DOUG DEATHS: 374, DEPTH: ABOUT 5 M and ARMS: 8 beside it. Staging only, never read aloud.]

In the Red Sea, scientists filmed octopuses punching fish.

By the end of this, Doug is almost seven kilometers down, face to face with the deepest octopus anyone has ever filmed.

The reef.

## The Punch

The Punch.

[A day octopus on a sandy reef, a loose ring of goatfish and groupers around it. Label: "HUNTING PARTY".]

The puncher is the day octopus, and it does not hunt alone, because on these reefs it teams up with goatfish, groupers and other fish that cross the seabed with it.

The fish cover the open water while the octopus pours itself into cracks where no fish can follow, and whatever bolts out, somebody catches. When a fish crowds in too close, the octopus fires out one arm and shoves it away.

[One arm snaps out. A grouper spins to the edge of the group. Red "!" stamp.]

Here's the strange part. A later study filmed these groups in Israel, Egypt and Australia. The goatfish did most of the scouting and pointed the way, but the octopus decided when the whole group actually moved. Its punches landed far more often on blacktip groupers, the fish that tag along and take without adding much.

So the hunting party has a scout, a freeloader, and a bouncer.

[Diagram: octopus outline, the arms shaded red. Label: "TWO THIRDS".]

Octopuses like the bouncer have more than five hundred million neurons, about as many as a dog, and over two thirds of them sit in their arms and body instead of their brain.

[Doug in a goatfish costume, cap on top, swims into the ring of fish.]

Doug joined the hunt dressed as a goatfish, because the goatfish are the ones the octopus keeps around. Doug did not agree to this. He was told goatfish only do the scouting.

[A crab bolts from a crack. Doug grabs it first. One arm flicks out. Doug tumbles to the edge of the group, where a grouper opens its mouth. Cut. The goatfish costume lies flat on the sand, X eyes, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 375.]

He grabbed the first crab himself, and one punch moved him out to the edge of the group, where the grouper was waiting.

Doug has died. Again. The next octopus doesn't hit anything, because it just pretends to be something worse.

## The Mimic

The Mimic.

[DEPTH: ABOUT 10 M. Open sand, a map inset with Indonesia highlighted.]

The mimic octopus does impressions, and its audience is anything that might want to eat it.

It lives on open sand off Indonesia, where there is nowhere to hide, and it feeds in broad daylight. So instead of hiding, it pretends to be something nobody wants to bite.

Its body only grows to about six centimeters long, roughly the length of your thumb, but its thin arms stretch the whole animal out to around half a meter.

[Split panel: the octopus as a flat striped oval beside a banded sole; then arms flared and striped beside a lionfish.]

It glides along with its arms pulled into a flat, striped oval, like a poisonous flatfish, or hangs above the sand with striped arms flared out, like a lionfish showing off its spines. That is a lionfish impression from an animal with no spines at all, which is like a raincoat doing an umbrella.

But that's not the clever part. When damselfish pestered one, it pushed six arms down a burrow and waved the other two, banded, like a sea snake, and sea snakes eat damselfish. The scientists who first described it concluded that it seems to choose its impression to suit the threat.

[Octopus eye close-up. Label: "ONE PIGMENT".]

It may be doing all of this without seeing colour the way we do, because octopus eyes have a single visual pigment, which is why they are usually described as colour-blind.

[Doug in a damselfish costume, cap on top, chasing the octopus across the sand.]

Doug went after it dressed as a damselfish.

[Two banded arms rise out of a burrow and sway. Doug turns and flees off-screen at full speed. The counter stays at 375.]

Two banded arms rose out of the sand, and Doug was gone before the impression had even finished.

Doug is fine. He lost to an impression, and the next octopus doesn't bother pretending, because it throws things.

## The Thrower

The Thrower.

[DEPTH: ABOUT 15 M. A wide seabed of broken shells with a few dens. Map inset: Jervis Bay, southeast Australia.]

Off southeast Australia, wild gloomy octopuses have been filmed throwing things at each other, and sometimes they hit.

A gloomy octopus can spread its arms up to about two meters, roughly the length of a bed.

In Jervis Bay, these usually solitary animals gather in small groups on beds of old shells built up from their own meals. Researchers watched them scoop up silt, shells or weed in their arms, fold the siphon, their water jet, into an unusual position underneath, and blast the whole load away.

[An octopus launches a puff of silt and shells across the frame. A dotted arc. Label: "102 THROWS".]

In about twenty-four hours of footage from a group of roughly ten octopuses, the team counted one hundred and two throws, and about one throw in six hit another octopus.

The uncomfortable part is this. Two thirds of the throws came from females, and darker, more worked-up octopuses threw harder and hit more often. One female kept hitting a male who would not leave her alone, and octopuses that got hit often ducked or raised their arms toward the thrower.

[Doug sits down cross-legged in the middle of the shell bed. Bubble: "hi friends?".]

Doug sat down in the middle of the shell beds and said hello.

[A shell bounces off his cap. Then a puff of silt. Then another shell. Doug stays sitting.]

A shell bounced off his cap, then some silt, then another shell, and that was all.

Doug is fine. The narrator had hoped for better aim.

[An arm lifts the cap off and places it on top of the shell pile.]

The cap has been added to the pile, and the next octopus doesn't throw its weapons, because it steals them.

Open water.

## The Blanket

The Blanket.

[DEPTH: ABOUT 20 M. Blue open water. A female blanket octopus, her wide web spread like a cape, holding clean blue man-of-war tentacle strands in two arms.]

The female blanket octopus goes armed, pulling the stinging tentacles off a Portuguese man-of-war and waving them at anything that comes too close.

The stings that make the man-of-war so dangerous do not seem to bother it at all, and it is described as immune to them.

Its name comes from the sheets of webbing between some of its arms, which it spreads into a huge, rippling cape when it feels threatened.

Here's the twist. The females are enormous, growing to about two meters, the height of a doorway. The first live male ever studied, found by a diver on the Great Barrier Reef, was two point four centimeters long, about the size of a walnut. It weighed no more than a quarter of a gram.

[Scale panel: a doorway beside a walnut. Label: "SAME SPECIES".]

One eighty centimeter female was more than ten thousand times heavier than that male, and the biggest females could be almost forty thousand times heavier.

[Doug in a Portuguese man-of-war costume, cap on top, bobbing at the surface.]

Doug went as a Portuguese man-of-war, since it is the one animal this octopus actually wants something from, and Doug likes to be wanted.

[An arm takes one of the costume's tentacles. Then another. The octopus waves them back at Doug. Cartoon zap. X eyes. The costume deflates, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 376.]

She took his tentacles first, and then she showed him exactly what they were for.

The next octopus doesn't need a weapon. You can barely see it.

## The Glass Octopus

The Glass Octopus.

[DEPTH: ABOUT 500 M. Dark blue. A faint outline of an octopus; only two long eyes, a thin nerve and a small gut are drawn solid.]

You can see straight through the glass octopus, and the only parts of it that show are its eyes, its optic nerves and its gut.

It drifts through the twilight zone, roughly two hundred to one thousand meters down. Living ones are so rarely filmed that scientists long had to study it from pieces found in the stomachs of its predators.

Spotting one is like finding a clean glass in a dishwasher full of water.

In twenty twenty-one, a robot sub on a Schmidt Ocean Institute expedition near the Phoenix Islands, in the middle of the Pacific, filmed one floating alone in the dark.

[The two eyes highlighted. Label: "FROM BELOW". A dotted line shows a predator's view looking up.]

The strangest part is the eyes, which are long and cylindrical rather than round. One idea is that the shape shrinks the eyes' silhouette when a hunter looks up from below, so even the parts you can see are hard to see.

[Doug in a small sub, face pressed to the porthole. The octopus is right in front of the glass.]

Doug went down in the sub to look for one, and the narrator told him it was right in front of the porthole.

[Doug squints. Doug looks left. Doug looks right. Bubble: "where?".]

It was. Doug looked left, then right, then left again, and never saw it once.

Doug is fine. The next octopus can weigh about as much as he does, and it is much easier to see.

The deep.

## The Seven Arms

The Seven Arms.

[DEPTH: ABOUT 1,000 M. A huge, soft, gelatinous red octopus in midwater. ARMS: 8 in the corner.]

The seven-arm octopus has eight arms.

[The narrator's hand crosses out the 8 and writes "7?". Beat. It crosses out the "7?" and writes 8 again.]

In the males, one arm stays coiled up in a sac under the right eye, where it is easy to miss, so somebody counted seven.

The females are the giants, reaching about four meters long and up to seventy-five kilograms, about as heavy as a grown man. Doug is about eighty.

[Scale panel: the octopus beside Doug, nearly level. Label: "75 KG".]

For something that big, it is very hard to find. In nearly forty years of deep-sea dives, the robots at one California research institute have run into it just four times.

This one lives on jellies.

In Monterey Canyon, a research robot filmed a female holding a large egg-yolk jelly in her arms and biting through the bell to get at the inside. When scientists checked five preserved specimens in a museum, every one had jelly-like animals in its stomach. Afterwards, it may even drag the jelly's stinging tentacles along behind it, possibly to protect itself.

That's seventy-five kilograms of octopus, living mostly on something that is mostly water.

[Doug in a jellyfish costume, cap on top, drifting hopefully in midwater.]

Doug went as a jelly, to get close.

[The octopus wraps its arms around Doug and holds him in front of it like a fruit bowl. Cut. The jelly costume flat, X eyes, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 377.]

It held him in its arms like a fruit bowl, and then it started at the top.

Doug has died. Again. The next octopus barely eats at all, for four and a half years.

## The Brooder

The Brooder.

[DEPTH: 1,397 M. A dark canyon wall. A pale octopus wrapped over a cluster of eggs on a rock ledge.]

At one thousand three hundred and ninety-seven meters down in Monterey Canyon, a deep-sea octopus guarded her eggs for fifty-three months.

That is four years and five months, longer than the gap between two Olympic Games, and it is the longest egg-brooding period known for any animal.

[A calendar card: MAY 2007. A robot sub's light sweeps the ledge.]

Researchers found her in May two thousand seven with a robot sub, and came back eighteen times over the next four and a half years. She was always in the same place, wrapped over the same eggs, and when crabs and shrimp wandered past, she pushed them away. When the team offered her pieces of crab, she refused, and she was never seen eating.

The slow part is the cold. Down there, the chill slows everything down, including the eggs, so when they finally hatched, the young were among the largest and most developed octopus hatchlings known.

[Doug sits beside the eggs on a folding stool. Bubble: "back soon?".]

Doug offered to watch the eggs while she popped out for a bit. She did not pop out.

[Calendar pages tear off one after another. Doug grows a long cartoon beard. Doug lies flat beside the stool, X eyes, cap on his chest. She has not moved. DOUG DEATHS: 378.]

Doug ran out first.

[Sober card: "53 MONTHS". No Doug, no joke. Hold.]

For most octopuses, a mother stops eating once her eggs are laid, and by the time they hatch, she is gone. A gland called the optic gland drives that decline.

When the researchers came back after the eggs had hatched, she was gone. Only the empty egg cases were left.

The next mothers found a way to cut the wait.

The bottom.

## The Garden

The Garden.

[DEPTH: ABOUT 3,200 M. Near-black water. A slope packed with small pale purple octopuses, each curled over its eggs, like pearls scattered on the rock. A faint shimmer over the cracks.]

About three thousand two hundred meters down, off central California, thousands of octopuses are brooding their eggs side by side.

They are pearl octopuses, pale purple and about the size of a grapefruit, and they gather on a small hill beside an extinct underwater volcano. In one patch of about six acres, roughly three and a half football pitches, researchers counted around six thousand of them, and the whole site may hold twenty thousand or more. It is the largest gathering of octopuses ever found.

[Label: "20,000+". Then a small inset of the 037 depth gauge, stopped at 2,000 M, with a red arrow pointing far below it.]

That is already deeper than the sperm whale ever took Doug.

The strange part is why they come. It's the heat. The water down here is about one point six degrees Celsius, but in the cracks around these springs it reaches nearly eleven. At near-freezing temperatures, scientists expected the eggs to take five to eight years, but here they develop in about one point eight years. The mothers do not eat while they brood, so a shorter wait gives them a better chance of lasting until the eggs hatch.

They are not here for each other. They came for the warm patch.

[Doug in a cartoon diving suit, cap on top of the helmet, tiptoes between the nests. Bubble: "excuse me".]

Doug wandered in among them, saying excuse me, looking for the one warm crack nobody had taken yet.

[Doug finds an empty crack and sits down on it, content. Cut. The diving suit flat on the rock, X eyes, cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 379.]

He found it, and it was warm, but it was also three thousand two hundred meters down, and the suit was not built for that.

The last stop is more than twice as deep.

## The Deepest

The Deepest.

[DEPTH: 6,957 M, stamped red. Black. A sandy trench floor lit by a single camera frame on legs. Map inset: the Java Trench, Indian Ocean.]

At six thousand nine hundred and fifty-seven meters, in the Java Trench in the Indian Ocean, a camera filmed the deepest octopus anyone has ever seen.

It was a dumbo octopus, a deep-sea octopus that rows itself along with two fins above its eyes. A camera frame dropped to the seafloor during the Five Deeps Expedition filmed it in twenty nineteen, and another one turned up at five thousand seven hundred and sixty meters.

[Two dots on a depth bar: 5,145 M, then 6,957 M. Label: "NEARLY 50 YEARS".]

The old record, five thousand one hundred and forty-five meters, had stood for nearly fifty years.

Pressure rises by about one atmosphere for every ten meters, so down here it is close to seven hundred times what you feel at the surface. That is more than seven hundred kilograms pressing on every square centimeter, a patch about the size of your thumbnail.

[A thumbnail outline with a big downward arrow. Label: "700 KG".]

Dumbo octopuses have no ink sac, and they swallow their prey whole.

The unsettling part is that nobody knows exactly what this one is. It may be a species that has never been named, and to prove it, someone would have to bring one up.

[Doug's sub settles on the trench floor. A pale dumbo octopus drifts up to the porthole and looks in. Doug looks out. Bubble: "hi?".]

Doug's sub reached the same depth, and the dumbo octopus drifted up to the porthole to look at him.

[The sub crumples inward like a can, cartoon, no Doug visible. DOUG DEATHS: 380.]

The octopus did not react. It lives here.

[Closing: the surface ship's monitor, red "LIVE" tag. The red cap drifts down past the dumbo octopus and settles on the trench floor.]

The cap has now been six thousand nine hundred and fifty-seven meters down. The octopus got there first, and it didn't need a sub.

Doug deaths: three hundred and eighty. Subscribing will not slow the counter down, but it does get you a better seat.
