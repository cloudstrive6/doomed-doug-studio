## The Punch (about 5 m, staging)

- Fish search open water, the octopus flushes prey from crevices fish can't reach, both benefit: CBC / NBC coverage of Sampaio et al. 2024.
  https://www.nbcnews.com/science/science-news/octopuses-hunt-with-fish-punch-video-rcna171705 (Search)

  https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1058886 (Fetched: "more than 100h of film material from dives in Israel, Egypt, and Australia")

  it says the fish pick the way and the octopus decides when the group moves, which is what the release says.
  https://earthsky.org/earth/octopuses-punch-fish-group-hunts/ (Fetched: "the octopus is in charge of the timing and initiation of group movement")

  fish without providing their own suggestions of where to go" (NBC, Search).
- **[d2]** "Octopuses like the bouncer have more than five hundred million neurons, about as many as a dog, and over two thirds of them sit in their arms and body" (F4: the figure is for octopuses in general / *O. vulgaris*, not *O. cyanea*, so it is now framed generically):

## The Mimic (about 10 m, staging)
- Mimic octopus (*Thaumoctopus mimicus*), Indonesia, forages on open sand in daylight in full view of predators: Norman, Finn & Tregenza 2001,
  *Proc. R. Soc. B* 268:1755-1758, "Dynamic mimicry in an Indo-Malayan octopus" (doi 10.1098/rspb.2001.1708). Abstract: "emerges during daylight
  hours to forage on sand substrates in full view of pelagic fish predators" (quoted in search result); dives around Sulawesi and Bali.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1088805 (Fetched)
- Flatfish impression (flat striped oval, models banded sole *Zebrias*), lionfish impression (striped arms flared, model *Pterois*), sea snake
  (*Laticauda*): paper figure captions (Search) and Science News: "arms aligned in a flattened, striped oval, looking much like a common poisonous
  flatfish"; "the sunburst of striped, poisonous spines that lionfish flare."
  https://sciencenews.org/?p=33513 (Fetched)
- "no spines at all": octopuses have no spines; the only hard part of an octopus is its beak (008 facts, Alaska Department of Fish and Game).
  Lionfish model has spines (Science News above).
- Damselfish / sea snake: Science News (Fetched): on four occasions when damselfish pestered an octopus, Norman "saw it poke six of its legs down a
  burrow and spread the other two. They sported bands and waved gently, resembling the sea snakes that prey on damselfish."
- "seems to choose its impression to suit the threat": paper (PMC, Fetched): "the octopus makes decisions about the most appropriate form of mimicry
  to use", used "according to the nature of perceived threats". **Hedge:** the script says the scientists "concluded that it seems to choose";
  the paper also notes convergence can't be fully excluded, so "seems" stays.
- "octopus eyes have a single visual pigment, which is why they are usually described as colour-blind": ScienceDaily 2016 on Stubbs & Stubbs,
  *PNAS* (doi 10.1073/pnas.1524578113): "despite having a single visual pigment in their retina"; paper title "Spectral discrimination in color blind
  animals". https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/07/160704223413.htm (Fetched). The script hedges ("may", "usually described as"), because the
  Stubbs paper itself argues they may detect colour another way. Ramirez & Oakley 2015 (skin opsins) is NOT used in the script.
- Raincoat/umbrella: joke analogy, no factual claim.
- **[d2] Scale (rule 8):** "Its body only grows to about six centimeters long, roughly the length of your thumb, but its thin arms stretch the whole animal out to around half a meter."
  Norman & Hochberg 2005, *Molluscan Research* 25(2), original species description: mantle length to 58 mm, total length over 480 mm, arms 7-10 times
  mantle length (via species summary, Search). https://octonation.com/?p=6319 (Fetched: mantle "up to 60 mm"; size "48 cm"; arm span "about 600 mm")
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimic_octopus (Fetched: "total length of about 60 cm (2 ft), including arms"). Sources range 48-60 cm, so the script
  says "around half a meter". Thumb about 6 cm long: common knowledge, approximate. Institutional source: SeaLifeBase https://www.sealifebase.ca/summary/100159
  (screener-confirmed, mantle to 58 mm, total length over 480 mm). Both are maxima, hence "grows to about" (CD polish, 2026-11-17).
- **[d2]** "just to keep things consistent" cut (rule 10).

  https://www.coralmagazine.com/2022/11/10/the-well-armed-octopus-shell-throwing-behaviors-documented-in-cephalopods

## The Brooder (1,397 m)
- 1,397 m, Monterey Canyon, *Graneledone boreopacifica*, female guarded eggs for 53 months, the longest known egg-brooding period of any animal:
  Robison, Seibel & Drazen 2014, *PLOS ONE* 9(7): e103437, "Deep-Sea Octopus (Graneledone boreopacifica) Conducts the Longest-Known Egg-Brooding
  Period of Any Animal". https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4116195/ (blocked by browser check; Search)
  Mongabay (Fetched): "At 1,397 meters (4,583 feet) deep"; "At 53 months, it is by far the longest egg-brooding period ever reported for any animal species".
  https://news.mongabay.com/2014/07/the-worlds-best-mother-meet-the-octopus-that-guards-its-eggs-for-over-four-years/
  Guinness: https://guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/405858-longest-egg-brooding-period (Search)
- "four years and five months": 53 months = 4 years 5 months (Arithmetic). "longer than the gap between two Olympic Games": Summer Games every four
  years (common knowledge).
- Found May 2007 by ROV; 18 return visits over about four and a half years; brooding ran May 2007 to September 2011: Mongabay (Fetched: "we returned
  to the site 18 times over the ensuing four-and-a-half years"); PMC search summary (May 2007 to September 2011).
- Pushed crabs and shrimp away; refused offered crab; never seen eating: Mongabay (Fetched: "the only interactions we observed were when she pushed
  these crustaceans away"; "refused food when the ROV offered her pieces of crab"). **Script says "never seen eating", not "never ate"** (brief).
- Cold slows development: Mongabay (Fetched: "the chilly temperatures slow down metabolic rates"; Robison: "the brooding period is temperature dependent").
- Hatchlings "among the largest and most developed octopus hatchlings known": Mongabay (Fetched, citing a 2004 paper: "largest and most
  developmentally advanced octopus hatchlings known").
- "For most octopuses, a mother stops eating once her eggs are laid, and by the time they hatch, she is gone. A gland called the optic gland drives
  that decline.": University of Washington, 12 May 2022, on Wang, Pergande, Ragsdale & Cologna 2022, *Current Biology* (doi 10.1016/j.cub.2022.04.043)
  (Fetched): "After a mother octopus lays a clutch of eggs, she quits eating and wastes away; by the time the eggs hatch, she is dead"; "The source of
  this bizarre maternal behavior seems to be the optic gland"; removing it (Wodinsky 1977) led mothers to resume feeding and live longer.
  https://washington.edu/news/2022/05/12/octopus-cholesterol
  "most octopuses": the release notes some species (e.g. *Octopus chierchiae*) don't self-destruct, hence "most", not "all".
- **[d2]** "When the researchers came back after the eggs had hatched, she was gone. Only the empty egg cases were left." (F1): Mongabay / Robison et
  al. 2014: on the visit after hatching (about September 2011) the mother was gone and the empty egg capsules remained; the team counted about
  155-165 casings (Search; confirmed by screener). Stated flatly; never drawn.
- **[d2]** "The slow part is the cold." replaces OctoLab's "here's why" framing (brief ban). Same sourced mechanism (Mongabay, cold slows development).
- **[d2]** Lean-in "The next mothers found a way to cut the wait.": the pearl octopus mothers at the Octopus Garden brood in about 1.8 years instead
  of the expected 5-8 (see The Garden, EurekAlert / Smithsonian, Fetched).

## The Garden (about 3,200 m)
- Octopus Garden at Davidson Seamount, off central California, about 3,200 m: Barry et al. 2023, *Science Advances* (PMC10446498).
  MBARI release via EurekAlert (Fetched): "located 3,200 meters (10,500 feet, or about two miles) below the ocean's surface".
  https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/998944

  species about the size of a grapefruit"; "MBARI researchers nicknamed the pearl octopus").
- "a small hill beside an extinct underwater volcano": EurekAlert / Search (hillock beside Davidson Seamount); Smithsonian (Fetched: "an extinct
  volcano two miles below the Pacific Ocean's surface").
- About 6,000 counted in a 6.2-acre area; at least 20,000 at the site; largest gathering of octopuses ever found: Smithsonian Magazine (Fetched:
  "roughly 6,000 of the octopuses in just a small, 6.2-acre area"; "could contain at least 20,000 pearl octopuses"; "the largest congregation of the
  cephalopods ever found"); EurekAlert (Fetched: "counted more than 6,000"; "20,000 or more").
  https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/why-hot-springs-draw-the-worlds-largest-gathering-of-deep-sea-octopuses-180982803/
- "about six acres, roughly three and a half football pitches": 6.2 acres = 25,090 m²; a 105 m × 68 m pitch (FIFA standard) = 7,140 m²;
  25,090 / 7,140 = 3.5 (Arithmetic). FIFA pitch dimensions: https://www.fifa.com/technical/football-technology/standards/football-pitch (not fetched).
- **[d2]** Twist marker "The strange part is why they come. It's the heat." (rule 4); facts unchanged.
- Ambient 1.6 °C; nearly 11 °C in the cracks: EurekAlert (Fetched: "1.6 degrees Celsius"; "reaches nearly 11 degrees Celsius").
- Expected 5 to 8 years at near-freezing temperatures: EurekAlert (Fetched: "researchers expected pearl octopus eggs to take five to eight years").
- About 1.8 years here: Smithsonian (Fetched: embryos "develop in a mere 1.8 years"); EurekAlert: "hatched in less than two years".
- Mothers do not eat while brooding; shorter brooding improves the odds they last: Smithsonian (Fetched: brooding females "do not eat", so a shorter
  brooding period lowers the risk they run out of energy).
- "That is already deeper than the sperm whale ever took Doug": 037's DEPTH gauge ended at "MORE THAN 2,000 M" (series bible, 037 log). Callback only.

## The Deepest (6,957 m)
- Dumbo octopus (*Grimpoteuthis* sp.) filmed at 6,957 m in the Java Trench, Indian Ocean; a second at 5,760 m; by a lander (camera frame on the
  seafloor); Five Deeps Expedition; deepest cephalopod ever recorded: Jamieson & Vecchione 2020, *Marine Biology* 167:82, "First in situ observation
  of Cephalopoda at hadal depths". phys.org, 4 June 2020 (Fetched): "One location was 5,760 meters deep, the other 6,957 meters"; "in the bottom of
  the Java Trench in the Indian Ocean"; "a type of frame with deep-sea cameras affixed to it"; "part of the Five Deeps Expedition".
  https://phys.org/news/2020-06-footage-captured-cephalopod-deepest-ocean.html
- Filmed in 2019: Five Deeps sightings were made in April 2019 (Search: Hakai Magazine / Guinness summaries).
  https://hakaimagazine.com/news/how-low-can-an-octopus-go/
- "The old record, five thousand one hundred and forty-five meters, had stood for nearly fifty years": phys.org (Fetched: "The prior record was
  5,145 meters and it had stood for nearly fifty years").
- "a deep-sea octopus that rows itself along with two fins above its eyes": BOEM (Fetched: "a pair of ear-like fins that protrude just above its
  eyes", flapped to propel it).
  https://www.boem.gov/newsroom/ocean-science-news/dumbo-octopus-whimsical-survivor-deep
- "no ink sac, and they swallow their prey whole": BOEM (Fetched: "lacks an ink sac that other octopus use as a defense mechanism"; "pounce on prey
  and swallow them whole"); UT Marine Science Institute (Search: "swallows it whole instead of ripping it apart").
  Collins & Villanueva 2006 not retrieved.
- "Pressure rises by about one atmosphere for every ten meters": NOAA Ocean Service (Search: "for every 33 feet (10.06 meters) you go down, the
  pressure increases by one atmosphere"). https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/pressure.html
- "close to seven hundred times what you feel at the surface": 6,957 / 10.06 ≈ 692 atm of water + 1 atm air ≈ 693 atm (Arithmetic).
- "more than seven hundred kilograms pressing on every square centimeter": 1 atm ≈ 1.033 kgf/cm²; 693 × 1.033 ≈ 716 kgf/cm² (Arithmetic).
  "about the size of your thumbnail": an adult thumbnail is roughly 1 cm² (common knowledge, approximate).
- "nobody knows exactly what this one is. It may be a species that has never been named, and to prove it, someone would have to bring one up":
  coverage of Jamieson & Vecchione 2020: likely a new species, unconfirmed without physical specimens (Search: phys.org/Hakai summaries).
- No Disney/elephant reference anywhere (brief); "dumbo octopus" is used only as the common name.
