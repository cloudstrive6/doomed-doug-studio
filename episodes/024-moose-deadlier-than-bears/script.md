## The Deer

The Deer.

Picture an ordinary deer, then keep enlarging it, and only stop once it is taller than your front door.

[A moose side-on next to a plain interior door. The shoulder lines up with the top of the door frame. Label: "UP TO ABOUT 2 M AT THE SHOULDER".]

The moose is the largest member of the deer family anywhere on Earth. According to the National Park Service, an adult can stand about two meters tall at the shoulder, roughly the height of a standard interior door.

[Doug stands beside it. His cap reaches the moose's chest.]

It has long legs, a drooping nose and a hanging flap of skin under its chin called the bell. Being a vegetarian that survives the winter mostly on twigs, it has no claws, no fangs, and no interest whatsoever in eating you.

## The Dive

The Dive.

When something enormous is after you in the forest, a lake looks like the obvious escape, and Doug has brought a paddleboard.

[A brown bear swimming, head and hump above the water. Label: "EXCELLENT SWIMMER".]

A bear can follow you in, because Alaska's Department of Fish and Game calls brown bears excellent swimmers. Moose are strong swimmers as well, so for now, it's a tie.

[A moose standing belly-deep in a pond, then its head going under. Label: "LUNCH".]

But a moose doesn't just swim across a lake. It goes under it. The National Park Service says the moose on Isle Royale, in Lake Superior, like to eat the water plants that grow beneath the surface. Biologists describe them submerging completely to reach those plants, something no other member of the deer family has been seen doing.

The strange part is the nose.

[Cross-section of a moose nose, the nostrils pressed shut by blue arrows. Label: "AUTOMATIC VALVE".]

Lawrence Witmer, an anatomy professor at Ohio University, dissected and scanned moose heads, and concluded that the nostrils work like a set of valves. When the head goes under, the difference between the water pressure and the air pressure closes them, so the moose can feed without flooding its nose.

[A basketball hoop beside a depth line almost twice its height. Label: "18 FT? UNMEASURED".]

A standard reference on moose biology says they can stay under for up to fifty seconds, and in nineteen fifty-five, a moose biologist reported dives of more than eighteen feet. That's about five and a half meters, nearly twice the height of a basketball hoop, and he never said how he measured it.

[The narrator's hand: BEAR 2, MOOSE 2.]

The lake isn't where you escape from a moose. It's where the moose goes for lunch.

[Doug lies back on a paddleboard in the middle of the lake, hands behind his head. The water bulges. A moose head rises beside him, wearing pondweed, chewing. It doesn't look at him. DOUG DEATHS: 196 holds. "MAYBE" is pencilled under FRIEND?.]

## The Calf

The Calf.

A newborn moose looks like it was assembled from spare legs, and although it appears to be standing in the willows entirely on its own, it almost never is.

[A calf among willow stems, all knees. A suitcase on a luggage scale next to it. Label: "13-16 KG".]

Calves are born from mid-May to early June, and a newborn usually weighs thirteen to sixteen kilograms, less than the usual weight limit on a checked airline suitcase. In good habitat, the twinning rate can be as high as three out of four, and in its first five months, a calf can grow to more than ten times its birth weight.

[A cow moose stepping out of the willows behind the calf. Ears back. Label: "LATE SPRING AND SUMMER".]

Its mother is never far away, and Fish and Game says cow moose with young calves are very protective in late spring and summer, and will attack people who come too close. Mother bears are famously the same, and the department calls them furiously protective of their cubs.

[The narrator's hand: BEAR 3, MOOSE 3. The "MAYBE" under FRIEND? is rubbed out.]

Here's the grim part.

[A simple bar: "CALVES DEAD AFTER ONE MONTH: 55%". Below it: "MOST OF THOSE: BEARS".]

The cow has a reason, because in one study on Alaska's Yukon Flats, fifty-five percent of calves died in their first month, and about three-quarters of those were killed by black bears or grizzlies.

In parts of Alaska, a moose calf's main predator is a bear, so anything approaching that calf through the willows is, as far as the cow is concerned, shaped like a bear.

[Far away on a ridge, the grizzly reference figure sits and watches. It does nothing. Doug crouches next to the calf. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

## The Apples

The Apples.

Most dangers in the northern forest live on the ground, but this one, briefly, lived in a tree.

[A suburban garden at night, near Gothenburg. An apple tree. Something very large is wedged in the branches.]

In September twenty eleven, a man in Saro, just south of Gothenburg in Sweden, came home from work and heard strange noises from his neighbor's garden. A moose was stuck in the apple tree, and police believed it had been eating fermented apples.

[Neighbors with a saw. Then a fire engine. The tree bent down, the moose sliding off. Label: "RESCUED".]

The neighbors sawed off branches, and firefighters bent the tree down until the moose could slide free. A cow moose can weigh up to about three hundred and sixty kilograms, as much as four average American men, all of it hanging in an apple tree.

The awkward part is that it may not have been drunk at all.

[A thermometer-style gauge labelled "MOOSE BLOOD ALCOHOL". The needle is replaced by a question mark.]

In twenty fourteen, Swedish Radio put the question to about a dozen researchers, and many said moose are simply too big to get drunk on the few fermented apples they eat. A drunk moose makes a great headline, and the science behind it is a lot shakier.

[An Idaho town street at night: a black bear under a backyard apple tree. Label: "ASHTON, IDAHO".]

Bears raid fruit trees too, which is why Idaho Fish and Game has sent volunteers to pick the apples in the town of Ashton before the bears get to them.

## The Run

The Run.

Everybody has heard that you cannot outrun a bear, but hardly anybody asks about the moose, which is a shame, because the answer is identical.

[A moose at full gallop, legs everywhere. Label: "ABOUT 56 KM/H".]

The Government of Newfoundland and Labrador says a moose can run at up to fifty-six kilometers an hour, and the National Park Service gives the same top speed. At that pace, a moose would cover a hundred meters in under seven seconds, while the fastest hundred meters ever run by a human took nine point five eight.

[A grizzly in a sprint beside it, slightly ahead. Label: "UP TO 64 KM/H".]

The bear, to be fair, is faster. Fish and Game says brown bears can sprint at up to sixty-four kilometers an hour, so on a flat, open meadow, the bear wins this race.

Here's the catch.

[A forest floor covered in fallen trees, criss-crossed at knee and waist height. Label: "NOT A MEADOW".]

Very little of the northern forest is a flat, open meadow. The National Park Service says a moose's long legs carry it through deep snow and even deep water, and that it uses those legs to get over obstacles. Fallen trees and deep drifts are the things that slow everybody else down, and they are exactly what those legs are for.

[The narrator's hand: BEAR 5, MOOSE 5. The last tie.]

The bear and the moose are both fast, but only one of them was built for the ground in between the meadows.

Doug ran into the fallen timber, certain nothing that large could follow him. It followed him at a walk.

## The Hooves

The Hooves.

When people picture an animal attack in the northern forest, they picture teeth and claws, but a moose has no claws and no fangs, and has never needed either.

[The scene darkens a shade. Snow on the ground. A moose rearing up on its hind legs, front hooves raised.]

Alaska's Department of Fish and Game says that when a moose charges, it often kicks forward with its front hooves. Most of those charges are bluffs, a warning to stay back, and the ones that aren't bluffs are where people get injured.

[A hoof print in snow beside a dollar bill. The print is longer. Label: "UP TO 7 IN".]

North Dakota Game and Fish puts an adult moose's front track at up to seven inches long, about eighteen centimeters, which is a little longer than a dollar bill. That is the business end of a leg long enough to step over a fallen tree.

[A cow moose standing over a calf, front legs raised toward a grey predator shape at the frame edge. Label: "DEFENSE".]

It isn't hunting you, and it never eats what it fights. The Alaska Science Forum describes how a cow defends her calf, rearing up on her hind legs and pounding the predator with her hooves.

The disturbing part is where the weapon is kept.

A moose doesn't have to reach for anything, open anything or get any closer than it already is. It's standing on the weapon.

[The narrator's hand chalks a tick under MOOSE only. BEAR 5, MOOSE 6.]

[Doug in a boxing stance, gloves up, bouncing on his toes, facing a moose. The moose rises onto its hind legs.]

Doug decided to settle things the old-fashioned way, and politely put up his fists. The moose stood up on its back legs.

[Cut to black on the first strike. Then flat snow, the cap lying on it. DOUG DEATHS: 199.]

[Hold the cap for a beat. Then a plain sober card, no Doug, no drawing: "ANCHORAGE, 1995".]

In nineteen ninety-five, on a university campus in Anchorage, a moose killed a seventy-one-year-old man as he tried to walk into a building. Witnesses said students had been harassing the moose and its calf for hours.

## The Snow

The Snow.

When winter arrives, most of Alaska's bears follow an excellent strategy, which is simply going to sleep, and the moose has no alternative plan.

[A cross-section of a hillside with a grizzly curled up in a den. A calendar beside it, eight months crossed off. Label: "UP TO 8 MONTHS".]

Fish and Game says most brown bears spend the winter in dens, sometimes for up to eight months in the north, while the moose stays awake through all of it, surviving on twigs.

[A map of Anchorage with mountains behind. Arrows pour down the valleys into the city. Label: "+600-700 IN WINTER".]

When heavy snow piles up in the Chugach Mountains, it pushes moose down the valleys and into Anchorage. In nineteen ninety-nine, a state wildlife biologist estimated that two or three hundred moose lived around Anchorage year-round, and the Alaska Science Forum reported another six or seven hundred arriving in winter.

[Snow up to a kitchen counter, a moose standing in it. Label: "OVER 90 CM".]

A state report says snow deeper than about ninety centimeters, around the height of a kitchen counter, can make it hard for a moose to move enough to feed itself. Moose therefore use whatever has already been cleared, meaning roads, driveways, railway lines and trails, and Fish and Game warns that hungry, tired moose are more likely to be aggressive, especially in deep snow. In one bad winter in the nineteen eighties, stressed moose living along the highway north of Anchorage chased dogs, children and adults.

What makes it worse is the timing.

For the hardest months of the year, the bears are asleep, and every moose is awake, hungry, exhausted and standing on the only cleared path.

[The narrator's hand: BEAR 5, MOOSE 7.]

[Doug shovels a narrow path from his front door to the gate. The moose steps into the far end. Doug stops. The moose doesn't move. Two shots, no more.]

Doug shoveled a neat path to his gate, and then a moose stepped into the other end and simply stood there. Doug waited. The moose waited longer.

[Cut to black. Then the path, the cap on the snow shovel. DOUG DEATHS: 200.]

Doug has died. Again. It was his path, but only for as long as he was the one using it.

## The Rut

The Rut.

A bear goes through its whole adult life with the weapons it grew up with. A bull moose grows a brand-new set every single year.

[A bull moose in velvet, the antlers half-grown. Label: "FASTEST GROWING TISSUE".]

Only bulls grow antlers, a new set every summer, and Fish and Game says they shed them every winter, mostly in December and January. The department calls antler the fastest-growing tissue in any mammal, and the biggest antlers in North America come from Alaska and northwest Canada.

[A rack of antlers laid across a double bed, nearly touching both edges. Label: "127 CM +".]

A trophy bull carries antlers at least a hundred and twenty-seven centimeters across, close to the width of a double bed, and the biggest racks usually grow at around ten to twelve years old.

[Two bulls pushing antler to antler. Label: "LATE SEPTEMBER - OCTOBER".]

The rut runs from late September into October, and that's when the new weapons get used, as bulls lock antlers and shove. Fish and Game warns that bulls may be aggressive toward people during the rut.

But that's not the disturbing part.

[Snow over a frozen slough. Only two sets of antlers stick up through the ice, locked together. No bodies. Label: "UNALAKLEET, 2016".]

In November twenty sixteen, near the village of Unalakleet in western Alaska, a teacher found two bull moose with their antlers locked together, frozen into eight inches of ice. A wildlife biologist at the University of Alaska Fairbanks said the pair had probably fallen into the water while locked together.

[The narrator's hand: BEAR 5, MOOSE 8.]

Every autumn, a moose arrives with a brand-new weapon, and it will use it on almost anything wearing antlers.

[Doug walks into a clearing wearing a cardboard antler hat tied under his chin, cap on top. A bull lowers its head.]

Doug made himself a pair of cardboard antlers, purely to blend in. The bull took them as a formal challenge.

[Cut to black. Then the moss: the cardboard antlers, slightly crumpled, and the cap beside them. DOUG DEATHS: 201.]

The cardboard antlers turned out to be a very effective disguise, which was exactly the problem.

## The Train

The Train.

A train cannot swerve, and it has to keep to its timetable through the whole Alaskan winter, which most sensible animals would treat as a reason to get off the line.

[A railway line through deep snow, walls of snow on either side. A moose walking between the rails. Label: "3-5 FT OF SNOW".]

In nineteen eighty-six, an article in Fish and Game's own magazine explained that in winters when the snow lies three to five feet deep, moose use the cleared railway line for walking and resting. That is about one to one and a half meters of snow.

[A calendar for one year, every day crossed with a small moose, a few days with two. Label: "385".]

On the last day of February nineteen eighty-five, a single train traveling from Anchorage to Fairbanks hit nineteen moose in one trip. By the end of that year, trains had killed three hundred and eighty-five moose along the line, more than one for every day of the year.

[A locomotive with a small whistle on its nose. A thermometer at minus thirty. The whistle has an icicle hanging from it.]

Closing the railroad for the winter was no solution, the article explains, because the trains have to keep running on schedule. People did try to fix it, and one experiment equipped the locomotives with sonic whistles designed to warn the moose. When the trains went slowly, the whistles didn't whistle, and when it got very cold, they froze silent.

This is where it turns.

[Close on the moose between the snow walls, turning to face down the track. A headlight far away.]

The same article says that once a moose is on the track, after struggling through deep snow, it won't leave, even with a train bearing down on it. It adds that, for the most part, moose have simply never learned to fear trains.

[The narrator's hand: BEAR 5, MOOSE 9.]

[Doug stands on the track waving at the moose. Speech bubble: "hi friend?" A horn. The moose turns to face the headlight, not Doug. Doug steps off the other side into the snow. Cut away on the horn. DOUG DEATHS: 201 holds.]

Doug stood on the tracks waving hello, and when the horn sounded, the moose turned to face the train instead of Doug, and Doug stepped off the other side. Doug is fine.

That was the first sensible thing Doug had done all winter, and the narrator found it a little hard to watch.

Worse than bears.

## The Wolves

The Wolves.

Out in the forest, nothing hunts a big animal quite like a wolf pack, but faced with a full-grown moose, the pack often goes home hungry.

[A map of Lake Superior, with one long island highlighted. Label: "ISLE ROYALE".]

Moose reached Isle Royale, an island in Lake Superior, in the early nineteen hundreds, and wolves arrived in the late nineteen forties, by crossing an ice bridge from Canada. Scientists began studying both in nineteen fifty-eight, more than ten years before the first Moon landing, and it is now the longest continuous predator and prey study in the world.

[A pack of wolves fanning out around a moose standing in the snow. Label: "77 TESTED".]

In the early years, the biologist David Mech followed the island's large pack from the air, and in sixty-eight hours of watching he saw sixty-six hunts. The pack seriously tested seventy-seven moose, holding them at bay or chasing them for long distances.

The unsettling part is what worked.

[Two columns. "RAN: 41. KILLED: 5". "STOOD STILL: 36. KILLED: 0".]

Of the forty-one moose that ran, five were killed, while thirty-six simply stood their ground and faced the pack until the wolves gave up, and not one of those was killed. Most of the kills he found were calves or old moose, many with signs of disease.

[The narrator's hand: BEAR 5, MOOSE 10.]

The narrator admits that this tick is about attitude rather than about people. A healthy adult moose doesn't outrun the pack. It just stands there until the wolves decide to leave.

[Doug in the middle of the pack, a wolf on each side, trotting along. Speech bubble: "hi friends?" The moose turns to face them. One by one, the wolves turn and trot away. Doug keeps trotting forward.]

Doug joined the pack as its newest member and trotted happily toward the moose. One by one, the other wolves quietly left, and Doug did not notice.

[Cut to black. Then snow, wolf tracks leading away, and the cap among them. DOUG DEATHS: 202.]

Even a whole pack of wolves knew when to walk away, and Doug was the only one who kept going.

## The Highway

The Highway.

The most dangerous thing a moose does to people has nothing to do with antlers, hooves or calves. It involves standing perfectly still, in the dark, in the road.

[A map of the island of Newfoundland, empty. Then two small moose icons at Gander Bay, "1878", and four at Howley, "1904".]

The island of Newfoundland had no moose at all, until people brought some. According to the provincial government, moose were introduced at Gander Bay in eighteen seventy-eight, and four more were shipped over from New Brunswick to Howley in nineteen hundred and four.

[The map fills with moose icons until it is solid. Label: "WELL OVER 100,000". Beside it, a giant stadium. Label: "107,601 SEATS".]

The provincial government says the island has no predator big enough to keep them in check, and the largest meat-eater on land there is the black bear. Today, estimates put the island's moose at well over a hundred thousand, enough to fill every seat in the biggest stadium in the United States.

Here's the part nobody planned for.

[A plain sober card, no Doug, no drawing: "NEWFOUNDLAND: ABOUT 539 MOOSE-VEHICLE COLLISIONS A YEAR, 2012-2020". Second line: "1987-88: 661 CRASHES, 133 PEOPLE INJURED, 3 KILLED".]

Government data show an average of five hundred and thirty-nine moose-vehicle collisions a year on Newfoundland's roads, between twenty twelve and twenty twenty. In just nineteen eighty-seven and nineteen eighty-eight, there were six hundred and sixty-one crashes involving a moose, in which a hundred and thirty-three people were injured and three died.

[A plain card: "MAINE, 2003-2017: 7,061 MOOSE CRASHES, 26 FATAL. ODDS OF A DEATH: ABOUT 13 TIMES A DEER CRASH".]

Hitting a moose is also far more dangerous than hitting a deer. Researchers in Maine went through fifteen years of crash records and found that twenty-six moose crashes had killed someone. Even after allowing for speed, the odds of a person in the car dying were about thirteen times higher with a moose than with a deer.

[The grizzly reference figure, alone, in the forest. Label: "0-2 A YEAR".]

To be fair to the bears, meet one alone in the woods, and it is the more dangerous animal. Bears kill somewhere between zero and two people in Alaska in a normal year, according to one of the scientists who reviewed more than a century of bear attacks there. It's just that moose live where people drive, and on Newfoundland's roads alone, cars hit one more than five hundred times a year.

[The narrator's hand: BEAR 5, MOOSE 11.]

It never had to attack anyone. It only had to stand in the road, on an island where somebody brought it on purpose.

[Night. An empty highway. Doug drives a small car, singing, one hand out of the window. Far ahead, at the edge of the headlights, a dark shape, far too tall. Cut to black before anything happens.]

Doug drove home along the empty highway at night, singing enthusiastically along to the radio. Far ahead, something very tall was standing very still.

[Morning. A yellow moose-crossing sign by the empty road, the cap hanging on its corner. DOUG DEATHS: 203.]

[Closing image: the field guide open on two pages. Left page: BEAR, "DOUG DEATHS: 0". Right page: MOOSE, "DOUG DEATHS: 8". The scoreboard beside it reads BEAR 5, MOOSE 11.]
