# Why Sperm Whales Form a Circle When Orcas Attack (draft 2)

[Opening frame: the thumbnail. Top-down ring of grey sperm whales, heads in, tails out, on dark-blue water. Tiny Doug in the sperm whale calf costume in the middle, cap on top. Black orca fins outside.]

A sperm whale can dive a kilometer down on one breath. When orcas attack, it stays at the surface.

By the end, Doug is in the middle of its circle, with thirty-five orcas outside.

## The Easy Way Out

The Easy Way Out. During one orca attack, a group of sperm whales never once tried to dive away, not even when the orcas backed off to rest.

[THE RING card appears beside the death counter: empty dark water, no whales, no fins.]

That should not happen. A tagged sperm whale stays down for about forty-five minutes per dive. So for decades, experts assumed its defence against orcas was simple: take a breath, go down, and wait it out in the dark.

Then, in nineteen ninety-seven, researchers watched orcas actually kill one, and the escape hatch stayed shut the whole time.

Here's the problem: the babies. Sperm whale mothers usually feed at depths too great, and for too long, for their young calves to follow. So when the hunters arrive, the whole family stays up top with the one member who can't leave.

It's a family that owns a bunker and won't use it, because the toddler can't manage the stairs.

[Doug in the grey, block-headed sperm whale calf costume, cap on top. No big eyes, no smile.]

The orcas are hunting whales, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as one, and he decides to follow a mother down anyway.

[Doug sinks about four meters, then pops back up like a cork.]

Last time Doug followed one of these down, the cap came back on its own. This time Doug makes it about four meters before the costume floats him straight back up.

Doug is fine. Doug floats.

Which means the calf is always at the surface, and somebody in the family has to stay up there with it.

Before.

## The Babysitters

The Babysitters. A newborn sperm whale is about four meters long and weighs around nine hundred kilograms, which makes it heavier than some small cars on its first day.

[THE RING: the first grey whale dots appear, loosely scattered.]

Female sperm whales live in family units of about a dozen, mothers, daughters, aunts and their young, and they can stay together for years. In one well-studied Caribbean population, the mothers' feeding dives last around forty minutes and reach around five hundred meters. A young calf can't keep that up, dive after dive, so it mostly stays near the top.

So the family staggers its dives. In a study off the Galápagos, groups with calves left shorter gaps with no adult at the surface. With enough adults around, calves were rarely alone up there for more than about five minutes.

It's a day-care rota where every parent takes a shift, for a toddler that outweighs the car that would normally drop it off.

The strange part is how far the sharing goes, because there is some evidence that calves also suckle from females who are not their mothers.

[Doug, in the costume, beside a clipboard titled SHIFT ROTA. His name is pencilled in last, in someone else's handwriting.]

Doug's name is on the rota, because he agreed to wear the costume. Doug did not agree to this.

[Speech bubble: "hi friends?" The family flukes up and dives.]

He says hello to the family, the family goes to lunch, and Doug is now in charge of nine hundred kilograms of sleeping calf.

[The calf rolls over in its sleep. Only the cap pops up beside it.]

Doug has died. Again.

The calf slept through the whole thing, in a system that is nearly perfect, except that the one piece it's missing happens to be the biggest whale in the family.

## The Missing Bulls

The Missing Bulls. The biggest sperm whales on Earth are almost never there when the family needs them.

[THE RING: more whale dots drift in. Still no fins.]

A grown male can reach sixteen meters and forty-five tons, about three times the weight of a female, but young males leave their family somewhere between four and twenty-one years old.

As they grow, they move toward the poles. Adult males live mainly in cold, high-latitude water, while the families stay in tropical and subtropical seas, and the big males visit the warm water mainly to breed. One team of researchers called the sperm whale the most sexually segregated animal species in the world.

Here's the catch: when the orcas come, the giants that made this species famous are usually somewhere near the ice, and the defence is run by mothers, aunts, grandmothers and teenagers.

It's a nursery whose only security guard works at the South Pole.

[Doug, out of the costume, paddles a kayak toward a wall of polar ice. Snow builds up on the cap.]

Doug paddles toward the polar ice to fetch one, and after weeks he finally finds a bull, floating alone in the cold, and explains the situation.

Out here, a grown male mostly lives alone, and he would like to keep it that way. The bull hears every word. The bull does not come.

[The bull does not look up. Doug turns slowly white and stiff. The cap freezes on top of him.]

Doug has died. Again.

The cap is frozen on top, still facing the tropics, where the family is listening carefully for trouble that doesn't make a sound.

## The Silence

The Silence. Orcas that hunt mammals hardly make a sound until the hunt is over.

[THE RING: the first two small black fins appear outside the scattered dots.]

Off British Columbia and Alaska, researchers found that the mammal-eating kind of orca called far less often than the fish-eating kind, and only got noisy after a kill or while splashing around at the surface. The likely reason is simple: seals, porpoises and whales can hear orca calls very well, and most fish can't. If your dinner can hear you, you stop talking.

The disturbing part is who's making all the noise. A hunting sperm whale clicks for most of its dive to find food in the dark, and survey ships towing underwater microphones have detected sperm whales thirty-seven kilometers away.

So one side moves in silence, and the other side is broadcasting.

It's a game of hide and seek where one team rings a bell the whole time, and the other team has taken a vow of silence.

[Doug in the costume, on the surface, wearing enormous headphones and holding a hydrophone on a cable.]

Doug is on listening duty, and his only job is to hear the orcas coming. He takes it very seriously.

[Speech bubble: "all quiet". Behind him, fins rise.]

Doug reports that it is all quiet, and he is correct. It is extremely quiet.

[Cut to black before anything reaches him. Back in: the cap hanging on the hydrophone cable.]

Doug has died. Again.

The cap heard nothing either, and a moment later the family saw the fins and did something very strange with its own bodies.

The circle.

## The Marguerite

The Marguerite. When the hunters close in, a small sperm whale family can turn itself into a flower.

[THE RING: the whale dots slide together into a ring, heads in, tails out. Fins: six.]

The whales lie at the surface in a ring, with their heads together in the middle and their tails pointing out like the petals of a daisy. Scientists call it a rosette, or a marguerite, after that daisy.

Researchers have seen it formed by groups of eight, nine and twelve whales, and in the Gulf of Mexico, a ring of twelve had two calves with it. Bigger groups of thirty or fifty pack into a tight raft instead, and either way the most vulnerable members can go in the middle.

It's a ring of school buses parked nose to nose, with the kids in the middle.

But that's not the strange part. In a ring, every adult is on the outside, and during one attack, researchers noticed that even the badly hurt whales kept an equal place in the circle. Nobody hid behind anybody.

[Doug, back in the calf costume, squeezes between two enormous grey heads and wriggles into the centre. The ring closes. This is the thumbnail shot.]

Doug squeezes into the middle, nobody asks to see his papers, and the ring closes around him. For the first time in four hundred and five deaths, Doug is the best-protected thing in the ocean.

Doug is fine. Doug is in the middle.

The safest seat in the ocean has one problem: every adult guarding it is facing the wrong way.

## The Tail

The Tail. In a marguerite, the only part of a sperm whale pointed at the enemy is the tail.

[THE RING: fins, nine.]

Sperm whale flukes can be five meters across, which is about the length of a family car, swung by roughly fifteen tons of female whale. So the plan seems obvious: point the heavy end outward and swing it.

During a real orca attack, researchers saw the whales lift their tails out of the water fairly often, both during the raids and in the quiet minutes between them.

But here's what they actually saw: most of those tails were waving limply with no obvious aim, and some were held at odd angles. The few hard slaps looked like reflexes, they didn't seem to target anyone, and they didn't seem to scare anyone off. The researchers never saw a sperm whale deliberately strike an orca. Four hours, and not once.

It's a castle wall lined with cannons, and nobody has lit a single one.

[Doug, outside the ring, holding a tiny sign: HELPING.]

Doug swims outside the ring to help, which makes him the only one out there with a plan. The plan is to point at the orcas.

[One reflex slap. Doug becomes a dot on the horizon.]

He gets one reflex. Doug has died. Again.

[The cap flutters down and lands on the water beside the ring.]

The cap landed right next to the ring, which had finally hit something. And for years, this was considered a winning defence.

## The Other Hunters

The Other Hunters. Before nineteen ninety-seven, the ring had a perfect record in every fight that scientists had written up in detail.

[THE RING card flips to a small map: the Galápagos gets two red ticks, the Gulf of Mexico one. Fins on the card: still nine.]

Off the Galápagos, false killer whales attacked a group of twenty to twenty-five sperm whales, and the group split into two separate rosettes. In the Gulf of Mexico, short-finned pilot whales harassed a group of twelve, and the group formed a ring.

And off the Galápagos, researchers had even watched orcas go after a group of thirty-one. The whales packed into a tight raft, then got away, and the orcas got nothing. Every time, the sperm whales came away with minor injuries, and the attackers gave up.

It's a fire drill the family has run three times, in two different seas, and passed every time.

The worst part is what that record did to the experts. A leading sperm whale researcher once wrote that the sperm whale has no enemies in the ocean except humans, because big size, big teeth, deep dives and a ring that works made it look untouchable.

[Doug, back in the calf costume, on the outer edge of a ring, saluting.]

Doug has met false killer whales. They gave him a tuna.

This time, Doug skips the middle and takes a spot on the outer edge, to be useful.

[Cut away to the empty sea for a beat. Cut back: a gap in the ring with the cap floating in it.]

Doug has died. Again.

The record said nobody gets through the ring, until one morning in October, when the orcas brought a team that just kept growing.

Contact.

## The Rescue

The Rescue. When orcas dragged a sperm whale out of the ring, another sperm whale came out after it within a minute.

[THE RING: one dot slides out of the ring. Fins: twelve, gathering around the loose dot.]

This was the attack of nineteen ninety-seven, watched by government whale researchers from their ship. Two hours in, the orcas pulled one whale out of the circle and went after it from every side. Almost at once, one of the larger whales left the ring, swam over to its companion and lined up alongside it, and it was attacked straight away. Then the pair moved slowly back into the ring, with the rescuer leading the way.

It wasn't a one-off, because every time a whale was pulled out, one or sometimes two others left almost immediately, flanked it, and led it back into the formation.

It's running back into a burning building for a neighbour, and then doing it again.

Here's the hard part: the orcas only needed one whale, and the researchers later wrote that if the family had let one go, the rest might have been spared. The family never let one go.

[Doug, in the costume, gets bumped out of the ring in the chaos. Two big grey whales peel off, one on each side, and steer him back into the centre.]

In the chaos, Doug gets nudged out of the ring, and two adults swim out, take one side each, and steer him straight back to the middle.

Doug is fine. The narrator had already drawn something else.

That was two hours in, and the orcas had two more.

Four hours.

## The Fourth Hour

The Fourth Hour. By the last hour, the orcas were attacking every one or two minutes.

[A crude ship at sunrise, a ring of nine grey whales on the horizon. THE RING: fins, twenty.]

It began on the twenty-first of October, nineteen ninety-seven, about a hundred and thirty kilometers off central California. At first light, a bridge officer on a research ship spotted orcas attacking sperm whales, and the ring had already formed. There were nine sperm whales, all about the size of adult females.

The researchers first counted about a dozen orcas, but the number kept growing as the hours passed, and by the end they estimated there could have been thirty-five.

The adult female orcas did the attacking, in waves of four or five, then pulled back and rested before going in again. The big males waited in the distance, while the sperm whales held the ring the whole time, and never tried to dive.

Nobody saw a calf that morning. Today, there is one, and it's Doug.

[Doug in the calf costume, in the centre of the ring. THE RING: fins, thirty-five.]

In the last hour, the waves came faster, with twelve or more orcas at a time. At eleven in the morning, the ring broke, and one whale was killed, and the orcas took it away and left the rest.

The cruelest part is what happened to the rest. Most, if not all, of the survivors were hurt, several seriously, and the scientists suspected that three or four died later. They thought it was quite possible that none of them made it.

The circle is the best defence sperm whales have, and that morning it wasn't enough. Today, the species is listed as Vulnerable.

[The ring holds, then a gap opens beside Doug. Cut to black before anything reaches him.]

Doug has died. Again.

[The LIVE feed on the survey ship's monitor, red LIVE tag. Calm, empty water where the ring was. The cap drifts into the exact middle and stays.]

On the ship's monitor, the water is calm and empty, and the cap is floating in the exact centre of where the ring used to be, still holding its place.

Doug deaths: four hundred and eight. Nobody ever pulls this number back. Subscribe, and watch it break four hundred and ten.
