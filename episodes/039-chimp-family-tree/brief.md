### Distant relatives.

**1. The Spear** (hook item, one of the three strongest; ~60+ million years apart; death 302)
- Facts:
  - At Fongoli, Senegal, savanna chimpanzees break off branches, trim them and **bite the tips to a point**, then jab them into tree hollows where bushbabies
    (galagos) sleep in the day. Pruetz & Bertolani 2007, *Current Biology*, reported 22 cases; verify the count.
  - **Most of the spear hunting is done by females and young chimps**, not adult males (Pruetz & Bertolani 2007; Pruetz et al. 2015, *Royal Society Open
    Science*, with a larger multi-year sample; verify the shares).
  - Described as the first non-human animal seen routinely making weapons to hunt vertebrates (verify the exact wording the papers or the press release use;
    keep "routinely" if the source does).
  - Galagos are prosimians, our most distant primate relatives on this list (divergence date from a phylogeny source).
- Analogy: the spear is about the length of a broom handle, sharpened at one end with teeth (use the paper's stick length if given).
- **Cold-open fact (first sentence):** "In Senegal, chimpanzees bite sticks into spears and stab them into the trees where bushbabies sleep." (The writer can
  reword; the stick, the teeth and the bushbaby must be in it.)
- **Twist by 0:45 (≤145 words):** it isn't the big males. The weapon was mostly invented and used by the females and the young, the ones who usually
  lose out when the males hunt. The smallest chimps built the first spear.
- Doug beat: Doug in a bushbaby costume (big ears; adult-coded, unsmiling, **no huge cute eyes**) asleep in a tree hollow; "Doug did not agree to this."
  (here, he was volunteered). A stick tip appears at the hole. Cut to black **before contact**. Death 302, flat. Cap on the hollow's lip.
  - Re-hook: "That's the small chimps, with a stick. The adult males hunt in a team, and they hunt monkeys."

**2. The Monkey Hunt** (~25-30 million years apart; Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - At Ngogo in Kibale National Park, Uganda, the chimps hunted red colobus monkeys so heavily that the local red colobus population fell sharply
    (Teelen 2008, *Primates*; Lwanga et al. 2011; verify the percentage and years before giving a number).
  - Hunts are mostly by adult males working together, and meat is shared with allies (Mitani & Watts; Gombe: Stanford's work; verify one sourced line).
  - **Adult male red colobus fight back**, and hunts fail more often when more colobus males are around (Stanford 1995 or a later study; verify).
- Analogy: one chimp community taking a share of a monkey population each year that a town would notice as "a whole school's worth of people
  gone" (only if the source gives a figure; otherwise use a simpler sourced comparison or none).
- Twist: the monkeys' best defence is their own big males, and it works on the day, but at Ngogo it didn't work over the years.
- Doug beat: Doug in a red colobus costume huddles behind three large colobus males on a branch. The chimps look at the males, look at Doug, and pick
  a different tree. "Doug is fine." The narrator notes that the population was not. (No monkey death on screen, no carcass.)
  - Re-hook: "Monkeys are cousins at about thirty million years. The next relative is an ape, and it weighs more than Doug and the chimp together."

### Close relatives.

**3. The Gorilla** (~8-10 million years apart; strong mid item; death 303)
- Facts:
  - In Loango National Park, Gabon, researchers saw chimpanzees attack gorillas twice in 2019, the **first recorded lethal attacks of chimps on gorillas**
    (Southern, Deschner, Pika et al. 2021, *Scientific Reports*; verify).
  - Before that, the two species were mostly seen feeding near each other peacefully, sometimes in the same fig trees (same paper; verify the wording).
  - The researchers' leading idea is competition for food (fruit) that may be getting scarcer; they offer it as a hypothesis (verify; say "may").
- Analogy: a silverback weighs about as much as two adult men; a chimp group is still willing to take them on in numbers (weights from a sourced range).
- Twist: the gorillas are bigger, and that isn't what decides it. The chimps came in a group of more than twenty and went for **the smallest gorillas in
  the group**. Doug is the smallest gorilla in this group. (Victims were infants: the script says "the smallest" or "two young gorillas" in one plain
  clause, **never** describes the attack, and no young gorilla is drawn.)
- Doug beat: Doug in a **dark** gorilla suit sits proudly between two silverbacks under a fig tree. A line of chimp silhouettes on the ridge, counted
  out loud by the narrator. The silverbacks stand up; Doug stays sitting. Cut to black. Death 303, flat. Cap under the fig tree.

**4. The Human** (~6-7 million years apart; death 304)
- Facts:
  - **Chimpanzees are more closely related to humans than to gorillas.** Chimps and humans share a common ancestor about 6-7 million years ago
    (Chimpanzee Sequencing and Analysis Consortium 2005, *Nature*, and/or a phylogeny source; give a range).
  - The genomes are about 98-99% identical in comparable single-letter DNA (same 2005 paper; give the paper's exact framing, which is narrower than "98% the
    same", and say so in one line if it matters).
  - Wild chimpanzees have attacked people where farms meet forest (e.g. Bossou, Guinea: Hockings et al. 2010, *International Journal of Primatology*;
    Uganda: McLennan & Hockings; verify). **Numbers or plain statements only: no names, no ages, no injuries, no children.** If a source's details are mostly
    about child victims, keep only the general statement and cut the rest.
- Analogy: if the whole ape family tree were a 24-hour day, chimps and humans parted ways at about (writer computes from the sourced dates) in the evening,
  and gorillas left at lunch. (Only if the arithmetic is shown in `facts.md`.)
- Twist: on the family tree, the gorilla Doug just dressed up as is a more distant cousin to the chimp than Doug himself is. Doug didn't need a costume
  for this one. He is already the relative.
- Doug beat: Doug, no costume, walks along a farm track at the forest edge with a sack of maize on his shoulder (crop raiding is the documented
  context; verify the crop). He waves at the trees ("cousin?"). Cut to black. Death 304, flat. Cap on the maize sack. One callback line to 011 allowed here:
  "Doug has already lost an arm-wrestle to one of these." (one sentence, nothing more about strength).
  - Re-hook: "Gorillas are further from chimps than we are. There's one ape closer than us, and it lives just across a river."

**5. The Bonobo** (~1-2 million years apart; Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine."; keep to about 180 words)
- Facts:
  - Bonobos are the chimpanzee's closest living relative; the two species split about 1-2 million years ago (verify a range, e.g. Prüfer et al. 2012, *Nature*).
  - Bonobos live only **south of the Congo River**, chimpanzees north and east of it; the river is often given as the barrier that split them
    (verify, and say "probably" if the sources do).
  - In the large multi-site comparison, lethal aggression in bonobos was **far rarer than in chimps** (Wilson et al. 2014 reported one suspected killing in
    bonobos; this is the one Wilson fact allowed, as a contrast, and it must not repeat 011's numbers).
- Analogy: the Congo River is several kilometres wide in places (verify) and the deepest river measured on Earth (verify; Oberg/USGS-type source), so the
  closest relative the chimp has is also the one it never meets.
- Twist: the closest relative is the safest one, and only because a river is in the way. That is the exception, and the narrator says so.
- Doug beat: Doug in the chimp costume with a hand-written "BONOBO" name tag sits with a bonobo group south of the river; "hi friend?" (the only one this
  episode). Nothing happens. "Doug is fine." Field guide: "MAYBE" pencilled. The narrator looks across the river.
  - Re-hook: "Now cross the river. On the other side, the relatives are the same species."

### Same species.

**6. The Patrol** (same species, a stranger; death 305)
- Facts:
  - Male chimpanzees at Ngogo go on **boundary patrols**: they walk in silent single file to the edge of their territory and sometimes beyond it
    (Watts & Mitani 2001, *Behaviour*; Mitani & Watts 2005; verify the description and how often patrols occur).
  - On patrol they move quietly, stop to listen and sniff, and attack a lone outsider when they outnumber it (verify the sourced behaviour; do **not**
    reuse 011's 8-to-1 figure).
  - Most victims of chimp killings are **males from neighbouring communities** (cite a source other than Wilson 2014 if possible, or a single clause).
- Analogy: a patrol walks a border a few kilometres long, about a (sourced length) walk, without a sound (verify the patrol distance).
- Twist: chimps are loud, all the time, except on patrol. The quiet is the warning.
- Doug beat: Doug in the chimp costume, now north of the river, walks alone along a ridge, whistling. The whistling stops when the forest goes quiet.
  Field guide: "MAYBE" erased. Cut to black. Death 305, flat. Cap on the ridge.
  - Re-hook: "One stranger is a patrol. A whole neighbouring family is a project."

**7. The Neighbours** (same species, a neighbouring community; death 306)
- Facts:
  - Over about ten years the Ngogo chimps killed at least 18-21 members of a neighbouring community and then **took over the area**, expanding their
    territory by about 22% (Mitani, Watts & Amsler 2010, *Current Biology*; verify the count and percentage).
  - A later study linked Ngogo's expansion to **more births and better infant survival** in the winning community (verify: Wood et al. / Sandel et al.,
    2023-2025, *PNAS*; give the finding, not a number, unless sourced).
- Analogy: the land they took is about (sourced area) square kilometres, roughly (an everyday sourced comparison, e.g. a number of football pitches or a
  small town's area).
- Twist: it wasn't random violence. It was real estate, and it paid off for the winners. (Say "the study suggests", no moral.)
- Doug beat: Doug in the chimp costume builds a tidy nest in the neighbouring territory and puts up a crude "DOUG'S" sign. The sign is pulled down;
  the narrator notes the territory has a new owner. Cut to black. Death 306, flat. Cap left in the nest.

### Family.

**8. The Old King** (same group, a former leader; death 307; bookends item 1 at Fongoli)
- Facts:
  - At Fongoli (the spear community from item 1), a former alpha male named **Foudouko** was ousted, lived on the edge of the group for years, and was killed
    by members of his own community when he came back in 2013 (Pruetz et al. 2017, *International Journal of Primatology*; verify the dates and the
    "inferred" wording; researchers did not see the attack).
  - Researchers linked it to unusual pressure: a male-biased group and scarce food in a savanna habitat (verify; "may" if the paper hedges).
  - **What happened to the body afterwards is not mentioned, in any form.** The script also gives no injury detail.
- Analogy: he had been the leader, then lived on the edge for about five years (verify), roughly the time between two football World Cups and a bit
  (writer uses a cleaner sourced comparison if this doesn't hold).
- Twist: this is the same community that invented the spear in item 1. The first weapon and the first old king, the same group.
- Doug beat: Doug in the chimp costume gives the returning old king a paper crown (`paper_crown.json`) "because someone should". The group notices the

  - Re-hook: "He was one chimp who came back. In the last one, a whole family splits in two, and Doug is standing on the line."

**9. The Split** (boss, the most extreme item; the closest relatives possible: former groupmates; death 308)
- Facts:
  - Gombe, Tanzania: in the early 1970s the Kasakela community split; the breakaway Kahama group was then destroyed in the **"Four-Year War"** (1974-1978),
    with the Kasakela males killing the Kahama males one by one (Goodall 1986, *The Chimpanzees of Gombe*; verify the dates and how many were seen killed vs
    disappeared).
  - Ngogo again: the largest known chimp community split into two groups in the 2010s (around 2015), and since about 2018 the groups have been killing
    each other, including chimps that had grown up together (verify the paper: Sandel et al. 2025, *Science*, or the 2024-2025 reporting; **verify before
    writing anything**, and give only what it says).
  - Scientists don't fully know why groups split; the paper's suggestions (e.g. the loss of individuals who linked the two sides, or too many males)
    are given as suggestions (verify).
- Analogy: the chimps on both sides had shared sleeping trees, food and grooming partners for years, like two halves of a school class who end up on
  either side of a fence (or a sourced, cleaner image).
- Twist (the title's payoff): the closest relative a chimpanzee has is the one it grew up with, and that is the one it ends up killing. The family tree
  doesn't protect anyone. It just tells you who's next.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug in the chimp costume, wanting to stay friends with everyone, stands exactly on the crude chalk line where the family split.
  - Both groups look at him. Each decides he belongs to the other side. "wait" (his only bubble here).
  - Cut to black. Death 308, flat, quiet. The FAMILY TREE sign gets its red stamp.
- Closing (≤30 words of outro, no subscribe pitch): the cap sits on the line. Neither side crosses to pick it up. Narrator's last line is about the
  cap ("Nobody will cross to get it. It is the only thing out here that still belongs to both sides." or similar); **not** a recap.

- **Kid appeal (HIGH: chimps are a kids' and meme favourite):** no baby chimps, no chimps in clothes, no circus, no TV/film chimps, no banana jokes, no
