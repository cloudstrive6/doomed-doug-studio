## The Female

The Female.

[A grid of seven hundred and eighty tiny snake icons. Label: "780 CAUGHT, 7 YEARS". One fat icon glows red.]

That anaconda was a female, and so is every giant anaconda you have ever pictured. Over seven years in Venezuela, the biologist Jesús Rivas and his team caught and measured seven hundred and eighty wild anacondas.

[Split screen. Left: a huge female coil, tag "97.5 KG". Right: a thin male, tag "17 KG". A stick man for scale between them, tag "AVERAGE MAN, 90 KG".]

The heaviest male in the whole study weighed just seventeen.

A grown female averages almost five times the weight of a grown male, the biggest size gap between the sexes reported in any land animal with a backbone.

[A grown man and a packed suitcase side by side, as silhouettes. Labels: "HER" and "HIM".]

Picture a couple where one weighs as much as a grown man and the other weighs about as much as a packed suitcase.

Here's the twist.

[A map of northern South America split by a red line. Top: "NORTHERN". Bottom: "SOUTHERN". A calendar page: "2024".]

In twenty twenty-four, a team led by Rivas compared anaconda genes from nine countries and argued that the green anaconda is really two species. In the genes they compared, the northern and southern snakes differ by about five and a half percent, after splitting roughly ten million years ago.

[A small "?" stamp on the red line.]

For now, the main reptile checklist still counts them as one species. If the split holds, the heaviest snake on Earth was quietly two different snakes, and scientists are still arguing about her name.

[Doug on the riverbank with a yellow tape measure, hopeful smile, holding the end out toward the water. A big female slides up out of the shallows toward the tape.]

Doug has brought a tape measure, because somebody should confirm the record. Doug did not agree to this, although he did volunteer for the measuring.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 323. The cap lies on the rolled-up tape measure. The field guide opens to a GREEN ANACONDA page; a red "NO" is stamped in the FRIEND? box.]

The record has confirmed Doug, and she was not even hunting yet.

## The Nostrils

The Nostrils.

[A flat brown pool. Two small olive bumps break the surface. Label: "EYES + NOSTRILS, ON TOP".]

A green anaconda's eyes and nostrils sit on top of its head, so it can breathe and watch while almost all of it stays underwater.

[Zoom out. Under the surface, a long dark shape stretches from the bumps to the far side of the pool.]

At first glance, the pool is empty, which is exactly the plan. The anaconda is an ambush hunter, and it lies in the shallows with only its snout above the surface, waiting for something to come down to drink.

[A close-up of one eye with a thin clear shield over it, like swimming goggles.]

A thin, clear scale covers each eye, which helps it see underwater and keeps the eye safe from the brush on land.

[A stopwatch over the water ticks to "10 MIN".]

When it does go fully under, it can stay submerged for about ten minutes before it needs those nostrils again.

[A log floating in the pool. Two olives sit on one end of it. Label: "NOT A LOG".]

From the bank, all you see is two bumps the size of olives on a log that isn't a log.

[A dry, cracked plain. Only one muddy pool is left. Several snake shapes crowd into it. Label: "DRY SEASON".]

In the dry season on the Venezuelan plains, the water shrinks, and the anacondas gather in whatever pools are left.

The unsettling part is the menu.

[A row of animals walking to the pool: a wading bird, a deer, a capybara, a caiman. A dotted line marks the water's edge.]

Rivas described them as opportunists that will take any prey they can kill and swallow. The snake does not care what comes to the water, as long as something does.

[Doug, cap on, steps from the bank onto the log in the middle of the pool, arms out for balance, hopeful smile.]

Doug needs to cross the pool, and there is a perfectly good log right there in the middle of it.

[A splash. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 324. The cap floats beside two olive bumps.]

Doug has died. Again. And the pool is about to get much busier, because it's mating season.

## The Breeding Ball

The Breeding Ball.

[A tangle of snakes in shallow water. One fat female in the middle, several thin males wrapped around her. Label: "1 FEMALE".]

The same dry season that crowds anacondas into the last pools is also when they breed, and it does not look romantic.

[Tally marks pop up around the tangle, one to thirteen. Label: "UP TO 13 MALES".]

One female ends up wrapped in a slow, writhing ball of males. Rivas found balls with anywhere from one to thirteen males, and three was typical.

[A calendar with pages flipping. Label: "AVERAGE 18 DAYS". A second label: "LONGEST 46".]

The balls he timed lasted eighteen days on average, and the longest went on for forty-six. Individual males hung on for about two weeks, and some stayed for more than a month.

[A knot of green garden hoses filling a bathtub.]

Imagine a knot of garden hoses the size of a bathtub, and every single hose is trying to be first.

[The female grows bigger on screen. A row of baby snakes appears behind her, larger and more numerous than behind a smaller female.]

Size is the prize here, because larger females are courted by more males, and they give birth to more babies, and bigger ones.

The darkest part is what happens afterwards.

[The female alone. A calendar flips through seven months. Her plate stays empty.]

A pregnant female does not eat for about seven months. Females have also been found eating males around the breeding balls, and Rivas suggested that some may eat a partner to help get through that long fast.

[Doug wades into the pool with a whistle, hopeful smile, arms out to separate the snakes. Nobody looks at him. The cap ends up sitting on top of the ball.]

Doug wades in with a whistle to break it up, but every snake is busy.

[The field guide page. "MAYBE" is pencilled in the FRIEND? box.]

Doug is fine. This is the only part of the hunt where Doug is not the point. In seven months, though, she will be very hungry.

Bitten.

## The Teeth

The Teeth.

[A big snake skull. Six rows of small hooked teeth glow white: four rows on top, two on the bottom. A venom bottle with a red X.]

The anaconda has no venom. What it has instead is six rows of sharp teeth, four along the top of the mouth and two along the bottom, and every one of them points backwards.

[The skull from the side. Red arrows on the teeth all point toward the throat.]

It strikes from the water, seizes the prey with those teeth, and drags it under. The teeth are fixed in place, with no folding fangs and no poison doing the work, so they simply hold on.

[A fish hook going into a cork. An arrow in: "EASY". An arrow out: red X.]

The teeth work like a fish hook, where going in is easy and backing out is not.

But that's not what the bite is for.

[A bite mark, then a coil whips around from behind it. Label: "HOLD", then "WRAP".]

The bite is not meant to kill anything. It's there so the prey can't leave while the rest of the snake arrives, and it matters, because some of this prey bites back.

[A capybara with big front teeth, standing its ground. Behind it, a coiled snake with a few small scar marks on its side.]

Capybaras have razor-sharp front teeth, and Rivas found female anacondas carrying scars that matched capybara bites. He once found a female constricting a young capybara in the bushes while an adult capybara stood nearby, staring at the spot. The anaconda's answer is to bite first and never let go.

[Doug on the bank with a white box marked ANTIVENOM, hopeful smile. He holds it up to the snake.]

Doug has brought antivenom, just in case, but there is nothing in this snake for it to cure. The last time Doug met a snake, the antivenom was the point.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 325. The cap sits on the unopened antivenom box.]

Doug has died. Again. The bite was only the handshake before the squeeze.

Squeezed.

## The Coils

The Coils.

[A thick coil wraps around a grey silhouette. A big red heart icon sits in the middle.]

For a long time, people said constrictors kill by suffocation, and in a close relative, the boa constrictor, that turned out to be wrong.

[A lab bench with a clipboard. A cartoon gauge labeled "BLOOD PRESSURE" swings down. A stopwatch reads "6 S".]

In a twenty fifteen study, researchers measured what happens inside an anesthetized lab rat while a boa squeezes it. Within six seconds, the blood pressure in an artery in its leg had fallen by half, while the pressure in the big veins near its heart went up sixfold.

[A second gauge, a heart icon. The stopwatch reads "60 S". Label: "HEART RATE: HALF".]

Within a minute its heart rate had nearly halved. By the end of the squeeze, the heart was beating almost four times slower, because it was pushing against a pressure it could not beat.

Here's the problem.

[The coil. Inside, a stick-figure lung still holds blue air. The red heart icon goes grey.]

The prey does not die from running out of air, because its blood stops moving first. An animal can last a while without a breath, but not without a pulse, which is exactly why this is so much faster. The study used boas, but its authors argued that killing this way gave all constrictors, anacondas included, an edge.

[A blood pressure cuff on a stick arm, puffing up bigger and bigger. A display reads "BEEP". It keeps puffing.]

Being constricted is like a blood pressure cuff that keeps on inflating long after it beeps.

[Doug in the coils, cheeks puffed out, holding a huge breath, thumbs up.]

Doug has taken a very deep breath, to wait it out. Air was never the problem.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 326. The cap hangs on a reed by the water. The field guide's pencilled "MAYBE" is rubbed out.]

Doug has died. And the snake knew the exact moment it happened.

## The Heartbeat

The Heartbeat.

[A boa wrapped around a rat-shaped dummy with a small pump and a tube. A green heartbeat line on a monitor.]

A constrictor can feel its prey's heartbeat, and it decides when to stop by paying attention to it.

[The heartbeat line is steady. The coil gets thicker and darker.]

In a twenty twelve study, boas squeezed dead rats that had been fitted with an artificial heart. While the heart was beating, the snakes squeezed longer and harder than they did when there was no heartbeat at all.

[The heartbeat line goes flat. The coil loosens.]

When the researchers stopped the heart halfway through, the snakes let go soon after. Even snakes that had never caught live prey did it, so it seems to be at least partly built in.

[A smartwatch on a stick wrist. The screen shows a pulse, then a flat line, then "OFF".]

The snake is a smartwatch that only switches off when your pulse does.

The worst part is that the squeeze barely gets in the way of the snake's own breathing.

[A long snake body with ribs drawn as stripes. A blood pressure cuff squeezes the middle. The ribs behind the cuff light up and move instead.]

You would expect a squeeze like that to make breathing hard for the snake, too. But in a twenty twenty-two study, researchers wrapped a blood pressure cuff around part of a boa, and it simply switched to breathing with ribs further along its body. It can squeeze with one part of itself and breathe with another.

[Doug in the coils, eyes closed, hands folded, a tiny calm heart beating slowly over his chest.]

Doug tries to calm his heart down, to fool it. A slow heartbeat is still a heartbeat, and it counts every one.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 327. The cap floats in the reeds.]

Doug has died, calmly. Which leaves the question of how the snake gets the prize inside.

Swallowed.

## The Jaws

The Jaws.

[A snake skull from below. The two halves of the lower jaw are separate at the chin, joined by a stretchy pink band. Label: "NOT FUSED".]

People say a snake dislocates its jaw to swallow big prey, but it really doesn't.

[The pink band stretches wide. The jaw halves spread apart.]

The two halves of an anaconda's lower jaw are not fused at the chin. They're joined by elastic ligaments that stretch, and the jaw bones hinge loosely on the skull, so the mouth opens far wider than the head looks.

[The snake's jaws ratchet forward over a round shape, one side at a time. Red arrows alternate left, right, left, right.]

Then it walks its jaws forward over the meal, one side at a time, while the teeth on the other side hold on.

[A cutaway of the snake's throat. A small tube pokes forward out of the floor of the mouth. Label: "WINDPIPE". A small wind icon.]

A big swallow can take a long time, so the snake can push the end of its windpipe forward and to the side, and keep breathing around a full mouth.

[Three silhouettes on scales: a capybara "55 KG", a deer "70 KG", a caiman "55 KG".]

Rivas recorded anacondas eating capybaras and deer, and he wrote that they can take prey as big as a grown capybara, a grown deer, or a full-grown caiman, up to around seventy kilos.

[A garden hose stretched around a beach ball.]

Swallowing a capybara is like a garden hose swallowing a beach ball, slowly, without a straw.

The strange part is what stops it.

[A deer silhouette lying on the riverbank with big antlers. The snake lies in the water next to it. A calendar flips through several days. Vultures circle.]

In nineteen ninety-two, Rivas found a female lying next to a full-grown deer she had apparently killed but could not swallow, because of the antlers, and she lay beside it for days.

[Doug in a full capybara suit, deadpan, red cap on top, standing at the water's edge. Speech bubble to the two bumps in the water: "hi friend?"]

Capybaras don't have antlers, and neither does Doug's capybara suit, so this time nothing gets in the way.

[The jaws open. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 328. Next shot: a smooth lump in the water, the cap balanced on top.]

Doug has died. Again. Next, the anaconda takes on prey that can eat it right back.

## The Caiman

The Caiman.

[A riverbank. A big anaconda and a spectacled caiman face each other. Label: "SAME POOL".]

Anacondas eat spectacled caimans, small crocodilian relatives of alligators that share their pools.

[A thick coil around a caiman. The caiman's tail sticks out at an angle.]

In nineteen ninety-nine, a snake about four and a half meters long was seen constricting a spectacled caiman nearly two meters long, and Rivas wrote that anacondas catch caimans often on the Venezuelan plains.

[A sleeping bag with its zip open, stretched around a suitcase. Knives poke out of the suitcase.]

An anaconda swallowing a caiman is like a sleeping bag swallowing a suitcase full of knives.

But here's the catch.

[The scene flips. The caiman holds a snake in its mouth. A tally board fills to "12".]

It goes both ways, and over seven years, Rivas's team found twelve dead anacondas, and the bite marks showed that caimans had killed at least eight of them, including every male. For a small male, the pool is full of things that eat him.

[A small anaconda wrapped around a caiman's back leg, flipping it upside down under the water. A clock spins through five hours.]

One small anaconda got a caiman's back leg in its coils and kept flipping it under the water for five hours. Five days later, the team found a dead male of the same size nearby, killed by a caiman bite.

[A researcher's bare foot steps on a log under the water plants. The log has eyes.]

The researchers waded through these swamps and often stepped on caimans hiding under the plants. They were so still that they were mistaken for logs, which is the anaconda's trick too.

[Doug stands beside the caiman with a little caiman-team flag. The anaconda and the caiman look at each other, then start wrestling again. Doug is left standing alone.]

The last crocodile Doug met ate him. This time, Doug sides with the caiman, and the two of them go back to fighting each other and forget he exists.

[Doug walks out of the pool. The cap rides away on the caiman's back.]

Doug survives, even though the narrator had the snake picked to win. There is one hunt left, and it starts in the dark.

Kept.

## The Biggest One

The Biggest One.

[An old poster, yellowed: "REWARD. LIVE SNAKE, 30 FEET." The amount crosses out "$1,000" and is rewritten "$50,000". A stamp: "NEW YORK ZOOLOGICAL SOCIETY".]

For decades, the New York Zoological Society offered a reward for any live, healthy snake longer than thirty feet, about nine meters. It began with a thousand dollars from Theodore Roosevelt in the early nineteen hundreds, and by nineteen eighty it was fifty thousand.

[A trophy cup with cobwebs on it. Label: "UNCLAIMED".]

Nobody ever tried to claim it.

[Two minivans parked bumper to bumper, with a tape measure along them reading "9 M"; the tape ends just short of the second van's back bumper. A single minivan beside them, labeled "5.21 M, THE REAL ONE".]

Thirty feet would be almost two minivans parked end to end. The longest anaconda Rivas caught, out of seven hundred and eighty, was just over five meters, about one minivan. In his thesis, he asked why he had never found one anywhere near nine meters, and suggested the really big ones near people may have been killed off long before.

Here's the honest part.

[A sober card, plain text on beige: "STRIKES ON 2 FIELD ASSISTANTS. KILLINGS OF PEOPLE: VERY FEW, HARD TO VERIFY."]

Rivas documented anacondas striking at two of his own field assistants as if they were prey, but reports of one actually killing a person are very few, and hard to verify.

[A night swamp under a thin moon. Black water. A pair of shoes left on the bank.]

To catch anacondas, Rivas's team waded barefoot through the swamp and found the submerged snakes by feeling for them with their feet.

[Doug, barefoot, wades in up to his waist, cap on, a handwritten sign under his arm: "PRIZE". He shuffles his feet, hopeful smile.]

Doug has come to claim the prize, so he shuffles his feet through the mud, and he finds something long that keeps going.

[The HUNT STAGE pointer drops to KEPT. A red stamp slams down: "KEPT". Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 329.]

Doug has died. Again. He may now be one of the very few that nobody can verify either.

[A smooth, still lump in the water at night. Nothing else moves.]

After a meal that size, an anaconda can go weeks or even months without eating. The narrator finds that more upsetting than the death.

[Dawn. Flat water. The red cap floats on two olive-sized bumps. The bumps sink slowly, and the cap stays on the surface. The field guide gets a yellow sticky note: "NO VENOM. NO NEED."]

The cap was not swallowed. She has standards.

[End slate. The field guide, HUNT STAGE: KEPT in red, DOUG DEATHS: 329.]

Doug deaths: three hundred and twenty-nine. Tell us in the comments which animal Doug should never be allowed to measure.
