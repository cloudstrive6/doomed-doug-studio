# Facts: 042 Every Reason the Green Anaconda Doesn't Need Venom (draft 2)

Every factual claim in script.md is listed here with its source. Checked 2026-11-09 by the script writer. Draft 2 applies script_review.md (draft 1 FAIL); changed rows are marked **(d2)**.

How each source was checked:
- **Fetched** means I read the passage myself (WebFetch, NCBI E-utilities, or PDF text extracted locally).
- **Search** means I confirmed it only from search-result text quoting or summarising the page.
- **Secondary** means a magazine, zoo or encyclopedia page, not the original study.

The backbone source is **Rivas, J. A. (2000), *The life history of the green anaconda (Eunectes murinus), with emphasis on its reproductive biology*, PhD dissertation, University of Tennessee.** I downloaded the full PDF from the university repository and searched the extracted text, so its rows are **Fetched** with page-level detail.

**FLAGGED (screener should look first):**
1. **The 2024 split is disputed (d2).** Wikipedia's *Eunectes akayima* page (Fetched) now lists akayima as a synonym of *E. murinus*, citing both Bionomina papers. The script no longer says "nobody noticed": the northern form was named *Boa gigas* by Latreille in 1801/1802 and long treated as the subspecies *E. murinus gigas* (Dirksen & Böhme 1998, *Salamandra* 34(4): 1-16, cited on the Wikipedia page). The kicker is now "scientists are still arguing about her name". Original flag: Rivas et al. 2024 (*Diversity* 16:127) is Fetched. Two papers in *Bionomina* 37(1), 2024, challenge it: Vásquez-Restrepo et al., pp. 1-7, on validity, and Dubois et al., pp. 8-58, on nomenclature. Both are Search only. So the script says the team "argued", and adds "Not every scientist accepts the split yet." Guinness World Records lists both species (Fetched).
2. **RESOLVED (d2).** The script now says reports of an anaconda actually killing a person are "very few, and hard to verify". Murphy & Crutchfield, *Giant Snakes: A Natural History* (2019), report four green anaconda attacks on people (1863, two in 1956, 2007) and say both 1956 attacks were fatal. My source for this is the African Snakebite Institute newsletter summary, Fetched: https://sendy.africansnakebiteinstitute.com/w/763TvJwMc1T4hrQHQjj2UrVA. Old flag, kept for the record: **"There has never been a documented case of a green anaconda killing a person."** The source is Discover Wildlife, 22 Oct 2026, Fetched, which gives that sentence word for word. It is consistent with Rivas 1999, Fetched, which documents two predatory strikes on his field assistants and calls them the first documented attacks. Neither source reports a death. This is a secondary source for a negative claim. If the screener wants a stronger one, the fallback is "Scientists have documented anacondas striking at people, but no scientific record of one killing a person." (Rivas 1999 only.)
3. **The New York Zoological Society reward.** Wikipedia's *List of largest snakes* is Fetched, and it cites Murphy & Henderson 1997, *Tales of Giant Snakes* (Krieger), pp. 2, 19, 37, 42, 55-56. That book is a scholarly history, but I did not read it myself. The script uses only what that passage gives: $1,000 from Theodore Roosevelt in the early 1900s, $50,000 by 1980, a live healthy snake over 30 ft (9.14 m), and "no attempt to claim the reward has ever been made". The script does not say whether the reward is still on offer, because one blog says it was withdrawn in 2002, which is unverified.
4. **The constriction and heartbeat findings come from boa constrictors, not anacondas.** The script says "a close relative, the boa constrictor" and "The study used boas". The National Geographic coverage of Boback 2015 quotes the team applying the finding to constrictors "which includes pythons and anacondas".
5. **"Larger females are courted by more males, and they give birth to more babies, and bigger ones."** Rivas 2000 abstract: "larger females are courted by a higher number of males"; "Larger females produce large clutches of large individuals". Fetched.

## Cut or changed from the brief
- **Jaguars killing anacondas (item 8):** cut. The only records I found were yellow anacondas in the Pantanal (photos, news) and one genus-level *Eunectes* sp. kill in Perilli et al. 2016 (PLoS ONE, southern Pantanal, where yellow anacondas live). Discover Wildlife says jaguars can kill grown green anacondas, but it cites no record. Caimans carry the twist instead, from Rivas et al. 1999.
- **Fawcett and other explorer claims:** cut. I didn't fetch a strong source, and the reward story and Rivas's "why have I never found one near 9 m" already make the point that giant claims aren't verified.
- **A number for teeth:** "six rows" (SF Zoo) is used. No count of individual teeth.
- **"Mostly at night":** not claimed. The night swamp in item 9 is staging.
- **Brief's "breeding ball of up to about a dozen males, days to weeks":** replaced with Rivas's measured figures: 1 to 13 males, 18 days on average, 46 days at most.
- **Constriction pressure studies (Moon & Mehta, Penning et al.):** not used. Boback 2015 carries the item.
- **The 97.5 kg record is not called a world record.** It is the heaviest wild female in one reliable field study. Guinness's 300+ kg is a species-level estimate from unweighed specimens, so the script doesn't use it.
- **"Average female 4.7 times heavier"** is Rivas's sentence. Table 3-1 means (20.3 vs 5.9 kg, all ages) give 3.4.
