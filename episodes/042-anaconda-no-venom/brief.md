## 2. Escalation axis and items
**Axis: severity, meaning how far one anaconda hunt has gone.** Doug moves through a single hunt from being watched to being kept.
Spoken bands, each said once the first time we enter it: **"Watched." / "Bitten." / "Squeezed." / "Swallowed." / "Kept."**
**Episode device:** a **HUNT STAGE** sign. It's a crude wooden swamp-marker post beside the death counter, and a red pointer moves down it
(WATCHED, BITTEN, SQUEEZED, SWALLOWED, KEPT). It only moves down. KEPT is stamped red at the boss. It's staging only and never read aloud. The
field guide gets a **GREEN ANACONDA** page: "NO" at the first death, "MAYBE" pencilled at The Breeding Ball, rubbed out at The Coils, and a
closing sticky note "NO VENOM. NO NEED."

Deaths go **323 to 329** (7 deaths; items 3 and 8 are survivals). Nine items, the most extreme last.

### Watched.
**1. The Female** (one of the three strongest: the record holder, plus the 2024 twist)
- Facts to research: the green anaconda (*Eunectes murinus*) is the heaviest snake on Earth. Mass of large females (find a reliably weighed
  maximum from a field study such as Rivas's Venezuelan Llanos work; never a hearsay record). Females are far bigger than males, among the most
  extreme size differences in land vertebrates (Rivas & Burghardt 2001, *Animal Behaviour*). **2024: the northern green anaconda (*Eunectes
  akayima*) was described as a separate species** from genetics (Rivas et al. 2024, *Diversity*). State the genetic difference only as the paper
  gives it.
- **Twist (by 0:45):** the giant everyone pictures is always a female, and until 2024 nobody knew there were two of her.
- Scale analogy: a big female weighs about as much as a grown man (check the source number), folded into something the width of a car tyre.
- Doug beat: Doug arrives with a tape measure "to confirm the record". The record confirms Doug. Death 323. "Doug did not agree to this." here,
  once.

**2. The Nostrils**
- Facts to research: eyes and nostrils on top of the head, so it waits almost fully submerged. How long it can stay underwater (sourced
  figure only). Ambush hunting at the water's edge, mostly at night or in the dry season when prey crowds the pools (Llanos field work).
- Scale analogy: all you see is two bumps the size of a pair of olives, on a log that isn't a log.
- Doug beat: Doug steps onto "the log" to cross the pool. Death 324, a cut to black at the splash.

**3. The Breeding Ball** (survival)
- Facts to research: breeding aggregations of up to about a dozen males wrapped around one female, lasting days to weeks (Rivas & Burghardt).
  Females have been recorded eating a male (cannibalism, Rivas 1999 or Rivas & Owens), one plain clause, no description. Why bigger females win:
  more and larger babies.
- Scale analogy: the breeding ball is a knot of garden hoses the size of a bathtub, and every hose is trying to be first.
- Doug beat: Doug wades in to "break it up". Every snake is busy. **Survival: "Doug is fine."** The narrator notes that this is the only part of
  the hunt where Doug is not the point.

### Bitten.
**4. The Teeth**
- Facts to research: no venom glands. Many small, backward-curved teeth that anchor the bite (find a number from a herpetology source, or say
  "rows of"). The strike from the water and the instant coil. The bite only holds; it doesn't kill.
- Scale analogy: the teeth work like a fish hook, easy in and impossible to back out.
- Doug beat: Doug brings antivenom "just in case". There is nothing for it to cure. Death 325. Callback to 010 (one line: "The last time Doug
  met a snake, the antivenom was the point.").

### Squeezed.
**5. The Coils**
- Facts to research: **constriction kills by stopping blood flow, not by suffocation** (Boback et al. 2015, *Journal of Experimental Biology*,
  on boa constrictors; say "in a close relative" when applying it to anacondas). How fast circulation fails in that study. Constriction pressure
  studies (Moon & Mehta, or Penning et al., cite only what's measured).
- **Twist:** the prey doesn't die from running out of air. Its blood stops moving first.
- Scale analogy: being constricted is like a blood-pressure cuff that keeps inflating after it beeps.
- Doug beat: Doug takes a deep breath "to wait it out". That isn't the problem. Death 326, played flat. Field guide "MAYBE" rubbed out.

**6. The Heartbeat**
- Facts to research: constrictors feel the prey's heartbeat and keep squeezing until it stops (Boback et al. 2012, *Biology Letters*, boas
  with simulated hearts). And the snake keeps breathing while it squeezes by moving which part of its ribs it uses (Capano et al. 2022, *Journal
  of Experimental Biology*, boas).
- Scale analogy: the snake is a smartwatch that only switches off when your pulse does.
- Doug beat: Doug tries to calm his heart down "to fool it". It counts. Death 327. No "plays dead" framing, nothing that reads as advice.

### Swallowed.
**7. The Jaws**
- Facts to research: the lower jaw halves are not fused at the chin and are joined by stretchy tissue, and the snake "walks" its jaws over the
  prey. It breathes during a long swallow by pushing its windpipe forward (glottis). "Dislocates its jaw" is a myth: say so in one line.
  Documented prey: capybaras, deer, birds, fish (cite a diet study, e.g. Thomas & Rivas or Rivas's thesis).
- Scale analogy: swallowing a capybara is like a garden hose swallowing a beach ball, slowly, without a straw.
- Doug beat: **costume gag (once): capybara suit**, played straight, cap on top. "hi friend?" once, to the snake. Death 328, cut to black on the
  jaws opening; the next shot is a lump with the cap balanced on it. No outline of Doug in the lump.

**8. The Caiman** (survival, the honest twist)
- Facts to research: anacondas eat spectacled caimans (cite a documented predation record or diet study). It runs both ways: caimans and jaguars
  also kill anacondas (cite jaguar predation on anacondas, e.g. a Pantanal/Llanos record). One callback to 027 (the croc), one line.
- **Twist:** the anaconda is not the top of the food chain here. It eats crocodilians, and the crocodilians and jaguars eat it right back.
- Scale analogy: an anaconda swallowing a caiman is like a sleeping bag swallowing a suitcase full of knives.
- Doug beat: Doug "sides with the caiman". The two of them go back to fighting each other and forget him. **Survival.** The narrator is quietly
  disappointed (no "Doug is fine." here, it's used at item 3).

### Kept.
**9. The Biggest One** (the payoff promise, the boss)
- Facts to research: after a big meal an anaconda can go weeks to months without eating (sourced figure). The Theodore Roosevelt / New York
  Zoological Society (now WCS) prize for a live snake over 30 feet (about 9 m), raised over the decades and **never claimed** (verify the
  amounts and years from WCS or a reliable history; say "never collected" only if the source says so). Explorer giant-snake claims (e.g. Fawcett's)
  as unverified, in one line. The longest reliably measured green anaconda (field-study figure only).
- **Honesty line:** there are almost no verified cases of a green anaconda killing a person, and none that were documented by scientists (verify
  the exact wording; if a reliable source records a case, say "very few" instead). Doug is about to be the exception nobody can verify either.
- Scale analogy: the 30-foot snake the prize asks for would be longer than a school bus. The real record holder is closer to a minivan (check).
- Doug beat: Doug wades barefoot into the night swamp to "claim the prize". (Field researchers really do find anacondas by wading and feeling
  for them with their feet: verify from Rivas's methods before using; cut the line if unsourced.) He finds her. The red KEPT stamp lands.
  Death 329. The narrator says the snake will not need to eat again for a long time and seems to find that more upsetting than the death.
- Closing: dawn, flat water, the cap floating on two olive-sized bumps that sink slowly. Kicker: "The cap was not swallowed. She has standards."
  Outro ≤30 words: death counter plus one CTA.

## 7. Risks
- **Gore:** every strike and coil is a cut to black or a cartoon wrap with X eyes. Swallowing is shown only as a smooth lump with the cap on top:
  no Doug outline, no bones, no digestion detail. Cannibalism is one plain clause. No crushing sounds described.
- **Safety advice (never approve):** no "what to do if a snake grabs you", no play-dead or pry-the-head guidance, nothing that reads as
  "anacondas are harmless" or "anacondas hunt people". The boss honesty line is about records only.
- **Kid appeal (medium: snakes and capybaras are popular kids' topics):** no cute capybara, no big-eyed snake, no Jungle Book / Kaa tone, no
  hypnotic-eyes gag, no baby snakes in the thumbnail. The capybara costume stays deadpan (no mascot voice). Jokes stay dry and adult-coded.
- **Myths to debunk, not repeat:** "dislocates its jaw" (it doesn't), "suffocates its prey" (circulation, Boback 2015), giant-snake explorer
  claims (unverified). The 1997 film is not referenced.
- **Facts that need strong sources:**
  - Mass and length of large females from a field study (Rivas; never a tabloid record).
  - Rivas & Burghardt 2001 (size dimorphism); breeding-ball size and duration; female cannibalism of males.
  - Rivas et al. 2024, *Diversity* (*Eunectes akayima*), wording exactly as published.
  - Boback et al. 2012 (heartbeat) and 2015 (circulatory arrest), Capano et al. 2022 (rib breathing), all on boas: say "a close relative".
  - Diet records: capybara, deer, caiman. Jaguar and caiman predation on anacondas.
  - The Roosevelt / NYZS / WCS 30-foot prize: amounts, years, and that it was never claimed.
  - Human-fatality wording for the honesty line.
  - Submergence time and fasting time after a large meal.
- **Pronunciation (editor):** *Eunectes murinus* "yoo-NEK-teez myoo-RYE-nus", *akayima* "ah-kah-YEE-mah" (check the paper), capybara "kap-ee-BAR-ah",
