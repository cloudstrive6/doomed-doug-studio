## Ashfall

Ashfall.

[Cross-section: the shaft begins in tan topsoil. The time machine waits at the bottom. DEPTH: 12 MILLION YEARS.]

[A sunny waterhole. Short-legged, barrel-bodied rhinos stand in the water like hippos.]

They were called Teleoceras, with short legs, a barrel of a body, and a lifestyle remarkably similar to a hippo's. Enormous herds of them came to this water to drink and wallow, along with horses, camels and birds.

The problem is that the volcano that buried them wasn't anywhere near Nebraska.

[Map: a dotted line from southwestern Idaho to northeastern Nebraska. Label: "1,450 KM".]

It was in southwestern Idaho, nearly fourteen hundred and fifty kilometers to the west, on the same hotspot that sits under Yellowstone today. The eruption threw so much ash into the sky that it drifted east and fell like grey snow two states away.

Across the region, the ash settled about thirty centimeters deep. At the waterhole, the wind kept piling it into the low ground until it was nearly three meters deep, which is about the height of a basketball hoop.

[A basketball hoop next to the waterhole. The ash line reaches the rim.]

Being the biggest animal at the waterhole only meant waiting the longest.

## Wasps Inside Fossil Flies

Wasps Inside Fossil Flies.

[The shaft deepens into ochre rock. DEPTH: 34 MILLION YEARS.]

In the late eighteen hundreds, phosphate miners in the Quercy region of southern France kept digging up thousands of tiny fossil fly pupae.

[A pile of small brown capsules on a workbench. Label: "3 MM".]

A pupa is the stage where a fly rebuilds itself inside a hard little case, and these ones had turned to stone somewhere between thirty-four and forty million years ago. Each was about three millimeters long, less than half the length of a grain of long-grain rice.

From the outside, they looked like exactly what they were, which was flies.

[A rice grain beside one pupa for scale.]

In twenty eighteen, a team of German researchers put fifteen hundred and ten of them through a synchrotron, an X-ray source powerful enough to see inside the stone without breaking it open.

Here's what the scan found. In fifty-five of those pupae, there was no fly left at all.

[X-ray glow: the pupa outline, and inside it the silhouette of a wasp, folded up. Red "!".]

There was a wasp instead, a parasitoid wasp. A female laid her egg inside the developing fly, and the young wasp grew up by eating its host from the inside until only the case was left. Most of them were preserved as adults, still sitting in the case.

The team described four new species, and the most common one is named Xenomorphia resurrecta, after the creature in the Alien films that develops inside its host the same way.

[Doug zipped into a fly-pupa costume, red cap on top, standing very still among the pupae.]

These wasps hunted fly pupae, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as one, and he has been told to hold still and think like a pupa.

[X-ray glow over Doug's costume. Cut on the glow. The costume goes flat, X eyes on the front. DOUG DEATHS: 123.]

For forty million years, they looked like flies, and it took an X-ray to see who had actually won.

## Lunch Inside Lunch

Lunch Inside Lunch.

[Deeper still, ochre turning red-brown. DEPTH: 48 MILLION YEARS.]

Forty-eight million years ago, a volcanic lake sat where the Messel Pit in Germany is today. One day, a young snake swallowed a lizard, and some time later the snake died with the lizard still inside it.

[Slab: a coiled fossil snake. A red arrow points at its belly.]

That alone would make a good fossil, since the snake is a young Palaeopython, a distant relative of today's boas and pythons. The lizard in its stomach is an early relative of the basilisks, and it went in head first. Its body, not counting the tail, was about eight centimeters long, a little shorter than the long side of a credit card.

Then researchers ran a detailed X-ray scan, and the strange part is how far down it goes.

[Scan view: the snake outline, the lizard inside it, and a tiny insect shape inside the lizard. Three labels: "SNAKE", "LIZARD", "INSECT".]

Inside the lizard was an insect, too far gone to identify but definitely somebody's last meal.

The deeper they looked, the more lunch there was.

It's a set of Russian dolls, except that every doll ate the one inside it. This is only the second fossil ever found with three levels of a food chain stacked inside each other. The first was a shark that had eaten an amphibian, which had eaten a spiny fish, more than two hundred and fifty million years ago.

[Doug arrives at the lakeshore with a sandwich, standing next to the snake. The snake ignores him.]

Doug arrived holding a sandwich, and the snake looked at him, looked at the sandwich, and went back to digesting the lizard.

## Sue's Jaw

Sue's Jaw.

[The shaft passes a thin pale stripe of clay. DEPTH: 67 MILLION YEARS.]

Sue is the most complete Tyrannosaurus rex ever found. She lived about sixty-seven million years ago in what is now South Dakota, and made it to roughly thirty-three.

[Doug's shovel scrapes the thin clay stripe. A red cap, turned to stone, pokes out. Doug looks at it, then puts it in his pocket.]

Doug's shovel goes through the thin band of clay that marks the end of the age of dinosaurs. In it is a red cap. His. From a previous trip. He puts it in his pocket and keeps digging.

[The Field Museum: Sue's skull in a glass case. Label: "1.5 M".]

Sue's skull is about one and a half meters long, and it's too heavy to sit on her mounted skeleton, so it has its own case. On the back half of her lower jaw are holes, some of them as wide as a golf ball.

[Red circles around the holes. A golf ball beside one for scale.]

For years, paleontologists interpreted them as bite marks from another tyrannosaur, until a team in two thousand nine proposed something much smaller. They said the holes looked like the damage caused by a single-celled parasite called Trichomonas.

Pigeons often carry it with few ill effects, but when hawks and falcons catch it from them, it can eat holes in their jaws. The team suggested it could have made eating so hard that Sue starved.

[Sue's silhouette next to a pigeon. Red "?".]

Which means the most famous predator on Earth may have had a pigeon disease.

Here's the catch: in twenty twenty-two, another team looked again and argued that the holes don't fit that infection. The bone around them had started to heal, so whatever made them didn't kill her, and what made them is still unknown.

So is what killed Sue.

[Doug in a white coat with a stethoscope, holding up a breath mint toward a huge, detailed, serious-looking T. rex. He leans in to check its mouth.]

Doug volunteered as her veterinarian, brought a stethoscope and a breath mint, and asked her to open wide.

[Cut away to the top of the shaft. A dull thud from below. A dust cloud puffs up. The cap floats slowly down. DOUG DEATHS: 124.]

The holes are still a mystery. Doug's case, unfortunately, is closed.

## The Snake in the Nest

The Snake in the Nest.

[A nest of large round eggs in red-brown rock. DEPTH: 68 MILLION YEARS.]

In nineteen eighty-four, a geologist named Dhananjay Mohabey was collecting dinosaur eggs in Gujarat, in western India, and wrapped around one crushed egg were some strange bones that didn't belong to a dinosaur.

[Slab: a coiled snake around a crushed egg. Two whole eggs beside it.]

A colleague recognized that they belonged to a snake a few years later, but the discovery went almost entirely unnoticed. In two thousand one, a visiting paleontologist looked at the slab again, and in twenty ten the snake finally got a name, Sanajeh.

Its rock lies under the Deccan lava flows, from the very end of the age of dinosaurs, around Sue's time.

Sanajeh was about three and a half meters long, roughly two tall adults lying head to toe, but the sauropod eggs were sixteen centimeters across, too big for it to swallow whole.

[Diagram: the snake's open jaw next to an egg. Red X.]

So it probably wasn't there for the eggs themselves, because next to it lay the remains of a hatchling about half a meter long. Other snakes have been found with eggs at the same site, so the scientists think these snakes regularly visited nesting grounds and waited for whatever came out.

The disturbing part is how this scene ends, because the nest sat beside a slope, and a storm sent a sudden flow of mud and sand down it.

It was waiting at the nest. Then the hill came down on both of them.

Two of the eggs never hatched, the snake never got its meal, and the mud got everybody.

## The Dinosaur Biter

The Dinosaur Biter.

[Grey-brown volcanic layers. DEPTH: 125 MILLION YEARS.]

For a long time, the mammals of the dinosaur age were imagined as small, nocturnal, and mostly on the menu.

The Lujiatun beds disagree. About one hundred and twenty-five million years ago, in what is now northeastern China, volcanic mudflows buried animals here so quickly that the place has been called China's dinosaur Pompeii.

[Slab: a mammal skeleton with tiny bones in its ribcage. Red arrow.]

In two thousand five, scientists reported a mammal from these beds called Repenomamus with something in its stomach: the bones of a young Psittacosaurus, a beaked, plant-eating dinosaur, swallowed in chunks.

Then another fossil was dug up there in twenty twelve, and in twenty twenty-three, another team described it. It shows the moment itself.

[Slab: the mammal on top of the larger dinosaur, jaws on its ribcage, paw on its jaw, foot gripping its leg. Label: "3X".]

A Repenomamus is lying on top of an adult Psittacosaurus about the size of a large dog, even though the mammal was only about a third of its size. One paw is clutching the dinosaur's lower jaw, a back foot grips its hind leg, and its teeth are clamped on the ribs.

That flips the old story. We were told that mammals hid from dinosaurs, but this one was eating them, at least some of the time.

Not every expert is convinced it was a hunt, since the dinosaur may already have been dying, but either way, a volcanic mudflow came down and buried them both mid-struggle.

[Doug between them in a striped referee shirt, whistle in his mouth, arm raised.]

Doug has seen a fight like this before, so this time he brought a whistle and a striped shirt, and he intends to keep it clean.

[A grey wall of volcanic mud sweeps across. The whistle pops up out of the mud, then the cap. DOUG DEATHS: 126.]

Neither one won, and the volcano called the match.

## The Fish That Held On

The Fish That Held On.

[Slate-grey layers begin. A pale blue lagoon. DEPTH: 150 MILLION YEARS.]

About one hundred and fifty million years ago, southern Germany was a string of warm, shallow lagoons. Near the bottom, the water was salty and low in oxygen, so creatures that sank down there didn't decompose.

That's why the Solnhofen limestone is famous, with fossils so clear that you can see the outline of a wing.

[A pterosaur skims low over the water with a small fish in its beak.]

A pterosaur called Rhamphorhynchus is skimming the surface, fishing. It catches a small fish and swallows it, and the fish is still sitting in its throat.

[A long, sharp-snouted fish rises under it.]

Under the surface is a much bigger fish called Aspidorhynchus, which grew up to about a meter long, roughly the length of a baseball bat. It lunges up and bites the pterosaur's wing.

[Close-up: rows of teeth tangled in the wing membrane. Red arrow. Label: "STUCK".]

Here's the irony: it grabbed the thin skin of the wing instead of the bone, and the fibers in that skin jammed between its tightly packed teeth.

The fish couldn't let go.

The researchers think it thrashed, sank into the oxygen-poor water near the bottom, and suffocated. The pterosaur drowned with its own catch still in its throat, and the two of them settled on the lagoon floor together. Researchers have identified five slabs with a pterosaur lying right beside the snout of one of these fish.

The fish won. It just couldn't stop winning.

[Doug on a rock at the lagoon's edge with a fishing rod, hopeful smile.]

Doug spent the whole morning fishing from a rock, catching nothing, and at long last, he felt a bite.

Something big was on the other end.

[The line goes tight: Doug's hook is in the big fish, the big fish is stuck to the pterosaur, the pterosaur has the small fish. All four go under. The cap floats alone on the lagoon. DOUG DEATHS: 127.]

It is the only fishing story in history where everyone involved got caught.

## Tanystropheus

Tanystropheus.

[Dark slate rock. A shallow, sunlit sea above. DEPTH: 242 MILLION YEARS.]

Two hundred and forty-two million years ago, in a shallow sea on what is now the border of Switzerland and Italy, lived a reptile that was mostly neck.

Today, that seabed is a mountain called Monte San Giorgio, a World Heritage Site because its rock holds the best known record of sea life from that time.

[A long reptile swims past. Its neck stretches across the whole frame. Label: "3 M".]

The big species was about six meters long, and roughly half of that was neck. That's three meters of neck, built from just thirteen extremely elongated vertebrae, which is close to twice the length of a giraffe's neck.

[A giraffe's neck beside it for scale.]

The neck was three times as long as its body, and it hunted fish and squid with nostrils on top of its snout like a crocodile's and long curved teeth for slippery prey.

In twenty twenty-three, two scientists in Stuttgart took a closer look at two fossils, one from each species of Tanystropheus.

[A rock slab: a skull and a long line of neck bones, and nothing else. Red "?".]

The unsettling part is what's missing. Both fossils are only a head and a neck, nothing else was ever found, and where the neck stops, there are tooth marks. The researchers think each animal was caught by the neck, probably in a single bite. For the big one, one of the main suspects is another marine reptile called Nothosaurus.

The neck was the best thing about it. It was also the only thing left.

[Doug in a snorkel next to the long-necked reptile, giving a thumbs up. Speech bubble: "nice neck".]

## The Shark's Tapeworms

The Shark's Tapeworms.

[Rust-brown rock with grey bands. A freshwater pond. DEPTH: 270 MILLION YEARS.]

About two hundred and seventy million years ago, in what is now southern Brazil, a shark was swimming around a freshwater pond.

We don't have the shark, but we do have something it left behind.

[A small spiral-shaped stone. Label: "COPROLITE". Label: "5 CM".]

This is a coprolite, a piece of fossil dung, about five centimeters long and twisted into a spiral, a shape that points straight to a shark. Inside it were bits of fish scale and bone, which is exactly what a shark would leave behind. Around five hundred of them have turned up at this one site, and scientists cut fourteen into thin slices to look inside.

One of them held a surprise.

[Microscope circle: a cluster of small oval shapes.]

Under the microscope, they found a cluster of ninety-three tiny oval eggs, all packed into a patch four millimeters long, each one about the size of a fine grain of sand. They were tapeworm eggs, and one of them had a larva already starting to develop inside.

It's the oldest record of parasite eggs ever found in a vertebrate's fossil dung, and the eggs resemble those of a group of tapeworms with hundreds of species still living in sharks. Their favorite spot is a twisting part of the gut called the spiral valve, and in sharks today it's usually full of them.

Here's the real horror. The scariest thing in this rock isn't the shark. It's what was living inside the shark.

[Doug waist-deep in the pond, waving at a large shark fin. Speech bubble: "hi friend?".]

Doug waded in to say hello, and in his defense, the shark looked lonely, and he had no idea it already had company.

[The fin turns toward Doug. Splash. The cap bobs on the water. Ghost Doug rises. DOUG DEATHS: 129.]

Two hundred and seventy million years later, those tapeworms' relatives are still living inside sharks.

## Tongue Worms

Tongue Worms.

[Near-black rock with grey nodules. DEPTH: 425 MILLION YEARS.]

Four hundred and twenty-five million years ago, a volcano dropped ash into a shallow sea over what is now Herefordshire, in England. The ash preserved delicate, soft-bodied animals inside hard little lumps of rock, in astonishing detail.

[A cracked-open stone nodule. Inside, a tiny shape.]

One of those animals is an ostracod, a tiny crustacean that lives inside a little two-part shell.

Attached to it are parasites called tongue worms, and this species is named Invavita piratica, which roughly means ancient intruder, with a nod to pirates.

[Magnified: the ostracod shell, with two slender worm shapes clinging to it.]

They're between one and four millimeters long, so the biggest one is still shorter than a grain of long-grain rice. Some are clamped to the outside of the shell, and others are tucked inside it, next to the host's eggs. They held on for four hundred and twenty-five million years.

Today, there are more than a hundred species of tongue worm, and most of them live in the airways of land animals, especially reptiles.

Here's the twist: these fossils are trapped in rock that's very difficult to scan, so the team had to grind the specimen away one thin layer at a time, photographing every new surface.

[A grinder removes a layer. A photo is taken. Another layer. The nodule shrinks. A 3D model builds up on a screen beside it.]

To see it, they had to destroy it.

The photos were then stacked on a computer into a three-dimensional model of the parasite and its host.

[Doug with a giant magnifying glass, leaning over the ostracod. He holds the ostracod up next to himself. He is much, much bigger.]

Doug inspected the host carefully with a magnifying glass, but he is far too big to qualify.

[Doug unharmed, cap on, giving a thumbs up. DOUG DEATHS: stays at 129.]

Doug is fine. The narrator read the paper twice, looking for a loophole.

The original fossil no longer exists, and the worm that never let go only exists as a model.

## The First Hole

The First Hole.

[The bottom of the shaft. Near-black rock with a faint green glow. The time machine opens onto a calm, green, empty sea. DEPTH: 550 MILLION YEARS.]

About five hundred and fifty million years ago, the sea floor in what is now China was quiet. There were no fish, no sharks, and nothing with jaws.

[A sandy sea floor dotted with little tubes standing upright.]

Most animals here were soft, flat and slow, and one of the exceptions was Cloudina.

[Close-up of one tube: a stack of small cones, one inside the next.]

Cloudina built a tube out of stacked cones, like a pile of paper cups, and lived in the cone on top. It's one of the first animals known to make a hard, mineral skeleton. Each cone was only a few millimeters wide, and in some places they grew together into the oldest known reefs built by animals.

[Doug steps out of the time machine and breathes in, hopeful smile.]

Doug was told this was the safest place he had ever been, and for a while, it was.

In nineteen ninety-two, two researchers looked at more than five hundred Cloudina tubes from China, and a few of them had tiny round holes drilled straight through the shell.

[Close-up: a neat round hole in a tube. Red circle.]

A later study found holes in almost one in five shells, and some of them were narrower than a human hair. The bigger the tube, the bigger the hole, which suggests that something was selecting its prey by size. Another kind of shell living right next to them was left alone, and the driller itself has never been identified.

[A human hair laid across one hole for scale.]

The researchers call these holes the oldest known evidence of one animal preying on another, and some think this pressure helped push animals toward armor, shells and everything else that came after.

That's what this whole dig has been leading to. The scariest fossil at the bottom of the shaft isn't a monster. It's a hole. It's the oldest known moment of one animal eating another, and everything in this video started there.

[Doug in a stacked-cone Cloudina costume, red cap on top, standing in a row of real tubes, perfectly still.]

The driller went after Cloudina and left its neighbours alone, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as one. He stood very still and tried his best to think like a cone.

[A tiny dot appears on Doug's costume. Then a neat round hole. Doug's eyes become X's. The costume stays upright. DOUG DEATHS: 130.]

He did very well. Something noticed.

[Back at the surface. The shaft drops out of frame below. The shovel stands upright in the dirt with two red caps on its handle: today's, and the stone one from the boundary clay. DEPTH: 550 MILLION YEARS.]

Five hundred and fifty million years of digging, and the only two things that came back up were caps.
