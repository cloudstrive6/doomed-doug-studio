# Why the 400-Year-Old Greenland Shark Is the Creepiest Fish Alive (draft 3)

[Opening frame: the thumbnail. A huge grey-brown Greenland shark face under a ceiling of sea ice, a small dot eye with a pale cartoon copepod streamer dangling from it. Above, through a hole in the ice, Doug in his cap holds a birthday cake crowded with candles and peers down. DOUG DEATHS: 414. SHARK AGE: ?]

Some sharks under the Arctic ice right now are likely older than the United States.

By the end, Doug lowers a birthday cake through the ice for a shark that's about four hundred years old.

## The Eye

The Eye. Many Greenland sharks swim around with a small crustacean hanging off the front of one eyeball.

[Close on the shark's eye, a simple dot. A pale streamer hangs from it. Red arrow, label "PARASITE".]

It's a parasitic copepod, a distant relative of shrimp about three centimeters long, and the female grips the clear surface of the eye, anchors herself into it, and stays there.

Researchers who studied infected eyes from the Canadian Arctic found scarring at every anchor point, and they warned that it could badly damage the shark's vision. So for decades, the Greenland shark was often called blind.

Here's the twist: in twenty twenty-six, a team led from the University of California, Irvine published a close look inside eyes from sharks caught off western Greenland. The light-sensing layer at the back showed no signs of wearing out, even in sharks estimated at more than a hundred years old.

[SHARK AGE: ? A red "?" stamped over the word "BLIND".]

The parasites didn't seem to stop the eyes working. Its retina is built for dim light and is tuned to the blue that reaches the deep, so a shark with a passenger on its eyeball still gets around.

That's like letting a stranger sit on your windscreen for years and driving to work anyway.

[Doug under the ice with a torch. He waves at the copepod. Speech bubble: "hi friend?" The copepod waves back.]

Doug has found the only thing down here that waves back.

[Behind Doug, the whole shark turns. Very slowly. Cut away.]

Doug has died. Again.

Nobody knows how old that shark was, but the next one is exactly zero.

Year zero.

## The Pup

The Pup. Almost everything known about how a Greenland shark is born comes from one pregnant female, reported in nineteen fifty-seven.

[SHARK AGE: 0. A crude yellowed scientific paper with "1957" in the corner.]

She was about five meters long, and inside her were ten pups close to birth, each about thirty-seven centimeters. A twenty twenty review of the species' breeding called her the only confirmed pregnant Greenland shark in the scientific record.

It's like trying to understand how humans are born from one hospital note written in nineteen fifty-seven.

The strange part is how little has been added since. Scientists estimate that a newborn is somewhere between thirty-five and forty-five centimeters long, based on the size of the eggs, but that's a calculation, not a sighting.

[A tape measure, with "35 TO 45 CM?" pencilled on it.]

Even that one record is shaky, because the reviewers suspect she may have lost some pups when she was caught, so ten might not be the real number. Nobody knows how long the pregnancy lasts. The same review listed finding the places where pups are born and raised as work that still needs doing.

So somewhere in the Arctic, the longest-living vertebrate known starts its life in a nursery that nobody has ever found.

[A small grey pup, plain dot eyes, nothing cute about it. Doug holds a "WELCOME" banner.]

Doug has made a banner, and it took him most of the afternoon.

[The pup swims past without slowing.]

Doug is fine. The pup has added him to a list of things to think about in a century or so.

And that pup is about to start the slowest growth spurt in the ocean.

Decades.

## The Growth

The Growth. A Greenland shark grows less than one centimeter a year.

[SHARK AGE: ~10. A young shark beside a ruler. Label "LESS THAN 1 CM A YEAR".]

That number goes back to a medium-sized shark tagged off Greenland in the nineteen thirties. When it was caught again sixteen years later, it had grown by only a few centimeters in total. It would take a completely different method, decades later, to work out how old these sharks actually get.

Here's the problem: your fingernails grow about four centimeters a year, which is more than four times faster than this shark.

[Split screen: a fingernail with a dotted line racing ahead of a shark with a dotted line barely moving.]

Now put that pace next to its size. A big Greenland shark can be five meters long. At less than a centimeter a year, a body that size is not built in a decade, or even in a century.

This isn't a sick or hungry shark, since everything about this animal runs slowly, and the water it lives in is a big part of the reason. Greenland sharks live in seas that can be as cold as minus two degrees Celsius, and in water that cold, there is no rush to do anything.

[Doug at a hole in the ice, clipboard in one hand, tape measure trailing into the water.]

Doug has volunteered to measure a ten-year-old shark for one full year, so that he can see the growth for himself.

[Frost-white flat Doug on the ice. The cap sits on the tape measure.]

Doug has died. Again.

The shark grew less than a centimeter that year, which was still more than Doug did. And its growth is quick compared with the way it swims.

## The Slowest Shark

The Slowest Shark. When scientists tagged six Greenland sharks, their usual cruising speed came out at about one point two kilometers an hour.

[SHARK AGE: ~50. A shark beside a snail on a racetrack. The snail is not far behind.]

That's less than a third of a normal walking pace. One full sweep of the tail can take about seven seconds, and even its fastest burst in that study stayed under three kilometers an hour. For a fish of its size, nothing measured has been slower, and the researchers who tagged it pointed to the cold water it lives in as the likely reason.

But that's not the creepy part. Seals turn up in its stomach.

In sharks caught around Svalbard, seal was found in more than a third of the stomachs that had anything in them, mostly ringed seals, and some of them were adults. In Greenland waters, one shark was found with two whole harp seals inside it, fresh, with no sign that anything else had been feeding on them first.

[A red "?" between the slow shark and a fast seal.]

Seals are fast, agile swimmers, and this shark loses a race to a brisk walk. Nobody knows for sure how it catches them. One idea from the team that measured its speed is that it sneaks up on seals while they're asleep in the water.

So the Greenland shark doesn't chase. It waits for things to end up in the water.

[Doug, in a seal costume with the cap on top, curled up asleep at the edge of an ice floe.]

Doug has heard that seals nap in the water, and Doug is excellent at napping.

[The floe tips. Splash. Cut away.]

Doug has died. Again.

Seals, though, are just what it catches, and some of its meals never lived in the sea at all.

A century.

## The Menu

The Menu. One Greenland shark was reportedly found with a whole reindeer inside it.

[SHARK AGE: ~100. A crude list card titled "FOUND INSIDE". Silhouettes only.]

Other reports mention parts of a horse, and one scientific survey of their stomachs found the remains of a polar bear. These are land animals, and this shark lives in the sea, often hundreds of meters down in near-freezing water.

Nobody has shown it hunting any of them, but it's a known scavenger that eats whatever comes down to meet it, including leftover whale meat that whalers near Svalbard threw back.

It's also very good at finding whatever comes down: in one Canadian Arctic study, researchers lowered squid-baited cameras into deep water, and Greenland sharks showed up at eight out of every ten. The team told more than a hundred and forty of them apart by the marks on their skin.

[Map of the Canadian Arctic, camera icons, a shark fin next to most of them.]

The disturbing part is the order of events. Something on the ice falls in, and the shark is already there.

It's the Arctic's lost property office, except that everything handed in gets eaten.

[The list card gets a new line in red pencil: "PICNIC".]

[Doug on the sea ice with a picnic blanket, a flask and sandwiches. He smiles at the view.]

Doug has packed a picnic, and he is sitting on the ice to eat it, which is sitting on the sea.

[Crack. Cut to: a neat hole in the ice. The red cap sits on the edge.]

Doug has died. Again.

The shark wasn't involved. It was only the next step.

Next, somebody tries eating the shark instead. The shark has a plan for that too.

## The Meat

The Meat. Fresh Greenland shark is poisonous to eat.

[SHARK AGE: ~100. A slab of grey meat under a yellow warning triangle.]

Its flesh is loaded with urea and a chemical called trimethylamine oxide, and eaten fresh, it's considered hazardous.

In Iceland, people found a way around it: they ferment the meat first, then hang it up to dry, and the result is called hákarl, which is still eaten there today.

[A drying shed with strips of meat hanging inside. Label "HÁKARL".]

Here's the interesting part: in one study by Icelandic food scientists, the meat was fermented for about eight weeks and then dried for five more. Within five weeks of fermenting, the problem chemical had dropped below the level their tests could detect.

It's a sandwich you have to leave alone for about three months before anyone will eat it, which is the exact opposite of how sandwiches work.

Iceland isn't hunting these sharks for it, either. A twenty fourteen Icelandic government release said that almost all the Greenland sharks landed there are caught by accident, by boats fishing for something else.

The researchers even think the fermenting stage could be made shorter. The traditional method never needed a lab at all, just people who learned that fresh shark was a bad idea and that time fixed it.

[Doug at a small Icelandic counter. One pale cube on a toothpick. He eats it.]

Doug has ordered one cube, which the menu describes as traditional.

[Doug, red in the face, holding an empty water glass up to the counter.]

Doug is fine. He has been asking for water for an hour.

Leave a Greenland shark alone for long enough, though, and it finally grows up.

Centuries.

## The Teenager

The Teenager. A female Greenland shark may not be ready to breed until she's around a hundred and fifty years old.

[SHARK AGE: ~150. A shark drifting past, looking unimpressed.]

Females are thought to mature at around four meters long. In twenty sixteen, the team that dated these sharks put that length at no younger than about a hundred and fifty-six years, give or take twenty-two.

That's like becoming an adult long after your grandchildren have grown old.

A twenty twenty review of the species' breeding found its smallest mature female at about three point nine meters. It also pointed out that these age estimates are now being rechecked against an updated radiocarbon calibration, so the exact number may still move.

Here's the problem with that: fishing boats catch Greenland sharks by accident, at least three thousand five hundred of them a year worldwide, according to a twenty twenty-five Canadian assessment. Nobody really knows how many of them survive being thrown back.

[A crude trawler on the surface. A net. A red "X" over a shark outline.]

When an animal takes a century and a half to start breeding, every adult female lost leaves a very long gap to fill. Since twenty twenty, the Greenland shark has been listed as Vulnerable.

[Doug in the water, holding up a card that says "CONGRATULATIONS".]

Doug has brought a card, signed by Doug and only Doug.

[The shark drifts past the card, past Doug, and away.]

Doug is fine, and nothing happened at all. The narrator waited a full hour just to make sure.

And this one isn't even old yet.

## The Deep

The Deep. Greenland sharks have been recorded as deep as about two thousand two hundred meters.

[SHARK AGE: ~250. Depth gradient, light blue to black. A shark far down. Label "2,200 M".]

They live in water close to freezing, and that cold is the main idea for why they live so long. A cold body means a slow metabolism, and a slow metabolism may mean less wear and tear on the tissues over the years.

It's a body kept on a fridge shelf for its entire life.

The strange part is in its genes: in twenty twenty-four, researchers released the first full Greenland shark genome as a preprint, and it is about twice as long as ours. More than seventy percent of it is made of repeating stretches sometimes called jumping genes, and many of its duplicated genes are involved in repairing damaged DNA. One of the body's main anti-cancer proteins also carries a change the team wants to study further.

[A crude double helix with a sticking plaster on it.]

That isn't the secret to living forever. It's a lead, and the researchers say it needs more work.

For this part, Doug has a submersible.

[A crude round submersible with one porthole. Doug inside, cap on, small light on the front.]

Doug did not agree to this.

[The sub sinks past a huge, slow shark.]

Doug has met this shark once before, two kilometers down. It didn't remember him. It hasn't been long enough.

[The sub's light fades out. The cap drifts up through the dark.]

Doug has died. Again.

And somewhere out there, one shark was older than all of these.

## The Oldest

The Oldest. The biggest Greenland shark ever dated was a female, five hundred and two centimeters long, and about three hundred and ninety-two years old.

[SHARK AGE: ~392 (±120). Red stamp. The biggest shark yet fills the frame.]

Give or take a hundred and twenty years.

In twenty sixteen, a team led by Julius Nielsen dated twenty-eight sharks using the center of the eye lens, which forms before the shark is born and doesn't change after. They used the burst of radiocarbon from early nineteen-sixties nuclear bomb tests as a time marker. Only the smallest sharks, two hundred and twenty centimeters or shorter, carried that signal, which meant every bigger shark had been born before the bombs.

[Crude eye lens diagram, a dot in the middle, label "BEFORE BIRTH".]

Now take the low end: even at two hundred and seventy-two years, she was born before the United States existed, and the top of the range is five hundred and twelve.

Her birthday cake would need three hundred and ninety-two candles, give or take a hundred and twenty.

She didn't die of old age, either, because all twenty-eight sharks in that study had died by accident during fish surveys. So nobody knows how long a Greenland shark can actually live.

[Doug kneels at the hole in the ice with the candle-crowded cake. The candles are all lit. The ice around the hole is shining wet.]

Doug has brought the cake, and the candles have brought the edge of the ice down with them.

[The ice edge gives. Doug and the cake go in. Below, a very large, very slow shape. Cut to black.]

Doug has died. Again.

[Closing: the ship's monitor, red "LIVE" tag. The cake lands upside down on the seabed. The red cap drifts down into frame and settles on top of it. The shark passes over both, very slowly, and leaves them. SHARK AGE: ~392 (±120) + 1 MIN.]

The shark went past the cap without a second look. It has plenty of time to come back for it.
