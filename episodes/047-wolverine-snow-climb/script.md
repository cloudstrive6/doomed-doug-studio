## The Reindeer

The Reindeer.

No predator costs the reindeer herders of Finland more than this one does. In two thousand thirteen, wolverines did more damage to the herds than bears, wolves and lynx combined.

Its usual target is a breeding female reindeer, mostly in early spring. A reindeer cow can weigh up to a hundred kilos, and she is not going to stand still for it. That is a toddler taking on a fridge-freezer that can run.

Here's the strange part. Next door in Sweden, the state stopped paying herders mainly for their dead reindeer. Since nineteen ninety-six, it has paid Sami herding communities for every wolverine family born on their land, meaning a mother with young that has been found and documented. Since two thousand two, that has been at least two hundred thousand Swedish kronor a time.

## The Glutton

The Glutton.

The wolverine's scientific name is Gulo gulo, which is the same Latin word said twice. Gulo is Latin for glutton, and the name came with one very strange story.

In fifteen thirty-nine, the Swedish writer Olaus Magnus drew a gulo on his famous map of the north, and in fifteen fifty-five he described it in a history of the northern peoples.

According to Olaus, the gulo eats a carcass until its belly is stretched tight like a drum. Then it finds two trees growing close together, forces itself through the gap to empty its insides, and goes straight back to the carcass to eat again.

It's a man at an all-you-can-eat buffet who squeezes between two lampposts in the car park and then walks back in for seconds.

But that's not the weird part. It's a legend, and it simply would not go away. Almost two and a half centuries later, a book about travels through Lapland was still repeating the tree story, even while the author admitted he doubted it. By then the name glutton had stuck to the animal for good.

The real habit behind the legend is far more sensible. When a wolverine has more food than it can eat, it hides the rest for later.

[Doug walks up to two pines growing close together and measures the gap with his hands. Staging: stuck between pines only, no strain or relief cues.]

Doug wanted to know if a gap between two pines was really wide enough for anything to squeeze through. It was wide enough for Doug to get in.

The pines won. And the wolverine never needed the trees in the first place, because it has a fridge.

## The Fridge

The Fridge.

A wolverine stores its spare food in the cold, and it treats whole mountainsides like a kitchen.

It buries meat in the snow, stuffs it into rock crevices, or wedges it up in trees, and then it comes back for it later, when there is nothing else around to eat. Researchers studying wolverines in the Rocky Mountains think that boulder fields and cold, sheltered spots are how it keeps food fresh through the winter.

The cold is doing two jobs at once, and the wolverine gets both of them for free. It keeps the meat away from other scavengers, and it slows down the insects and bacteria that would happily eat anything left out in the open. A cache in a boulder field is a chest freezer with no plug and no lid, and only one animal on the mountain knows where it is.

The worst part is the timing. From February to April, a mother wolverine is feeding her young with her own milk, and that is exactly when fresh food is hardest to find. The caches she built over the winter are what get her through those weeks, and if she runs out, she can lose the whole litter.

So the glutton was never really overeating at all, because it was meal prepping for the hungriest part of the year.

[Doug climbs into the boulder cache, cap on, arms crossed, guarding the food.]

Doug climbed into the cache to guard the food from thieves.

He is now part of the food, and up at the treeline, the wolverine raids fridges that belong to other people.

## The Trapline

The Trapline.

Trappers in the far north once set long lines of traps through the forest and the treeline, and the wolverine treated those lines like a shopping route.

Wildlife biologists in Alaska say wolverines are known to steal animals straight out of traps, and to damage the cabins trappers use out in the wilderness. In Canada, its reputation among trappers was so bad that European settlers treated it as a pest.

And a wolverine that breaks into a cabin doesn't only take things. A wolverine marks its food and landmarks with a strong musk from glands under its tail, which is why one of its nicknames is the skunk bear.

A wolverine in a cabin is a burglar who also leaves his signature on everything he couldn't carry.

Here's the problem with a trapline. It's a long row of free food, tied down and waiting for collection, along a path that the trapper has made easy to follow. To a wolverine, somebody has kindly set the table.

[Doug's log cabin, door hanging open. Shelves empty. A single sad loaf on the table with a stink line rising off it. The cap sits on the table beside it.]

Doug left his cabin for ten minutes to go and fetch firewood from the woodpile. When he got back, the shelves were empty, and the one thing left behind had been marked.

Doug is fine. The cabin is not.

[Doug looks at the loaf. He does not eat the loaf.]

The cabin was easy to find, but the next meal is buried under the snow.

## The Nose

The Nose.

A wolverine can smell a carcass through almost two meters of snow, which is deeper than most grown men are tall.

In the far north, animals die all winter long, and nobody comes along to clear them away. Some are killed by wolves, some by hunters and some by the winter itself, and then the snow quietly covers them up. To us, a body under that much snow is gone until the spring melt. To a wolverine, it is still out there, and it has a smell.

It follows that smell across the surface, picks its spot, and digs straight down until it hits dinner.

The unsettling part is how little the snow really changes. Burying something doesn't hide it from a wolverine, because to a wolverine burying is only storing, which is exactly what it would have done with the body anyway.

[Doug digs a hole and climbs in with a sandwich. He pulls snow over himself. Only a dent and the tip of the cap show.]

Doug decided to hide from the wolverine under the snow, and he brought a sandwich in case it took a while.

[Snow sprays. The wolverine digs straight down beside Doug's head, takes the sandwich, and leaves. Doug blinks.]

It found the sandwich almost at once and walked away with it. Doug was right there, and it did not take Doug.

It was a little disappointing. Doug was not the most interesting thing in that hole.

And the wolverine didn't stay to eat, because it had somewhere to be, and for a wolverine somewhere can be very far away.

## The Range

The Range.

In two thousand nine, one young male wolverine walked from Wyoming to Colorado, a trip of about eight hundred kilometers.

Researchers from the Wildlife Conservation Society called him M fifty-six. They had fitted him with a radio collar near Grand Teton National Park, and they started tracking him in early April.

Over the next two months, he worked his way south through Wyoming, crossed Interstate eighty, one of the busiest roads in the state, and kept going into northern Colorado. That is more than ten kilometers a day on average, by one small animal with nobody carrying the snacks. The whole trip is roughly the distance from New York to Detroit, on foot, through mountain country, with no map.

For a wolverine, a trip like that is surprisingly normal, because the whole animal is built for covering ground. An adult male can use a home range of up to about thirteen hundred square kilometers, often as big as a grizzly bear's. Young wolverines also travel between mountain ranges to find territories of their own.

Here's what made the news. For ninety years nobody had confirmed one in Colorado, and then one simply walked in by himself.

Doug followed M fifty-six with a backpack, a paper map and a great deal of confidence.

The map ran out first. And the higher the wolverine goes, the more the ground itself starts working for it.

## The Snowshoes

The Snowshoes.

One wolverine can kill several reindeer, one after another.

Not one reindeer. Several. All it needs is the right kind of snow, at the right time of year.

Among the weasels that live on land, the wolverine has the largest feet, and it is built to travel through deep, soft snow.

So when the snow is soft enough to hold up a wolverine but not a reindeer, the chase is over before it starts. A hard crust on top of the snow works the same way. The wolverine runs across the surface on its broad paws, while the reindeer, which weighs several times more, breaks through and sinks.

Doug in boots is a high heel in wet sand, and the wolverine is a snowshoe.

The twist is that the wolverine's best weapon in that reindeer hunt was never its teeth. It was the snow. Even then, there are limits, and wildlife experts in Finland say it still cannot take down an adult moose.

Doug followed the tracks across fresh snow, and every step went a little deeper than the last. The wolverine never once looked back.

## The Snow Den

The Snow Den.

Researchers checked five hundred and sixty-two wolverine dens across North America and northern Europe, and every single one was in a place where the snow lasted into spring.

That two thousand ten study matched the dens against satellite maps of snow cover from late April to the middle of May.

A wolverine mother digs her den deep into the snowpack, often as a network of snow tunnels around boulders and rocks. The snow holds in the warmth like a blanket laid over the cold ground. Her kits arrive between February and May, and that roof has to hold until spring.

The timing of all this is planned months ahead, back in the middle of summer. Wolverines mate in summer, but the embryo waits, and only settles in to grow in late fall or early winter, which is called delayed implantation.

A den under snow is a house whose roof melts every spring, so you'd better pick the right roof.

But that's not the scary part. The toughest animal in the snow cannot raise a family without snow that stays.

[Doug digs his own small snow den next to a big drift. The mother wolverine's silhouette watches from a tunnel mouth. No kits on screen.]

Doug dug a snow den right next door, to be neighbours, and settled in to wait for spring.

Spring came. Doug didn't. And somewhere above the den, a male wolverine was about to go straight up a mountain.

The summit.

## The Climb

The Climb.

One January, a wolverine in Glacier National Park climbed about fifteen hundred meters up Mount Cleveland in about ninety minutes.

The peak was a tower of ice and snow, and the climber was a radio-collared male the researchers called M three.

He was heading for a mountain goat carcass. The lead researcher said that at the bottom he seemed to stop, as if asking himself whether he really wanted to do this. Then he went straight up.

Fifteen hundred meters in an hour and a half is like running up four stacked Empire State Buildings, in snow, without a break.

The astonishing part is that he didn't stop at the top. He crossed the very summit and kept going down the far side. To a wolverine, the highest mountain in the park was not the destination. It was a shortcut.

Only about three hundred wolverines are left in the lower forty-eight states. The United States Fish and Wildlife Service has listed them as threatened, because climate change is expected to shrink the spring snow they need.

The last time Doug met a wolverine, a grizzly walked away from both of them. This one paused beside Doug, looked up the face, and went up anyway.

Doug followed M three up the face in crampons he had never used. The wolverine reached the summit. Doug reached about halfway.

It went over the mountain because the mountain was in the way. So was Doug.
