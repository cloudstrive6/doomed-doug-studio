### The forest.
**1. The Reindeer** (one of the three strongest: the cold-open item)
- Facts to research: wolverines kill semi-domestic reindeer in Fennoscandia, including adults several times their own weight, more often in
  winter and when snow conditions favour them; and they kill as well as scavenge (Mattisson et al. 2016, *Ecosphere*, "Predation or scavenging?";
  Mattisson et al. 2011, Scandinavian wolverine diet/predation studies; give a reindeer adult weight range and the weight ratio only as sources
  allow). Caribou and moose calves in North America (Alaska Dept. of Fish and Game species page).
- **Cold-open fact (sourced):** a wolverine about the weight of a medium dog can kill an adult reindeer several times heavier (exact ratio from
  the source).
- **Twist (by 0:45):** Sweden pays reindeer-herding Sami communities **for every wolverine litter born on their land**, not for each dead
  reindeer, so a newborn wolverine has a price tag before it opens its eyes (verify: Swedish carnivore compensation system based on confirmed
  reproductions, from 1996; Zabel & Holm-Müller 2008, *Conservation Biology*; Swedish EPA / Naturvårdsverket; state the current figure only if
  sourced, otherwise no number).
- Scale analogy: compute from the sourced weights, e.g. a 15 kg animal against a 90 kg one is a toddler taking on a fridge-freezer
  that can run. Don't reuse "medium dog" as the analogy (041 spent it; the field guide note carries it visually).
- Doug beat (costume gag, the only one this episode): wolverines are not known to hunt people, "which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a
  reindeer", cap on top of the antlers. The field guide reads "DOG-SIZED"; Doug writes "GOOD DOG?". Death 357. "Doug did not agree to this." here,
  once. The narrator crosses out "GOOD DOG?".

**2. The Glutton**
- Facts to research: the scientific name *Gulo gulo*, Latin for "glutton"; the 1555 account by Olaus Magnus (*Historia de Gentibus
  Septentrionalibus*) that the "gulo" gorges on a carcass, then squeezes between two close-growing trees to empty its stomach and eats again
  (cite the text or a history-of-zoology source; tell it as a myth). Other names: "carcajou", "skunk bear" (sourced etymology only). The real
  explanation for the "glutton": it eats what it can and stores the rest (sets up item 3).
- **Twist:** the squeezing-tree story is wrong, but it was in the books for centuries, and the name stuck to the animal for good.
- Scale analogy: the myth is a man eating a buffet, squeezing between two lampposts, and going back for seconds.
- Doug beat: Doug tests the Olaus Magnus method between two pines after a large lunch. He gets stuck. The pines win. Death 358.
  041 callback line can go here (one line only).

**3. The Fridge**
- Facts to research: wolverines cache food in snow, boulder fields and cold spots and come back to it for months (Inman et al. 2012, *Canadian
  Journal of Zoology*, "The wolverine's niche: linking reproductive chronology, caching, competition, and climate"); cold caches slow spoilage
  and keep food from competitors (beetles, bacteria, other scavengers). The upper molar turned sideways (about 90 degrees) for shearing frozen
  meat (cite a mammal anatomy source or Chadwick 2010; use only if sourced, and do **not** restate 041's "cracks bones and frozen meat" as new).
- **Twist:** the "glutton" isn't overeating; it is meal-prepping for a winter where food might not come for weeks.
- Scale analogy: a cache in a boulder field is a chest freezer with no plug and no lid, and nobody else can find it.
- Doug beat: Doug climbs into the boulder-field cache "to guard the food". He is now in the fridge. Death 359, played as a frozen Doug among
  frozen leftovers, cap on top, label "LEFTOVERS".

### The treeline.
**4. The Trapline** (survival)
- Facts to research: wolverines raiding traplines and trappers' cabins in the North American and Scandinavian historical record (cite a
  natural-history source or Chadwick 2010 / Alaska Dept. of Fish and Game); marking food and dens with musk from anal glands (the "skunk bear"
  name, sourced). Keep it historical; no trapping how-to, no fur-trade promotion.
- **Twist:** the animal trappers came for kept emptying the traps first.
- Scale analogy: a wolverine in a cabin is a burglar who also marks everything he doesn't take.
- Doug beat: Doug's cabin is cleaned out and the remaining food is musk-marked. **Survival: "Doug is fine. The cabin is not."** (if the
  writer wants a fresh variant; "Doug is fine." must appear once).

**5. The Nose** (survival)
- Facts to research: wolverines find carcasses buried under deep snow (including avalanche-killed animals) by smell and dig down to them
  (cite Copeland / Glacier Wolverine Project, Chadwick 2010, or an agency species page; give a depth only if sourced).
- **Twist:** a buried carcass under metres of snow is not lost; to a wolverine it's just stored.
- Scale analogy: smelling a dinner through snow as deep as a garage door is tall (recompute from any sourced depth; drop if none).
- Doug beat: Doug buries himself in snow to hide, with his sandwich. The wolverine digs straight down to the sandwich, takes it and leaves Doug
  there. Survival; the narrator is quietly disappointed that Doug was not the interesting thing in the hole. No avalanche, no rescue, no
  burial-safety content.

**6. The Range**
- Facts to research: very large home ranges (males often hundreds of km², sourced figures from Glacier NP / Greater Yellowstone studies, e.g.
  Inman et al. 2012 or Copeland); wolverine **M56**, radio-collared by the Wildlife Conservation Society, walked from Wyoming's Grand Teton area
  to Colorado in 2009, roughly 800 km, the first confirmed wolverine in Colorado since 1919 (cite WCS / Colorado Parks and Wildlife; use their
  distance). Travels over mountains rather than around them (sourced).
- **Twist:** it didn't take a road or a valley; it went over the mountains, and nobody had seen one there for ninety years.
- Scale analogy: 800 km is roughly London to Edinburgh or New York to Detroit on foot over mountains (pick one, check the distance).
- Doug beat: Doug follows M56 with a backpack, a paper map and confidence. The map runs out first. Death 360 (round number: no milestone bit),
  cartoon flat, map blowing away.

### The snow.
**7. The Snowshoes**
- Facts to research: wolverine foot loading (body weight per area of foot) is among the lowest of large North American mammals, so it travels
  on snow that a reindeer, caribou or moose sinks into (Telfer & Kelsall 1984, *Ecology*, "Adaptation of some large North American mammals
  for survival in snow"; give the comparison as the paper does). Connect back to item 1: deep or crusted snow is what makes the big prey
  catchable.
- **Twist:** the wolverine's real weapon in item 1 was the snow; it stays on top while the prey and Doug go through.
- Scale analogy: Doug in boots is a high heel in wet sand; the wolverine is a snowshoe (use the paper's ratio if a number is said).
- Doug beat: Doug follows tracks across deep snow and sinks to the cap. Only the cap is visible. Death 361.

**8. The Snow Den**
- Facts to research: female wolverines den in deep snow (tunnels and chambers dug under snow, often among boulders or fallen trees) and need
  snow that lasts into spring: Copeland et al. 2010 (*Canadian Journal of Zoology*, "The bioclimatic envelope of the wolverine") found that
  almost all known dens and most locations lie in areas of persistent spring snow cover (to about mid-May; state as the paper does);
  Magoun & Copeland 1998 on den sites. Delayed implantation (sourced, one line). Kits born late winter (sourced).
- **Twist:** the toughest animal in the snow can't raise a family without snow that lasts.
- Scale analogy: a den under snow is a house whose roof melts every spring, so you'd better pick the right roof.
- Doug beat: Doug digs his own snow den nearby "to be neighbours" and waits all winter. Death 362 as a frozen Doug-shaped snowdrift with the
  cap on top. **Kits stay off-screen** (no cute babies; at most a single mother wolverine silhouette at the tunnel mouth).

### The summit.
**9. The Climb** (the payoff promise, the boss)
- Facts to research: wolverine **M3** of the Glacier Wolverine Project climbed Mount Cleveland, the highest peak in Glacier National Park
  (about 3,190 m), gaining about 1,500 m in roughly 90 minutes, in winter, up the steep face (cite the National Park Service, the Glacier
  Wolverine Project / Jeff Copeland, Rick Yates, or Douglas Chadwick's *The Wolverine Way* 2010; use their exact figures and say "about").
  Wolverines regularly cross high peaks (sourced). **Honesty line:** in the contiguous US there are only a few hundred wolverines, and in
  November 2023 the US Fish and Wildlife Service listed them as threatened, citing loss of spring snowpack (cite the FWS final rule; state
  the figure only as FWS gives it).
- Scale analogy: 1,500 m in 90 minutes is like climbing the stairs of the Empire State Building about four times without stopping (recompute
  from heights), in snow, uphill, in winter.
- Doug beat: Doug follows M3 up the face with crampons he has never used. The wolverine reaches the summit and keeps going over it. Doug
  reaches about halfway. Death 363, played flat: the cap blows off the ridge and up past the summit flag, then black. No fall shown.
- Closing: the summit at dawn, wolverine tracks crossing the top and continuing down the other side. The cap sits in the last track. Field guide
  sticky note "NEEDS SNOW." Kicker idea: "It went over the mountain because the mountain was in the way. So was Doug." Outro 30 words or fewer:
  death counter plus one CTA.
