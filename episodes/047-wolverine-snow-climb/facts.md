## 1. The Reindeer
- Wolverine is the most significant cause of reindeer damage across Finland's herding area; in 2013 it caused more reindeer damage than bear, wolf and lynx combined: https://www.largecarnivores.fi/large-carnivores-and-us/damages/reindeer-damages.html ("the wolverine is the most significant cause of reindeer damages when looking at the reindeer herding area as a whole"; "In 2013 the wolverine caused more reindeer damages than the other three species combined.") (Fetched). Script: "no predator costs herders more" and "did more damage to the herds than bears, wolves and lynx combined".
- "Its usual target is a breeding female reindeer, mostly in early spring": same page, "Wolverines kill reindeer primarily in early spring and the target is usually a breeding female." (Fetched)
- Cow up to 100 kg: Vildriket above. (The 16 kg wolverine weight is now spoken only in the cold open; the toddler image leans on it.)
- Toddler vs fridge-freezer: image, not a measurement (a toddler is roughly 10-15 kg; a fridge-freezer roughly 60-100 kg).
- Sweden: from 1996 payments to reindeer herders changed from predation losses to documented wolverine reproductions, irrespective of predation (Persson et al. 2015, Conservation Letters, "Paying for an endangered predator leads to population recovery"): https://conservationevidencejournal.com/individual-study/7798 (Fetched) ; https://openpolar.no/Record/crwiley:10.1111%2Fconl.12171 (Search). Script hedges "stopped paying herders mainly for their dead reindeer".
- Paid to Sami reindeer herding communities since 1996; a reproduction = a breeding female with young, documented; SEK 200,000 per documented reproduction from 2002, plus SEK 25,000 added in the 2023/24 budget (so "at least two hundred thousand Swedish kronor"): SLU news, June 2026: https://www.slu.se/en/news/2026/06/crisis-for-swedens-celebrated-model-for-coexistence-between-carnivores-and-reindeer-herders/ (Fetched)
- "Wolverine kits are born blind": ADF&G, "Wolverine kits are born blind and weigh less than 1 pound." (Fetched) "Price on it before it can see" = narrator's framing of the payment-per-documented-reproduction rule.
- "grew by about four percent a year": Conservation Evidence summary of Persson et al. 2015, "The wolverine population grew at an annual rate of 4%." (Fetched)
- "Wolverines are not known to attack people": Defenders of Wildlife wolverine ID card, "there are no documented wolverine attacks on people": https://defenders.org/publications/wolverine-id-card (Search); Ontario government, wolverines are shy and normally avoid humans: https://www.ontario.ca/page/wolverine (Search). Script makes no stronger claim.
- Doug beat (reindeer costume, cut to black, death 357): staging only; no kill shown.

## 2. The Glutton
- Gulo gulo; gulo is Latin for glutton (script says only that "the name came with" the story, not that the story produced the name; the English "glutton" is likely a folk etymology of Old Norse fjallfress via German Vielfraß, and the legend grew around the name, Wikipedia "Wolverine" etymology section https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolverine and Wiktionary "Vielfraß" https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Vielfra%C3%9F, per screener):  National Geographic (2019), "from the Latin word for 'glutton'": https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/wolverines-battling-climate-change-shrinking-north-territory-feature (Fetched); Hinterland Who's Who (Canadian Wildlife Federation / Environment Canada), nickname "glutton" for its appetite: https://www.hww.ca/en/wildlife/mammals/wolverine.html (Fetched)
- Olaus Magnus put the gulo on his 1539 map (Carta Marina) and described it gorging on a carcass until swollen, squeezing between trees to empty itself, then returning to eat again: University of Minnesota, James Ford Bell Library, Carta Marina tour: https://apps.lib.umn.edu/bell/map/OLAUS/TOUR/tourb7.html (Fetched; cites Olaus Magnus, Description of the Northern Peoples, 1998 Hakluyt ed., vol. III p. 888)
- Historia de gentibus septentrionalibus, Rome, 1555 ("a history of the northern peoples"): University of Tromsø Northern Lights library page on Olaus Magnus: https://www.ub.uit.no/northernlights/eng/omagnus.htm (Search) ; https://apps.lib.umn.edu/bell/map/OLAUS/BIO/book.html (Search). "Swedish writer": Olaus Magnus was a Swedish writer and churchman (same pages).
- It is a legend: Wikipedia "Gulon" (the legendary gluttonous creature derived from Olaus Magnus's account): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulon (Search)
- "Almost two and a half centuries later, a book about travels through Lapland was still repeating the tree story, even while the author admitted he doubted it": Giuseppe Acerbi, Travels through Sweden, Finland, and Lapland, to the North Cape, in the years 1798 and 1799, vol. II (London, 1802), p. 218: repeats that the glutton eases its stomach "by squeezing out its contents between two trees ... after which he falls to eating again", and prefers a more probable explanation: https://runeberg.org/acerbi/2/0242.html (Fetched). 1802 - 1555 = 247 years.
- Real habit: "Wolverines hide excess food by burying it or stashing it in rock crevices or in trees." Hinterland Who's Who (Fetched)
- Buffet and lampposts: image.
- Doug beat (pines): staging only; no bathroom cues (screener A5). The 041 callback moved to item 9.
- Item 2 kicker "named after an appetite, and the story behind that name was never true": Gulon legend (above) + Acerbi's doubt; the appetite meaning from NatGeo/HWW.

## 3. The Fridge
- Buries food in snow, rock crevices, trees: Hinterland Who's Who (Fetched).
- "comes back for it later, when there is nothing else around to eat": Inman et al. 2012 (below): caches amassed over winter fuel lactation when food is scarce; Finland: "The wolverine often returns to the carcass": https://largecarnivores.fi/species/wolverine/wolverines-diet-and-hunting-behaviour.html (Fetched)
- Researchers in the Rockies think boulders and cold, structured sites keep food through the winter ("refrigeration-zone" hypothesis): Inman, Magoun, Persson & Mattisson 2012, "The wolverine's niche: linking reproductive chronology, caching, competition, and climate," Journal of Mammalogy 93(3):634-644 (the brief listed it as Can. J. Zool.; it is J. Mammal.): https://complete.bioone.org/journals/journal-of-mammalogy/volume-93/issue-3/11-MAMM-A-319.1/The-wolverines-niche--linking-reproductive-chronology-caching-competition-and/10.1644/11-MAMM-A-319.1.short (Search); WCS / ScienceDaily release, July 2012: "the crevices and boulders of the mountainous terrain"; caches protect food "from scavengers, insects and bacteria": https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/07/120712141518.htm (Fetched). Script says "think", because it is a hypothesis.
- February to April lactation; food scarce then; caches built over winter carry the female; otherwise early litter loss: same paper abstract ("Females need to fuel lactation (February-April) with caches amassed over winter ... otherwise, early litter loss occurs") and ScienceDaily (Fetched).
- Chest freezer analogy: image.
- Rotated upper molar: sourced (The Wolverine Foundation, "upper molars being rotated 90° inward", https://wolverinefoundation.org/general-characteristics, Fetched) but cut from the script: it is a family-wide trait and its only payoff is frozen meat, which 041 already spent.

- Burglar analogy and "set the table": images.

## 6. The Range
- 2009; young male "M56"; WCS researchers; radio-tracking collar; captured near Grand Teton National Park; tracked from early April; about 500 miles (= about 800 km); crossed Interstate 80, "heavily trafficked route across Wyoming"; into northern Colorado; first wolverine in Colorado since 1919; young wolverines disperse between mountain ranges; adult males may use up to 500 square miles (about 1,300 km²), often as large as a grizzly's: WCS news release, June 2009: https://newsroom.wcs.org/News-Releases/articleType/ArticleView/articleId/4656/After-90-Years-Wolverine-Returns-to-Colorado.aspx (Fetched)
- "Within two months, the wolverine had traveled 500 miles": WCS release / Mongabay coverage: https://news.mongabay.com/2009/06/wolverine-returns-to-colorado-after-90-year-absence/ (Search). 800 km / 60 days = 13 km/day, so "more than ten kilometers a day on average" (straight-line division; the real route was longer, so this is conservative).
- "ninety years": 2009 - 1919. Script says "nobody had confirmed one there", matching WCS ("first confirmed"), not "none at all".
- The brief's twist "it didn't take a road" was cut: WCS says M56 crossed Interstate 80. The script uses the road crossing as a fact instead.
- New York to Detroit: straight-line distance about 770 km (NYC 40.71 N 74.01 W to Detroit 42.33 N 83.05 W, great-circle calculation) - "roughly" 800 km.
- "one small animal": general (ADF&G males 11-18 kg). "Dog-sized" stays on the field-guide page only, not spoken. "Two months" now spoken once.

## 7. The Snowshoes
- "One wolverine can kill several reindeer, one after another" / "All it needs is the right snow": can kill several reindeer in quick succession in favourable snow; snow must be deep and soft enough to support the wolverine but not the larger prey; cannot take down an adult moose: Large Carnivores in Finland, diet page: https://largecarnivores.fi/species/wolverine/wolverines-diet-and-hunting-behaviour.html ("In the winter the wolverine may kill several reindeer in quick succession."; "The snow has to be deep and soft enough so that it will support the wolverine but not the larger prey animal."; "it can't take down an adult moose") (Fetched)
- Hard crust works too; broad paws stay up while reindeer sink: Skansen wolverine page, "with hard snow crust, or loose snow that supports its broad paws while reindeer sink down" (Fetched)
- Largest feet among land weasels: National Geographic, among the nonaquatic mustelids the wolverine has the "largest feet" (Fetched). Built for deep, soft snow: ADF&G, "well suited to travel through deep, soft snow" (Fetched); Wolverine Foundation, large feet facilitate travel through deep snow (Fetched).
- Reindeer "weighs several times more": item 1 weights.
- Telfer & Kelsall 1984 foot-loading ratio: not used. The abstract ranks the wolverine's snow adaptation above most ungulates but below caribou and moose, which does not support a "lowest foot loading" line, and no foot-load number could be confirmed (https://zendy.io/title/10.2307/1937779, Search).
- High heel / snowshoe: image.

## 8. The Snow Den
- All 562 reproductive dens from Fennoscandia and North America were at sites with persistent spring snow cover; snow composite 24 April to 15 May: Copeland et al. 2010, "The bioclimatic envelope of the wolverine (Gulo gulo): do climatic constraints limit its geographic distribution?", Canadian Journal of Zoology 88:233-246: https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/34712 (Fetched). "northern Europe" = Fennoscandia.
- Females burrow deep into snowpacks to den: National Geographic (Fetched). Dens are "a complex of snow tunnels associated with boulders or rocks": Hinterland Who's Who (Fetched).
- Snow acts like a blanket over the cold ground: NPS, Grizzly Bear and Wolverine Superpowers exhibit: https://www.nps.gov/places/grizzly-bear-and-wolverine-superpowers-exhibit.htm (Fetched)
- Births between February and May; mate in summer; delayed implantation: Hinterland Who's Who (Fetched). Implantation in late fall or early winter: ADF&G (Fetched).
- "that roof has to hold until spring": dens in persistent spring snow (Copeland 2010).
- House-with-melting-roof: image. Kits never on screen.

- Jeff Copeland (lead researcher): "He ascended over 5,000 feet vertical and crossed the very top of Mt. Cleveland.
