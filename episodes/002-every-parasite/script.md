## Zombie Ant Fungus

Zombie Ant Fungus.

Carpenter ants in the rainforests of Thailand build their nests high up in the trees, so when one of them turns up alone near the forest floor, something has gone very wrong.

That ant is infected with a fungus called Ophiocordyceps, which spreads through its entire body. The ant leaves the trails the other ants use and wanders at random, shaken by occasional convulsions, until it ends up low in the undergrowth.

Then, at around noon, in a strangely synchronized way, it climbs a small plant, finds the thick main vein on the underside of a leaf, and bites down. Researchers found these ants dying in clusters about twenty-five centimeters off the ground, a little lower than a ruler standing on end, where the humidity and temperature suit the fungus perfectly. But that's not the disturbing part. ## Horsehair Worm

Horsehair Worm.

After a rainstorm, people sometimes find what looks like a long, tangled hair twisting in a puddle, a swimming pool, or an animal's water trough. It's alive.

It started life as a tiny larva in the water, and when a cricket or a grasshopper eats something carrying one, the larva moves into its body and begins to grow. For about three months, the cricket carries on as normal while the worm grows long and thin inside it. The adults are sometimes called Gordian worms, because they twist themselves into knots.

An adult worm can reach thirty centimeters or more, while a field cricket is only about two and a half centimeters long. The worm is only about a millimeter thick, so it can fold itself up again and again, like a thirty centimeter phone charger coiled up inside a matchbox.

There's a catch. The worm can only finish its life in water, and crickets are not swimmers.

So near the end, the cricket starts looking for water, and in one experiment, infected crickets were more likely to jump into a pool than healthy ones. Once the cricket is in the water, the worm has what it came for.

Horsehair worms can't infect people, pets, or any other animal with a backbone, which is exactly why Doug is dressed as a cricket.

## Green-Banded Broodsac

Green-Banded Broodsac.

This parasite needs a bird to eat a snail, and its solution is to disguise the snail's eyes as a caterpillar.

It's a flatworm, and its eggs end up on the leaves where amber snails feed. Inside the snail, it develops into swollen sacs that push up into the eye stalks, until the stalks are fat, translucent, and striped in bright green bands.

## Toxoplasma

Toxoplasma.

This single-celled parasite is the one on the list you're most likely to be carrying yourself, since more than forty million people in the United States are thought to have it.

Toxoplasma can infect almost any warm-blooded animal, including birds, rodents and people, but it can only reproduce sexually inside one kind of animal. Only a cat will do. For the parasite to finish its life cycle, a cat has to eat whatever it's living in.

That's a problem, because rats have an instinctive fear of the smell of cats. A healthy rat avoids any spot that smells of cat, even if it has never seen one in its life.

What makes it remarkable is its precision. In a study published in two thousand, researchers at Oxford let rats explore a pen with corners that smelled of different animals. Infected rats lost their fear of the corner that smelled of cat, and some of them even seemed to be drawn to it. ## Blood Flukes

Blood Flukes.

These worms don't need Doug in a costume, because they're perfectly happy living inside a human being, and they get in through bare skin.

Blood flukes start out inside freshwater snails, which release swarms of tiny larvae with forked tails that swim around looking for skin. When they find some, they can get through it in a matter of minutes, dropping their tails on the way in, with no bite and no wound. Within a few weeks, they grow into adult worms that live in the blood vessels. The disease they cause, schistosomiasis, has been reported in seventy-nine countries, and more than ninety percent of the people who need treatment for it live in Africa.

The adults are only one to two centimeters long, about the length of a fingernail, and males and females pair up, with the female living inside a groove along the male's body. A pair can stay in the blood vessels for years, typically three to seven, producing eggs the entire time.

The strange part is that the worms themselves aren't really the problem. The eggs are. Some of them leave the body, but many get stuck in the organs, and it's the body's reaction to all those trapped eggs that slowly causes the damage.

## Kissing Bug

Kissing Bug.

Its nickname comes from its favorite place to bite, and there's nothing affectionate about it. Kissing bugs hide in cracks in walls and roofs during the day, and they come out at night to feed on blood, often from the face of someone asleep.

The bugs are about the length of a paperclip, and the bite itself isn't really the problem. The parasite, a single cell called Trypanosoma cruzi, lives in the droppings the bug leaves close to the bite, and gets in when those are rubbed into the bite, the eyes or the mouth. The disease is named after Carlos Chagas, the Brazilian doctor who first described it in nineteen oh nine. ## Malaria

Malaria.

Of every parasite on this list, this is the one that kills the most people, and it arrives in something you would swat without thinking.

The parasite is called Plasmodium, and it's carried by some female Anopheles mosquitoes. Only females bite, because they need the blood to make their eggs. A typical house mosquito weighs about two and a half milligrams, which is lighter than a single grain of rice. In eighteen ninety-seven, a British doctor named Ronald Ross found the parasite inside a mosquito, which helped prove how the disease spreads, and it won him a Nobel Prize.

When an infected mosquito bites, the parasite travels to the liver and quietly multiplies there. Then it moves into the red blood cells, multiplies again, and breaks out to invade more of them, over and over.

Five species of Plasmodium cause malaria in people, and the deadliest, Plasmodium falciparum, is also the most common in Africa. What makes it so dangerous is a single trick. It changes the surface of the red blood cells it infects, making them sticky, so they cling to the walls of small blood vessels instead of flowing freely. When that happens in the brain, it can lead to the most severe form of the disease.

## Raccoon Roundworm

Raccoon Roundworm.

Raccoons have adapted comfortably to living alongside people, in towns, in cities, and often right next to houses. In some parts of the United States, surveys have found this roundworm in more than four out of five raccoons.

In a raccoon, the worm lives in the gut and mostly does no harm. A single female worm can produce more than a hundred thousand eggs a day, and an infected raccoon can shed millions of them. Raccoons also use shared latrines, often close to homes. Each egg is about as wide as a human hair, and after two to four weeks outside, it becomes infectious.

The twist is that humans were never supposed to be part of this life cycle. If a person accidentally swallows the eggs, the larvae hatch and start wandering, because they're in the wrong animal and have nowhere they're meant to go. They can end up in the eyes or the brain.

Human cases are rare, and in four decades, physicians worldwide have published only about sixty. But when the larvae invade the nervous system, the consequences are serious. In one review of those cases, about one in five of those patients died, and most of the survivors were left with lasting neurological problems.

## Sleeping Sickness

Sleeping Sickness.

The tsetse fly is found only in sub-Saharan Africa, where it feeds on blood, and a few of them carry a passenger that rewires when you sleep.

That passenger is a single-celled parasite called Trypanosoma brucei. After the bite, it spreads through the blood and the lymph, and that's the first stage. In the second stage, the parasite crosses into the central nervous system, and that's where the disease gets its name.

It scrambles the sleep cycle, so people become drowsy during the day and can't sleep at night, and the total amount of sleep barely changes. It's like jet lag that follows you home and never wears off. There are two forms. The slow one, found in western and central Africa, causes about ninety-two percent of cases and can take months or even years to reach this stage. The fast one, found in eastern and southern Africa, can get there within weeks, and without treatment, both are generally fatal.

The part scientists only worked out recently is how it might be doing this. In a study on mice, the parasite shortened the animals' internal body clock, and it even did the same thing to cells growing in a dish, just by being near them.
