# Facts: 002 Every Parasite

Every factual claim in script.md is listed here with its source. Doug's deaths, costumes, Buddy, and the "it would feel
like" lines (framed as hypothetical, non-medical sensations drawn from the sourced mechanism) are fiction. Everyday-object
sizes in the analogies (paperclip about 3 cm, ruler 30 cm, fingernail about 1 to 1.5 cm, grain of rice about 20 to 30 mg,
phone charger 30 cm, matchbox about 5 cm) are rough common-knowledge comparisons, as in 001.

## Severity order (act 2) and why it differs from the brief
Axis: host proximity (animal hosts, then human hosts), then how likely the parasite is to kill an untreated human host.
The brief put Raccoon Roundworm before Malaria. The sourced figures reverse them, so the script reorders (brief section 2
allows this; the last item is unchanged).

| # | Item | Figure used for the order | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | Blood Flukes | about 14,353 deaths a year against at least 253.7 million needing treatment (well under 0.01%) | WHO schistosomiasis fact sheet |
| 7 | Kissing Bug | more than 10,000 deaths a year among about 8 million infected; up to a third develop cardiac disorders decades later | WHO Chagas fact sheet |
| 8 | Malaria | 610,000 deaths from 282 million cases in 2024 (about 0.2% per case) | WHO malaria fact sheet |
| 9 | Raccoon Roundworm | 8 of 42 (19%) published neurological cases with known outcome died; 71% left with deficits | PMC12261942 review |

| 11 | Brain-Eating Amoeba | 97% fatality (4 survivors of 167 US cases, 1962 to 2024), even with treatment | CDC |

Sleeping sickness and Naegleria are both close to always fatal untreated; Naegleria goes last because it is the only one
that kills almost everyone even when treated.

## Opener / costume premise
- Opener "Every parasite on this list can kill the animal it lives in" (draft 2, fix 1; replaces "in this video kills the thing it lives in"): each item has a host it can kill, as sourced per item below: roach (jewel wasp), ant (fungus), cricket driven into water (horsehair worm), snail delivered to a bird (broodsac), rat delivered to a cat (Toxoplasma), and humans for items 6 to 11 (WHO/CDC death figures). "Can" is used because several are usually harmless to some hosts (Toxoplasma in healthy people, Baylisascaris in raccoons), which the script says.
- Act 1 parasites use insects, snails and rodents, not people: jewel wasp hosts are cockroaches (Emerald cockroach wasp refs
  below); zombie ant fungus hosts are carpenter ants (Hughes et al. 2011); horsehair worms cannot parasitize vertebrates
  (UC IPM); broodsac hosts are snails and birds (Wesołowska and Wesołowski 2014). Toxoplasma does infect people (CDC) and
  the script says so.

## 1. Jewel Wasp
Draft 2: all Wikipedia URLs removed (screener fix 5).
- Metallic green, tropical; female about 22 mm long, "a little shorter than a paperclip" (paperclip about 3 cm). Primary source: Williams, F. X. 1942, "Ampulex compressa (Fabr.), a cockroach-hunting wasp introduced from New Caledonia into Hawaii", Proc. Hawaiian Entomol. Soc. 11: 221-233. https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/7d4d17bd-3c28-4485-bb7a-282812204814 (PDF: https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/b4a7a9ec-4bb7-4bb9-850a-8e736a2b7652/content). Note: the repository refused connections on 2026-09-30; the 22 mm female length was confirmed from the search-index excerpt of that PDF, not from a full read. Screener re-check 2026-09-30: server still unreachable (ECONNREFUSED; Cal Academy mirror 403). Corroboration: Wikipedia "about 22 mm" (cites Williams 1942); Orkin "22-28 millimeters" (low reliability); literature excerpt "2–3 cm". CD 2026-09-30: script line changed from "nearly twice its own length" to "far bigger than itself", so no spoken claim now depends on the exact 22 mm figure (American cockroach about 4 cm, UF/IFAS IN298).
- Host is the American cockroach, average about 4 cm long ("nearly twice its own length"). https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/IN298
- Two stings: first "a swift sting into the thorax resulting in transient paralysis of the prothoracic legs" ("briefly disables the front legs"), second into the head targeting the brain; the stinger's mechanosensory inputs locate the ganglia; the wasp then "clips the antennae", leads the roach into its burrow "by grasping the truncated antennae", lays a single egg on the host and seals the burrow entrance with debris (Arvidson, Landa, Frankenberg and Adams 2018, J. Hymenoptera Res. 63: 1-13, UC Riverside). https://jhr.pensoft.net/article/21762/
- Stinger sensory organs identify the brain (Gal et al. 2014, PLoS ONE). https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0089683
- Not paralysed; "moves sluggishly"; led "like a dog on a leash"; egg laid on the roach; the roach "doesn't struggle or fight" as the larva feeds. https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/the-wasp-that-walks-cockroaches
- Righting response and swimming remain intact while spontaneous walking/escape drops (hypokinesia). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30293061/ ; https://www.science.gov/topicpages/w/wasp+ampulex+compressa
- Grooming frenzy lasting "a few minutes", giving "the wasp time to finish preparing her burrow". https://nhmu.utah.edu/articles/zombie-wasps-link-parkinsons-research
- Burrow closed "with twigs, leaves, stones etc."; all larvae hatched on day three after oviposition ("within about three days"); roaches dead 7.9 plus or minus 2.1 days after oviposition ("roughly a week after the egg was laid") (Weiss, Parzefall and Herzner 2014, PLoS ONE). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4041758/

## 2. Zombie Ant Fungus
- Ophiocordyceps unilateralis s.l. and carpenter ants (Camponotus leonardi) in a Thai rainforest; nests in the canopy; infected ants descend to the understory. Hughes et al. 2011, BMC Ecology 11:13. https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/1472-6785-11-13.pdf
- Random (not directional) walking, repeated convulsions; death grip on the main (abaxial) leaf vein, synchronised around solar noon; graveyards at about 25 cm above the soil where conditions suit the fungus. Same paper; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/05/110509065536.htm
- Death grip locked by damaged, hypercontracted jaw muscles (Mangold et al. 2019, J. Exp. Biol.). https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/222/14/jeb200683/20797/Zombie-ant-death-grip-due-to-hypercontracted
- Fungal cells throughout the body, surrounding and invading muscle fibres, many interconnected into a network; none inside the brain (Fredericksen et al. 2017, PNAS). https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1711673114 ; https://phys.org/news/2017-11-zombie-ant-brains-left-intact.html
- A stalk grows and releases spores onto foraging ants below. https://phys.org/news/2017-11-zombie-ant-brains-left-intact.html

## 3. Horsehair Worm
- Found as single worms or knotted masses in puddles, swimming pools, animal drinking troughs; called Gordian worms because they twist into knots. https://ipm.ucanr.edu/PMG/PESTNOTES/pn7471.html
- Larvae ingested by crickets/related insects, develop inside; about three months later the host is impelled to seek water and the worm emerges there. Same page.
- Adults about 1/25 inch (1 mm) thick and 1 foot (30 cm) or more long. Same page; https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/horsehair-worms/
- Field cricket about 1 inch (2.5 cm). https://extension.okstate.edu/programs/digital-diagnostics/insects-and-arthropods/field-crickets-gryllus-sp ; https://extension.umd.edu/resource/crickets
- Infected crickets more likely to enter water than uninfected ones (Thomas et al. 2002, J. Evol. Biol.). https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1046/j.1420-9101.2002.00410.x
- Cannot parasitize people, pets, livestock or birds (vertebrates). https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/horsehair-worms/

## 4. Green-Banded Broodsac
Draft 2: "songbirds mostly leave snails alone" cut (fix 3); pulse rate re-sourced and "stop completely in the dark" cut (fix 6).
- Opener "needs a bird to eat a snail ... disguises the snail's eyes as a caterpillar": the pulsating broodsac in the tentacle "resembles like crawling caterpillar which attracts its definite host, insectivorous birds"; birds prey on the snails and the adult lives in the bird. Nandy et al. 2022, J. Environ. Biol. 43: 722-726, University of Calcutta, doi:10.22438/jeb/43/5/MRN-3079. http://jeb.co.in/journal_issues/202209_sep22/paper_13.pdf ; also Wesołowska and Wesołowski 2014 (below).
- Leucochloridium paradoxum, a flatworm; amber snails (Succinea putris); eggs reach snails via bird droppings on vegetation ("eggs leave the bird in its droppings, land on vegetation, and wait for the next amber snail"); swollen, green-banded, translucent broodsacs fill the eye stalks; adult develops in the bird. https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/insects-invertebrates/green-banded-broodsac ; Nandy et al. 2022 (figure: "faecal matter of birds" with Leucochloridium eggs on vegetation).
- "Dozens of times a minute, and faster in daylight than in darkness": in two snail species, Leucochloridium broodsacs beat a mean of about 66 and 99 times a minute by day versus about 31 and 77 at night, a significant light/dark difference (Nandy et al. 2022, above). Different Leucochloridium and snail species from L. paradoxum, so the script gives no exact rate and no temperature claim.
- Infected snails with pulsating broodsacs moved farther, sat higher in vegetation, in more exposed and better-lit places; 53% of infected snails stayed fully exposed for the whole observation versus 28% of healthy ones ("healthy snails usually stay low and hidden"); authors conclude the parasite manipulates host behaviour (Wesołowska and Wesołowski 2014, J. Zool., fieldwork in Poland). https://zslpublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jzo.12094 ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259553764_Do_Leucochloridium_sporocysts_manipulate_the_behaviour_of_their_snail_hosts

## 5. Toxoplasma
- Single-celled parasite; "Over 40 million people are infected with the parasite in the United States"; most healthy people have no symptoms. https://www.cdc.gov/toxoplasmosis/about/index.html
- Infects warm-blooded animals including birds, rodents and humans; felids are the only definitive hosts (sexual reproduction only in cats). https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/toxoplasmosis/index.html ; https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/toxoplasmosis-cats
- Rats have an innate ("instinctive") aversion to cat odour; infected rats lost it and some showed attraction, in an enclosure test with differently scented areas (Berdoy, Webster and Macdonald 2000, Proc. R. Soc. B, University of Oxford). https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2000.1182 ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12316933_Fatal_Attraction_in_Rats_Infected_with_Toxoplasma_gondii
- Effect specific to cat odour; other predator odours, anxiety and fear tests unaffected (Vyas et al. 2007, PNAS). https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0608310104 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1851063/
- No claim is made about behaviour change in humans (brief).

## 6. Blood Flukes
- Larvae released by freshwater snails penetrate skin in contact with infested water; adults live in blood vessels; eggs trapped in tissues cause immune reactions and organ damage; at least 253.7 million people required preventive treatment in 2024; deaths estimated at 14,353 per year; transmission reported from 79 countries; at least 93.9% of those requiring treatment live in Africa. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/schistosomiasis
- Cercariae are forked-tailed, shed their tails on penetration; males 6 to 12 mm, females 7 to 17 mm ("one to two centimeters"); female sits in the male's groove (gynecophoral canal); adults in mesenteric venules. https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/schistosomiasis/index.html
- Penetration takes minutes; adult lifespan estimated 3 to 7 years. https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/infectious-diseases/trematodes-flukes/schistosomiasis
- Adult worms within weeks. https://www.cdc.gov/schistosomiasis/about/index.html

## 7. Kissing Bug
- Triatomine bugs hide in cracks of walls or roofs by day, feed at night, often bite exposed skin such as the face, and defecate near the bite; parasites enter when faeces are smeared into the bite, eyes or mouth; acute phase about two months with absent or mild symptoms in most; up to a third develop cardiac disorders one to three decades later; about 8 million infected, mostly in Latin America; more than 10,000 deaths a year; named after Carlos Chagas, Brazilian physician, who diagnosed the first human case on 14 April 1909, now World Chagas Disease Day. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/chagas-disease-(american-trypanosomiasis)
- Adult triatomines about 0.5 to over 1 inch; US species 0.7 to 1.1 inches (paperclip length). https://www.cdc.gov/chagas/spreads/species.html
- 1909 description. https://www.scielo.br/j/rsbmt/a/khpWcRrFsyq4mRBnSvjmBpx/?lang=en

## 8. Malaria
- Spread by some infected female Anopheles mosquitoes; P. falciparum is the deadliest and most prevalent in Africa; five species cause human malaria; 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths in 2024; African Region about 95% of deaths; children under five about 75% of malaria deaths in the Region; malaria is preventable and curable. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/malaria
- "Kills the most people" on this list: 610,000 a year versus at most about 14,000 for any other item (WHO figures above).
- Only females bite, for the protein needed for eggs; "Smaller species found around houses commonly weigh about 2.5 milligrams" (script: "a typical house mosquito", advisory A1). https://www.mosquito.org/faqs/
- Liver stage, then red blood cell invasion, repeated cycles; falciparum-infected red cells adhere to walls of small blood vessels (sequestration), linked to cerebral (severe) malaria. https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/malaria/index.html ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3699179/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21458590/
- Symptoms fever, chills, flu-like illness. https://www.cdc.gov/malaria/hcp/clinical-features/index.html
- Ronald Ross found the parasite in a mosquito's stomach in 1897; Nobel Prize 1902 "for his work on malaria, by which he has shown how it enters the organism". https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1902/ross/facts/ ; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1902/summary/

## 9. Raccoon Roundworm
- "In some parts of the United States, surveys have found this roundworm in more than four out of five raccoons": CDC DPDx, "Prevalence in raccoons is extremely high in the Northeast, Midwest, and on the West Coast, sometimes in excess of 80%." https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/baylisascariasis/index.html (draft 2, fix 4; the earlier western-US 68% to 82% figure was not in the EID article and is removed).
- A female worm produces more than 100,000 eggs a day and infected raccoons shed millions. https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/31/10/25-0658_article
- Raccoons use communal ("shared") latrines, often close to homes. https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/9/12/03-0039_article
- Egg 80 to 85 by 65 to 70 micrometers (about the width of a human hair, see hair width below); infective after 2 to 4 weeks; humans are accidental hosts infected by ingesting eggs; larvae migrate through tissues including the brain and eyes. https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/baylisascariasis/index.html ; https://www.cdc.gov/baylisascaris/about/index.html
- Human infections rare. https://www.cdc.gov/baylisascaris/about/index.html
- Sixty infections published worldwide, 1984 to 2023 ("four decades"); 8 of 42 neurological cases with known outcome died (19%, "about one in five"); 30 (71%) had residual neurological deficits. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12261942/
- Raccoons living near people and houses. https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/9/12/03-0039_article

## 10. Sleeping Sickness
- Transmitted by tsetse flies, found only in sub-Saharan Africa (blood-feeding); first stage in blood and lymph, second stage invades the central nervous system, disturbing the sleep cycle; gambiense form about 92% of cases, chronic, months or years; rhodesiense acute, weeks to months; without treatment generally fatal; cases peaked near 40,000 in 1998. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trypanosomiasis-human-african-(sleeping-sickness)
- Gambiense form in western and central Africa, rhodesiense in eastern and southern Africa. Same WHO fact sheet.
- Daytime somnolence, night insomnia, total sleep time similar; T. brucei shortens the circadian period in mice and in co-cultured explants/fibroblasts (Rijo-Ferreira et al. 2018, Nature Communications). https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-017-02484-2 ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5754353/
- 663 cases reported in 2020. https://journals.plos.org/plosntds/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pntd.0010047
- With Guinea (January 2025), eight countries validated by WHO for eliminating gambiense HAT as a public health problem. https://www.who.int/news/item/29-01-2025-guinea-eliminates-human-african-trypanosomiasis-as-a-public-health-problem

## 11. Brain-Eating Amoeba
- Naegleria fowleri, an amoeba (single-celled) in soil and warm freshwater lakes, rivers, ponds and hot springs; enters through the nose; cannot infect by swallowing water; not spread person to person; 167 US cases 1962 to 2024, 4 survivors, 97% fatality; fewer than 10 US cases a year. https://www.cdc.gov/naegleria/about/index.html ; https://www.cdc.gov/naegleria/causes/index.html
- Thrives in heat, grows up to 46 °C (115 °F); normally eats bacteria. https://www.health.state.mn.us/diseases/naegleria/index.html ; https://www.cdc.gov/naegleria/about/index.html
- Trophozoites 10 to 35 micrometers, 10 to 15 when rounded; migrates to the brain via the olfactory nerve; rapid course. https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/freelivingamebic/index.html
- Human hair about 70 to 100 micrometers wide ("about five" 15-micrometer cells across). https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?id=106856&ver=2
- Most US exposures in southern-tier states; most recreational-water infections in July to September. https://cdc.gov/naegleria/media/pdfs/naegleria_factsheet508c.pdf ; https://www.cdc.gov/naegleria/data/index.html
- "People are an accident": humans are not part of its life cycle; it is free-living and feeds on bacteria. CDC pages above.
