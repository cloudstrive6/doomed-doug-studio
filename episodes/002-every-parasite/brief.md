## 2. Escalation axis: distance to the viewer (host proximity), then severity
Declared axis for the script header: **"How close the parasite gets to you."** Act 1 parasites only kill insects, snails
and rats, so Doug has to wear a costume to be a host. Toxoplasma is the bridge ("this one is probably in someone watching").
Act 2 is human parasites, ordered by severity (how likely it is to kill an untreated human host), ending on the highest
case-fatality rate. The spoken section headers are "Animal hosts." (optional, before item 1 or folded into the opener) and
**"Human hosts."** before item 6: that is the midpoint zone shift, with the background moving from lime/cream to a
darker, redder palette (art director to define the ramp).

The writer must confirm the severity order in act 2 with sourced figures. If a source contradicts it, reorder rather than
bend the fact. The last item must stay the one with the highest fatality rate.

**Title honesty:** the title promises a death from every parasite. **Every item ends in a cartoon Doug death** (11 items,
11 deaths). In act 1 he dies as the costumed host (which is what really happens to the host). In act 2 the death is the
hypothetical untreated outcome, stated flatly, without symptoms in bodily detail. "Would Feel Like" is delivered as one
short, non-graphic sensation line per item (for example "an urge to climb", "tired at noon, wide awake at midnight"),
never as injury description.

### Act 1: Animal hosts (Doug in costume; the red cap stays on over every costume)
**1. Jewel Wasp** (Ampulex compressa; Doug in a cockroach costume; the hook item)
- Research: two stings, first into the thorax, then into the brain region; the roach is not paralysed but loses the drive
  to escape; the wasp clips its antennae and leads it by one "like a dog on a leash" to a burrow; one egg, and the larva
  feeds inside the living roach (Libersat and Gal; cite a primary or university source).
- Scale analogy: a wasp the size of a paperclip walking a roach several times heavier, like a toddler walking a sofa.
- Doug beat: Doug the roach could run at any time and simply doesn't; he follows the wasp home. Death 8.
- Twist (must land by 0:45, about 145 words): "it isn't paralysed; it just stops wanting to leave". "Doug did not agree to this"
  lands at the costume fitting in the opener.

**2. Zombie Ant Fungus** (Ophiocordyceps unilateralis)
- Research: infected carpenter ants leave the colony, climb, and bite into a leaf vein in a "death grip" at a consistent
  height and time of day (Hughes et al. 2011, BMC Ecology); fungal cells surround and invade the muscles while the brain
  stays largely intact (Fredericksen et al. 2017, PNAS); a stalk grows from the head and rains spores on the ants below.
- Scale analogy: the fungus is a puppeteer that never touches the head, like a marionette worked from the strings, not the puppet.
- Doug beat: Doug the ant, feeling "an urge to climb", bites a leaf and cannot let go. Death 9.

**3. Horsehair Worm** (Nematomorpha)
- Research: the worm grows inside a cricket or grasshopper and can be several times the host's body length; infected
  crickets are more likely to enter water (Thomas et al. 2002, J. Evolutionary Biology), where the adult worm leaves;
  harmless to humans (say so, once, with a source).
- Scale analogy: a worm coiled inside the cricket like 30 cm of phone charger stuffed into a matchbox (confirm lengths).
- Doug beat: Doug the cricket finds himself very attracted to a hotel swimming pool. Death 10 (cartoon splash, cap floats).

**4. Green-Banded Broodsac** (Leucochloridium paradoxum)
- Research: the sporocyst swells into the snail's eye stalks and pulses in green bands, looking like a caterpillar;
  birds are the next host; claims that infected snails move toward light need a source (Wesołowska and Wesołowski 2014,
  J. Zoology), otherwise cut them.
- Scale analogy: eye stalks flashing like an unanswered phone notification.
- Doug beat: Doug the snail's eyes become a disco; he sits in full daylight on top of a leaf. A bird. Death 11.

**5. Toxoplasma** (Toxoplasma gondii; Doug in a rat costume; the bridge item)
- Research: it can reproduce sexually only in cats; infected rodents lose their aversion to cat odour and some show
  attraction to it (Berdoy et al. 2000, Proc. R. Soc. B; Vyas et al. 2007, PNAS); the CDC estimate of how many people in
  the US carry it (use the current CDC figure). **Do not claim** personality or behaviour changes in humans: the evidence is
  disputed, so cut it rather than hedge.
- Scale analogy: the parasite's whole plan is the rat making friends with the one animal built to eat it, like a mouse sending a cat a calendar invite.
- Doug beat: befriend-the-thing gag. Doug the rat walks up to a cat for a hug. Death 12.
- Kicker leads into the header: the cat is not the last host on this list. Then "Human hosts." Costume comes off.

### Act 2: Human hosts (Doug as himself)
**6. Blood Flukes** (Schistosoma; freshwater)
- Research: larvae (cercariae) in freshwater enter through intact skin; adult worm pairs live in blood vessels for years;
  the WHO figures for people needing preventive treatment and the estimated deaths per year (use WHO, cite the year).
- Scale analogy: a larva thinner than a hair, finding Doug in a lake the size of a football field.
- Doug beat: Doug goes for a relaxing paddle in a lake. Years later, in a slow cartoon fade, death 13.
- **No advice about water, swimming or prevention.**

**7. Kissing Bug** (Trypanosoma cruzi, Chagas disease)
- Research: triatomine bugs feed at night, often near the face; the parasite is in the bug's droppings, not its bite;
  the infection can stay silent for decades before heart problems in a share of people (WHO and CDC figures for people
  infected and deaths per year). Say "droppings" once, clinically, no bathroom jokes.
- Scale analogy: a bug the size of a paperclip whose bill arrives twenty years later, like a library fine.
- Doug beat: Doug wakes up, feels fine, and is fine for 20 years. Then he isn't. Death 14.

**8. Raccoon Roundworm** (Baylisascaris procyonis)
- Research: raccoons shed eggs in huge numbers; in accidental human hosts, larvae can migrate to the eyes and brain;
  human cases are rare and severe (CDC case counts). Keep it rare and clinical: this is not a panic item.
- Scale analogy: a single raccoon can shed eggs in numbers bigger than a small city's population (confirm the figure).
- Doug beat: befriend-the-thing, act 2 version. Doug adopts a raccoon named "Buddy". Death 15. Buddy keeps the cap
  for a moment; the cap is recovered.

**9. Malaria** (Plasmodium falciparum; the famous item)
- Research: the parasite is carried by female Anopheles mosquitoes; it travels to the liver, then invades red blood cells;
  falciparum-infected cells become sticky and can block small blood vessels; the latest WHO World Malaria Report for
  cases and deaths, and the share of deaths in children under 5 (state it once, respectfully, no joke near it).
- Scale analogy: the mosquito, lighter than a grain of rice, is the deadliest animal to humans on the planet (only if sourced
  that way; otherwise use the death count alone).
- Doug beat: after everything so far, Doug is killed by the smallest, most boring-looking thing yet. Death 16.
  Narrator tone drops the jokes for this item's kicker (real human toll).

**10. Sleeping Sickness** (Trypanosoma brucei, tsetse fly)
- Research: spread by tsetse flies; the late stage reaches the central nervous system and disrupts the sleep cycle;
  usually fatal without treatment; the WHO case decline (cite the year of the lowest count) is the hopeful kicker.
- Scale analogy: its body clock swap is like jet lag that flies with you on every trip home.
- Doug beat: "tired at noon, wide awake at midnight". Doug falls asleep mid-sentence in his own speech bubble. Death 17.

**11. Brain-Eating Amoeba** (Naegleria fowleri; the boss)
- Research: a free-living amoeba in warm freshwater; it enters through the nose, never by drinking water; it cannot
  spread person to person; CDC US case count since 1962 and number of survivors, which gives the fatality rate (about 97%;
  confirm the exact figure); cases are extremely rare. It is a single cell.
- Scale analogy: a cell smaller than the width of a hair, against the largest organ in Doug's head (keep it at that level).
- Twist: the deadliest thing on the list is one cell, and it wasn't even looking for Doug; people are an accident for it.
- Doug beat: Doug does a cannonball into a warm lake, the most harmless thing he has done all episode. Death 18.
  The final beat is the cap floating alone on the lake. Narrator's kicker is about rarity, not fear.
- **The riskiest item for policy.** One clinical sentence on what it does, zero symptom detail, zero advice.

## 8. Risks
- **Gore (highest risk of any episode so far):** parasites are body horror by nature. Cartoon only: X eyes, ghost Doug,
  floating cap, costume collapsing flat. Never draw or describe anything leaving, entering or growing inside a body
  (no emerging worms, no larva-eating-host visuals, no cysts, no brains). Each "inside the host" mechanism gets one
  clinical sentence and a diagram-level drawing at most (a dotted path on a silhouette). The jewel wasp larva,
  horsehair worm exit, Baylisascaris and Naegleria are the items to watch.
- **Medical/safety advice:** none. No prevention tips, no "see a doctor if", no treatment names or dosages, no statements about
  tap water, neti pots, swimming or pets beyond the sourced mechanism. Saying that treatment exists is allowed; telling people
  what to do is not.
- **Health scare / misinformation:** state rarity where it applies (Baylisascaris, Naegleria). Do not claim Toxoplasma changes
  human behaviour. Do not imply that insect-only parasites (fungus, horsehair worm, broodsac, jewel wasp) can infect humans;
  the costume makes the hypothetical obvious and the narration should say it once.
- **Real human suffering:** malaria, Chagas and sleeping sickness kill real people, mostly in poorer regions, and many are
  children. No jokes about the victims, no jokes in the same beat as a death toll, no regional stereotypes. Doug's deaths
  are the only comedy; the numbers are delivered straight.
- **Kid-appeal:** costumes are a kid-appeal risk. Keep the humour adult-coded and deadpan (the roach's lack of will to live,
  the 20-year library fine, the calendar invite), no silly voices, no nursery words, no "bug buddies" tone.
- **Facts that need strong sources (cut the claim if it can't be sourced):** jewel wasp sting sites and the "not paralysed"
  claim; ophiocordyceps biting height, timing and the muscle-not-brain finding; horsehair worm length and the water-seeking
  study; broodsac light-seeking behaviour; toxoplasma rodent fear loss and the CDC carrier estimate; WHO schistosomiasis,
  Chagas, malaria and sleeping sickness figures (cite the report year); Baylisascaris egg output and human case count;
  Naegleria case and survivor counts. Prefer WHO, CDC, and primary papers over news rewrites.
- **Description length:** the 001 lesson applies. The titler keeps the filled description at 4,800 characters or fewer,
  with at least one institutional or primary source per item.
