## The Ambush

The Ambush.

The Komodo dragon lives on only a handful of islands in Indonesia, mainly Komodo, Rinca and Flores, and most people picture it running its prey down across the grass.

[Map of Indonesia. Three small islands highlighted in red. Label: "KOMODO, RINCA, FLORES".]

It can do that.

[A dragon in a short sprint, dust behind it. Next to it, a stick cyclist pedalling at an easy pace. Label: "UP TO 20 KM/H".]

In a short burst, a Komodo can reach about twenty kilometers an hour, which is a little faster than the average commuter cycling across Copenhagen, but it can only keep this up over short distances.

Here's the problem.

[A game trail through dry grass. Beside it, what looks like a fallen log.]

It rarely needs the sprint at all. The Smithsonian's National Zoo describes dragons spending hours in one spot beside a game trail, waiting for a deer or a wild boar to walk past. Field researchers have found that the largest dragons are the ones most likely to sit and wait, while the medium-sized ones go out looking for food.

[Red arrow to the log. Label: "NOT A LOG".]

So the biggest, most dangerous dragon on the island is usually the one doing the least. It lies low beside the path, it stays perfectly quiet, and it lets the island bring breakfast to it.

It can chase you. It almost never has to, because it started waiting before you arrived.

## The Teeth

The Teeth.

You would expect a giant predator to have a crushing bite. A Komodo dragon's bite is surprisingly weak, and that turns out to be the whole point.

[Close-up of the dragon's mouth, slightly open. A row of curved teeth. Label: "ABOUT 60".]

It has about sixty teeth, flattened like little blades, curved backward and serrated along the edges like a bread knife. The longest are only about two centimeters, shorter than a quarter is wide, and a dragon goes through several full sets of them in its life.

[One tooth, enlarged. An orange stripe along its edge and tip. Label: "IRON".]

In two thousand twenty-four, scientists led by King's College London found that the cutting edges and tips of the teeth are coated in iron, which shows up orange and seems to keep them sharp.

The strange part is the bite itself.

[A dragon skull beside a saltwater crocodile skull of the same size. The crocodile has a big arrow, the dragon a small one. Label: "6.5 TIMES WEAKER".]

In two thousand nine, a team led by Bryan Fry ran a computer model of the dragon's skull. Its bite came out about six and a half times weaker than a saltwater crocodile's with a skull the same size.

[The dragon pulling backward, arrows running down its neck and body. Label: "PULL".]

Instead of clamping down, it bites and pulls back using the muscles of its neck and body. The researchers compared the method to sharks and to sabre-toothed cats, which also paired sharp teeth with a modest bite.

It is not built to hold on to you, because it is built to let you go.

[Doug, with a tiny white bandage on his ankle, walks away whistling. The dragon watches him leave. THE WAIT: 1 S.]

The last time Doug met this lizard, he brought mouthwash. This time he brought a bandage, and he walked away whistling. Doug is fine.

[The narrator's hand presses a button on THE WAIT clock. It starts ticking.]

The dragon let him go on purpose, then it settled down, flicked its tongue and quietly started counting.

Hours.

## The Tongue

The Tongue.

Doug has a head start, and he intends to use it. The dragon does not seem worried, because it can find him without ever seeing him.

[The dragon's head in profile. A long yellow forked tongue flicks out. Label: "SMELL".]

A Komodo dragon smells with its tongue, flicking it out to collect scent from the air and the ground, then pulling it back to a pair of sensing organs in the roof of its mouth. As far as a Komodo is concerned, the whole island is something to taste, and that includes whoever happens to be walking across it.

[The tongue, enlarged, with the two tips spread apart. A red arrow on each side. Label: "LEFT, RIGHT".]

The tongue is long and deeply forked, and scientists think the two tips have separate jobs. Each tip samples a slightly different spot, so the animal can compare left and right and turn toward the stronger smell. The San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance notes that dragons use this sense to find territory boundaries and to track wounded prey.

The worst part is the range.

[A dotted line from the dragon across a wide landscape to a small dead tree on the horizon. Label: "4 KM".]

The Smithsonian's National Zoo says a Komodo can smell carrion up to four kilometers away. That's ten laps of a standard running track, or around fifty minutes on foot for a person walking at an ordinary pace.

[Doug crouched behind a big rock, very still, very pleased with himself. THE WAIT: 50 MIN.]

Doug ran for nearly an hour and hid behind a big rock, convinced that what cannot see him cannot find him. He held his breath.

[A forked tongue curls around the side of the rock. Cut to the rock alone with the cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 180.]

Doug left. His smell didn't. The tongue had found him long before the dragon even bothered to look.

## The Follow

The Follow.

Most predators fail the moment their prey gets away. For a Komodo dragon, the prey getting away is usually just the next step of the plan.

[A deer bounding off through tall grass. The dragon stays put, watching. Label: "RELEASED".]

The bite is fast, the dragon lets go and the animal runs, which doesn't seem to bother the dragon in the slightest, because it has done this many times before.

[A simple pie chart. Big slice: "MINUTES". Smaller slice: "UP TO 4 HOURS". Thin slice: "SURVIVE".]

Ecologist Tim Jessop, who has spent years tracking these dragons in the field, told Smithsonian magazine that about seventy percent of bitten animals go down within minutes. Roughly one in five lasts up to four hours, and only about one in ten survives. Most hunts end quickly, and the ones that don't are where the waiting begins.

[The dragon walking at an easy pace along a winding dotted trail, tongue out. Label: "UP TO 10 KM A DAY".]

During those hours, the dragon simply walks after it, following the scent at its own pace, and adult dragons can cover up to ten kilometers in a day. Field researchers have even photographed an injured deer being stalked by a different dragon from the one that bit it.

That's the unsettling part.

Four hours is longer than the whole of the film Titanic, with time left over for a snack. The prey spends that time running, while the dragon spends it strolling.

[A plain card, no Doug, no drawing: "ATTACKS ON PEOPLE ARE RARE. THEY DO HAPPEN."]

Attacks on people are rare. They do happen.

[Doug jogs along, looks over his shoulder. Nothing. He sits down on a log and unwraps a sandwich. THE WAIT: 4 HR.]

Doug jogged on, checked behind him every few minutes, saw absolutely nothing, and sat down on a log for lunch.

[The dragon walks into frame at the same easy pace. Cut to the log, the sandwich and the cap. DOUG DEATHS: 181.]

It never once sped up, because the longest part of the hunt is a walk, and the dragon is very good at walking.

Days.

## The Swim

The Swim.

If the dragon lives on this island, the obvious answer is to leave the island. The water is warm, the next island is right there, and Doug has an inflatable ring.

[A dragon swimming, only its head and a line of back above the water. Label: "GOOD SWIMMER".]

Komodo dragons can swim, and like other monitor lizards they swim well, so they are comfortable in the water and sometimes seen paddling along the shore.

[Map of Komodo National Park with ten red dots. Label: "TEN SITES".]

So when the same Tim Jessop led a team tracking dragons at ten sites across Komodo National Park, you might expect them to be moving between sites all the time. The study came out in two thousand eighteen, and of all the dragons they caught a second time, only two had moved from one site to another. The team concluded that dragons take to open water only in very rare circumstances, even though they are perfectly able to swim.

[A dragon walking a loop inside one valley, dotted line. Label: "UP TO 10 KM A DAY".]

A dragon can walk up to about ten kilometers a day, roughly a quarter of a marathon, but almost all of it happens inside the same valley. Most of them rarely, if ever, leave the valley they hatched in, and their genes show the populations are becoming somewhat inbred.

Here's the catch.

The dragon does not follow you to the next island. It doesn't have to, because the next island already has its own dragons.

[Doug paddles across on an inflatable ring and plants a tiny flag on a new beach. Speech bubble: "safe". THE WAIT: 1 DAY.]

Doug paddled for a whole day, reached the next island and claimed it with a small flag.

[A different, larger dragon lifts its head from the sand behind him. Cut to the flag and the empty ring floating at the shore, the cap resting inside it. DOUG DEATHS: 182.]

Leaving the island is not leaving the dragons. The island next door came with its own set, already waiting on the beach.

## The Crowd

The Crowd.

Komodo dragons spend most of their lives alone, and they seem to like it that way. Then one of them finds food, and suddenly it is not alone at all.

[A ridge line at dusk. One dragon, then three, then six, appear over it, tongues out.]

Every dragon in the area can smell the same meal. Researchers who watched twenty dragon meals in the wild found that seventy percent of them involved more than one lizard. At a really large animal, tens of dragons can end up sharing the food over several days, and nobody at that table eats slowly. San Diego Zoo has recorded dragons eating about two and a half kilograms of meat in a single minute.

[A crowd of dragon heads. Label: "MORE THAN A FOOTBALL TEAM".]

That's more dragons around one meal than there are players on a football team. Nobody counts the guests.

The disturbing part is that they have rules.

[A stack of dragons drawn by size, the biggest on top with a tiny crown. Label: "BIGGEST FIRST".]

When food is shared, the dominant dragon eats first, typically the largest male. Smaller males come next, then the females, and the young come down from the trees only once the adults have left. When two big males disagree, they settle it with ritual combat.

[Two big dragons rearing up on their hind legs, chest to chest, wrestling. Label: "RITUAL COMBAT".]

So one bite does not end the hunt, it sends out a signal that the whole neighborhood answers.

[Doug walks over the ridge carrying a tiny paper plate. Speech bubble: "can I help?" A dozen heads turn toward him. THE WAIT: SEVERAL DAYS.]

Doug saw the gathering and came over to help. He did not ask where he was supposed to stand in the line.

[Cut away to the empty ridge. DOUG DEATHS: 183.]

He arrived as a guest, and somewhere near the top of the hierarchy, someone decided he was the menu instead.

## The Buffalo

The Buffalo.

The water buffalo is the biggest prey on these islands. People brought it here a few centuries ago, and nobody asked the dragons what they thought about it.

[A huge water buffalo, side-on. Next to it, a concert grand piano. Label: "400 KG OR MORE".]

Even the smallest domestic buffalo breeds weigh around four hundred kilograms, close to the weight of a concert grand piano. The biggest dragons in the field studies weigh around ninety, and while dragons are used to tackling prey as heavy as themselves, or heavier, a buffalo is several of them stacked together.

The dragons attack them anyway.

[The buffalo walks away from a dragon with a confident expression. Label: "ESCAPED".]

Here's where it turns.

The dragon usually loses. Fry's team say buffalo almost always escape the first attack, and Smithsonian magazine adds that dragons seldom succeed in killing animals this large.

[Many dragon heads around an off-screen shape, cut off at the frame edge. Label: "SEVERAL DAYS".]

And yet buffalo are still on the menu. Researchers have recorded groups of dragons feeding on them together, a meal that can go on for several days. Buffalo do not live forever, and when one finally lies down for good, the dragons are never far away.

It never has to win the fight, it only has to outlast the buffalo.

[Doug stands next to the buffalo with a small sign: "MORAL SUPPORT". The dragon looks at the buffalo. Then it looks at Doug. A page-a-day calendar flips. THE WAIT: SEVERAL DAYS.]

Doug stood beside the buffalo for several days, holding up his sign in solidarity. The dragon studied the two of them very carefully.

[Cut to black. Then the grass, the sign and the cap. The buffalo is gone, grazing far away. DOUG DEATHS: 184.]

The buffalo was a long-term project. Doug was a short one, and he did not weigh four hundred kilograms.

## The Meal

The Meal.

A predator that rarely hunts needs a good reason to stop hunting. For a Komodo dragon, the reason is that a single meal can be enormous.

[The dragon beside a scale. The scale reads "80%". Label: "IN ONE MEAL".]

The Smithsonian's National Zoo says an adult can eat up to eighty percent of its own body weight in one sitting. It eats fast, and it wastes very little of what it catches.

[A stopwatch reading "17 MIN". Beside it, fifteen big water bottles in a row. Label: "31 KG".]

The San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance records a fifty-kilogram female swallowing a thirty-one-kilogram wild boar in seventeen minutes. That's about the weight of fifteen big two-liter bottles of water, gone in less time than a lunch break.

Then the dragon does nothing at all.

[A heavy, bloated dragon lying flat on a warm rock in the sun. Label: "DIGESTING".]

How fast it digests depends on the temperature, so the dragon lies out in the sun to warm up, and a run of cool nights can delay digestion by up to five days. That's a full working week, spent lying on a rock.

What makes it worse is the logic. It doesn't need to hunt often, because every time it does eat, it eats like it might never get the chance again.

[Doug lays out a picnic blanket with a basket, sandwiches and a thermos. Speech bubble: "peace?" THE WAIT: 5 DAYS.]

Doug brought a full picnic as a peace treaty, and the dragon accepted the basket, the blanket, the sandwiches and the thermos.

[The dragon walks away, slightly larger. Doug stands alone on the bare grass, holding one napkin. DOUG DEATHS: 184 holds.]

Doug is fine. The picnic is not. The thermos was not technically food, and the dragon did not see that as a problem.

Weeks.

## The Fast

The Fast.

Most large hunters need to eat often, or they quickly stop being large hunters. A big Komodo dragon can sit out weeks between meals as if it were nothing.

[A wall calendar. One deer drawn on a single square each month. Label: "ONE A MONTH".]

According to the San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance, a large Komodo kills about one large animal a month, usually a deer or a wild boar. The rest of the time, it digests, it basks in the sun, and it waits. One wild dragon that a Smithsonian reporter watched was still digesting a small deer it had eaten earlier that week.

[A second calendar, every square crammed with tiny plates. Label: "1,095".]

Compare that with you. A person eating three meals a day gets through more than a thousand meals a year. A large dragon gets by on about a dozen big ones, plus the odd small snack.

[The dragon on a sunny rock, a little sun icon pointing at it. Label: "SOLAR POWERED".]

Being cold-blooded is a big part of the trick, because a dragon doesn't burn food to keep its body warm the way we do. It soaks up heat from the sun and from warm surfaces instead.

But that's not the disturbing part.

[The dragon lying on a rock in the sun, eyes half-closed, watching a deer path in the distance.]

An animal that only has to land one big meal a month can afford to wait for any one of them. It never has to take a bad chance, and it can let a hundred easy chances walk right past, because the next one is already on its way down the path.

[Doug walks past a large, bloated, basking dragon and waves. Speech bubble: "hi friend?" The dragon doesn't move. THE WAIT: 1 MONTH.]

Doug walked straight past a resting dragon and waved hello, and it did not even turn its head.

[Doug walks away, unharmed. DOUG DEATHS: 184 holds.]

Doug survived. The narrator would like it noted, with some disappointment, that the dragon was full, not friendly.

Months.

## The Nursery

The Nursery.

A Komodo dragon begins life very small, and very much on its own. The first thing it learns is that the adults are not family. They are the danger.

[A nest mound in the dirt, with a second, fake mound beside it. Label: "UP TO 8 MONTHS".]

A female lays around twenty eggs, which can take up to eight months to hatch, and some females dig decoy nests nearby, to put off predators, including other dragons.

[A tiny hatchling next to a tennis ball. Label: "ABOUT 80 G".]

A hatchling weighs about eighty grams, less than two tennis balls, while the biggest adult males can weigh more than a hundred kilograms.

[A young dragon scrambling straight up a tree trunk. Label: "UP".]

The moment it hatches, a young dragon climbs the nearest tree, where it lives on insects. A radio-tracking study on Komodo Island found that the smaller the dragon, the more of its life it spent up in the trees.

Here's the grim part.

[An adult dragon at the foot of the tree, looking up. Label: "TOO HEAVY TO CLIMB".]

Adults eat young dragons. The Smithsonian's National Zoo says it plainly: large Komodos cannibalize young ones. The adults are too heavy to climb after them, so the young stay up for months, until they grow too big for the branches themselves.

The first lesson in a Komodo's life is that the adults wait at the bottom of the tree. The second lesson is that they are very patient about it.

[Doug climbs a tree to escape an adult below. On the branch above him sits a young dragon, lean, hard-eyed and not at all cute. THE WAIT: MONTHS.]

Doug climbed the tree to escape the adult, the one sensible thing he did all episode. The young dragon was already up there, and it was tired of insects.

[Cut to the branch with only the cap on it. DOUG DEATHS: 185.]

The tree is the only safe place on the whole island. It is only safe if you happen to be a dragon.

Years.

## The Virgin Birth

The Virgin Birth.

It doesn't need to chase its prey. It turns out that, in the right situation, a Komodo dragon doesn't strictly need a partner either.

[A zoo enclosure sign: "CHESTER ZOO". A female dragon beside a clutch of eggs. Label: "NO MALE".]

In two thousand six, keepers at Chester Zoo in England noticed that a female named Flora had laid a clutch of eggs, even though she had never been kept with a male.

[A second sign: "LONDON ZOO". Four young dragons. Label: "OVER 2 YEARS".]

At London Zoo, another female, Sungai, had produced four healthy young more than two years after her last contact with a male. That's longer than an elephant's pregnancy, which lasts up to about twenty-two months.

The surprising part is what the genetics showed.

[A simple diagram: one parent icon, arrow, egg. Label: "ONE PARENT".]

Researchers from the University of Liverpool ran genetic tests on the eggs and the young, and in the journal Nature they reported the same answer for both females. In each case, the mother was the only parent, which is called parthenogenesis, or virgin birth.

[Two chromosome letters: "Z Z". Label: "ALWAYS MALE".]

Because of how sex chromosomes work in Komodo dragons, every offspring made this way is male. The researchers suggested that a lone female stranded on an empty island could, in theory, still start a population. The same trick has turned up in other animals that end up isolated.

[Doug arrives at a nest mound holding a bunch of flowers. Speech bubble: "hello?" The eggs hatch beside him without any help. THE WAIT: 2+ YEARS.]

Doug arrived at the nest with flowers, and the eggs hatched while he was still introducing himself. Doug was not required.

[Doug stands there holding the flowers. DOUG DEATHS: 185 holds.]

Doug survived, but only in the technical sense, because nobody here was waiting for him, and nobody ever had been.

## The Last Giant

The Last Giant.

For a long time, scientists thought the Komodo dragon was a strange island accident, an ordinary lizard that reached these islands and slowly grew huge.

[A scientist stick figure holding a small lizard on an island, with a big arrow toward a giant dragon. Label: "ISLAND GIANT?" A red X stamps over it.]

In two thousand nine, a team led by paleontologist Scott Hocknull showed that this was wrong. Fossils from eastern Australia, some of them about four million years old, belonged to the Komodo dragon itself.

[Map: Australia on the right, an arrow sweeping west to the islands. Label: "FROM AUSTRALIA".]

The dragon was already a giant when it arrived, so it was not an island experiment at all, but a survivor from a lost world of giant lizards.

[A huge monitor lizard silhouette towering over a dragon. Label: "MEGALANIA".]

Its relatives included Megalania in Australia, the largest lizard known to science. A conservative estimate puts it at more than five and a half meters long and at least five hundred seventy-five kilograms, heavier than a typical adult horse.

[The Megalania silhouette goes grey, then fades. Label: "GONE".]

Megalania is gone, and so are the other giant monitors, which leaves the Komodo as the one that lasted. On Flores, its body size has stayed about the same for nine hundred thousand years, through major turnovers in the animals living around it. The same paper also rejected the idea that it grew big by specializing in hunting the small elephant relatives of Flores, called Stegodon.

[A timeline, very long, with a tiny sliver at the end. Label: "WRITING".]

Four million years is about eight hundred times as long as humans have been writing anything down.

Here's the part it can't wait out.

[The islands on a map, the coastline creeping inland. Label: "ENDANGERED, 2021".]

In twenty twenty-one, the International Union for Conservation of Nature moved the Komodo dragon from Vulnerable to Endangered. Fewer than one thousand four hundred adult dragons are left. In the most extreme projections, rising seas could take up to seventy-one percent of their habitat within forty-five years.

It outwaited every other giant monitor lizard. The one thing it cannot wait out is the sea rising around its islands.

[Doug sets up a folding chair on a rock, facing the dragon, with THE WAIT clock on his lap. Speech bubble: "I can wait too". The dragon lies down opposite. Day, night, day, night. Doug yawns.]

Doug decided to beat the dragon at its own game, so he brought a folding chair and the clock and settled in to outlast it.

[Doug's head nods forward. Cut to the empty chair and the cap. DOUG DEATHS: 186. THE WAIT: ABOUT 4,000,000 YR.]

Doug lasted nine hours, and the dragon has been doing this for about four million years.

[Closing image: the tide edging up the beach, the dragon lying perfectly still, the cap on the folding chair. THE WAIT clock keeps ticking. Cut to the field guide KOMODO page. A red "NO" is stamped in the FRIEND? box.]

It waited out the giants. It will have no trouble at all waiting for the next Doug.
