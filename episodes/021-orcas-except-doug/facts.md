## 1. Chinook Salmon
- Resident orcas off Washington and British Columbia eat fish, mainly salmon; Southern Residents prefer Chinook (Fetched, NOAA): https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/killer-whale
- Chinook is the largest Pacific salmon; typical mature fish about 3 ft and 30 lb (Search, NOAA Fisheries): https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/chinook-salmon . "About a meter, around fourteen kilograms" = 0.91 m, 13.6 kg.
  - Comparison: 2 bowling balls = 14.5 kg.
- Residents select Chinook; smaller sockeye and pink salmon are not significant prey despite far greater abundance: Ford & Ellis 2006, Marine Ecology Progress Series 316:185-199, as summarised in DFO documents (Search): https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/csas-sccs/publications/resdocs-docrech/2009/2009_101-eng.htm ; https://dfo-mpo.gc.ca/csas-sccs/Publications/resdocs-docrech/2005/2005_041-eng.htm
- Canadian government (DFO) scientists attribute the Chinook preference to its large size, high lipid content and year-round occurrence (Search, DFO Research Document 2009/101, Ford, Wright, Ellis & Candy; also 2005/042): https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/csas-sccs/publications/resdocs-docrech/2009/2009_101-eng.htm ; https://dfo-mpo.gc.ca/csas-sccs/Publications/resdocs-docrech/2005/2005_042-eng.htm
- Southern Residents endangered (ESA, 2005); main threats include a shortage of Chinook (Fetched, NOAA): https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/killer-whale
- UBC study (lead author Fanny Couture, PLOS ONE 2022): for six of the last 40 years, Southern Residents did not have enough to eat (Fetched, The Weather Network / CBC report): https://www.theweathernetwork.com/en/news/nature/animals/bcs-endangered-killer-whales-have-been-going-hungry-for-years-study ; https://www.cbc.ca/1.6503594
- Harbor seals are common in the same waters, and Bigg's orcas there eat them and are doing well (Search, KUOW): https://www.kuow.org/stories/why-are-these-killer-whales-increasingly-showing-up-in-the-salish-sea
- Researchers have never confirmed a resident orca eating a marine mammal: Ford et al. 1998, Canadian Journal of Zoology 76:1456-1471, abstract: residents sometimes harassed marine mammals but no kills were confirmed and no mammal remains were found in stomachs of stranded residents (Search): https://www.sealifebase.se/References/FBRefSummary.php?id=80661 ; DOI 10.1139/z98-089
- Script wording check: "They would rather go hungry than change the menu" is the narrator's summary of the two facts above (hungry in some years; never confirmed eating mammals). It is not presented as a researcher's claim about motive.

## 2. Sea Otter
- Floats on its back, puts food on its chest, uses rocks as tools and stores them under its forelimbs (Fetched, Alaska Department of Fish and Game): https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=seaotter.main
- Densest fur of any mammal, 800,000 to one million hairs per square inch (Fetched, ADF&G, same page; NPS: https://www.nps.gov/chis/learn/nature/sea-otter.htm)
- Adult males 80-100 lb (36-45 kg), some more; females 50-70 lb (Fetched, ADF&G, same page).
  - Comparison: 45 kg / 7.26 kg = 6.2 bowling balls, "roughly six".
- Otters fell by about 25% a year during the 1990s across large parts of western Alaska; in Kuluk Bay, Adak Island, down 76% from 1993 to 1997 ("about three out of every four ... in four years") (Fetched, ScienceDaily on Estes et al. 1998, Science 282:473-476): https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/1998/10/981016075816.htm ; paper: https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/1008002
- Researchers had never seen orcas feeding on otters before 1991; it took Estes about two years to become convinced orcas were responsible; Estes linked it to a collapse in the orcas' usual prey, seals and sea lions (Fetched, ScienceDaily, same page). Framed in script as a proposal ("proposed a suspect", "it seems").
- In Clam Lagoon, inaccessible to killer whales, otter numbers stayed stable (Fetched, ScienceDaily, same page).
- As few as four killer whales feeding exclusively on otters could have driven the decline over a large area (Fetched, ScienceDaily, same page).

## 3. Harbor Seal
- Harbor seals weigh 180 to 285 lb as adults (82 to 130 kg) (Fetched, NOAA Fisheries): https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/harbor-seal
  - Comparison: 130 / 7.26 = 17.9, "nearly eighteen bowling balls". A small adult (82 kg) is about Doug's fictional 80 kg.
- Bigg's (transient) orcas prey on marine mammals; in Ford et al. 1998, over half of observed attacks were on harbour seals (Search, abstract): https://www.sealifebase.se/References/FBRefSummary.php?id=80661
- Marine mammals have excellent underwater hearing and can detect killer whale calls from several kilometres; seal-hunting groups vocalise rarely, mostly after a kill (Search, Deecke, Ford & Slater 2005, Animal Behaviour 69:395-405): https://mmru.ubc.ca/wp-content/pdfs/Deecke2005.pdf
- Playback experiments: harbour seals responded strongly to mammal-eating killer whale calls but not to the familiar calls of the local fish-eating population (Search, Deecke, Slater & Ford 2002, Nature 420:171-173): https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v420y2002i6912d10.1038_nature01030.html
- 1972: an orca bit a surfer at Point Sur, California (Fetched, Wikipedia, gives 9 September 1972): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orca_attacks ; "the orca quickly let go"; Erich Hoyt, after interviewing the surfer, said the whale may have mistaken him for a seal (Fetched, Live Science report reposted on Yahoo News): https://www.yahoo.com/news/killer-whales-attack-boats-off-133856646.html ; original https://www.livescience.com/animals/how-often-do-orcas-attack-humans
- He survived: Wikipedia records his injuries were treated (no details used in script) (Fetched, same Wikipedia page).
- "Most maintain that this remains the only fairly well-documented instance of a wild orca biting a human" (Fetched, Wikipedia, same page). The script says "still described as", which is how the source frames it.
- No name and no injury details in the script, as the brief requires.

## 4. Dolphin
- The orca is the largest member of the dolphin family; up to 32 ft and 11 tons (Fetched, NOAA): https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/killer-whale . "Almost ten meters" = 9.75 m.
  - Comparison: 22,000 lb / 4,372 lb = 5.0 average new cars.
- Pacific white-sided dolphin, adults 300 to 400 lb (136-181 kg), "up to about one hundred and eighty kilograms" (Fetched, NOAA): https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/pacific-white-sided-dolphin
- Off BC, killer whales are the only predators that regularly kill Pacific white-sided dolphins (Search, CBC): https://www.cbc.ca/1.5021585
- Over 400 photo-identified dolphins (415); healed killer whale scars ~8x more common than fishery scars, 3.9% vs 0.5% ("roughly one in every twenty-five" = 3.9%); one kill reported in BC 1973-1996, 14 more events 1996-2011 (Search, Ashe et al. 2021, Frontiers in Marine Science): https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2021.606876/full
- Dall's porpoise "possibly the fastest of all dolphins and porpoises"; killer whales hunt them by cooperatively blocking their escape route to open water in a bay (Fetched, NOAA feature): https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/dalls-porpoise-expands-territory-changing-prince-william-sound
- Transients in Ford et al. 1998 were seen taking six marine mammal species (Search, abstract): https://www.sealifebase.se/References/FBRefSummary.php?id=80661

## 5. Stingray
- New Zealand orcas frequently feed on rays in shallow water off the North Island (Search, Visser 1999, Marine Mammal Science 15:220-227): https://2025.orcaresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Visser-1999-benthic-foraging-by-orca.pdf ; eagle rays and short-tailed stingrays in the Bay of Islands (Search, Visser 2005): https://www.orcaresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Visser-2005-thresher-hammerhead-orca.pdf
- Rays live on shallow sandy beaches where New Zealand orcas hunt them; orcas sometimes strand doing it; Visser has watched them get stuck and get themselves off (Search, Orca Research Trust): https://www.orcaresearch.org/rescues-strandings/
- Elsewhere orcas eat fish, seals and dolphins; NZ orcas are "the only group known to eat stingrays, eagle rays and electric rays as a staple food" (Search, Te Ara, the Encyclopedia of New Zealand, Ministry for Culture and Heritage): https://teara.govt.nz/en/orcas/page-2
- Short-tail stingray is the largest stingray species: at least 2.1 m wide, 4.3 m long, 350 kg (Search, Wikipedia; FishBase for 430 cm length): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short-tail_stingray ; https://fishbase.org/summary/6381
  - Comparison: 2.1 m across vs a 2.03 m door.
- Stingray tail has barbed stingers with venom-containing grooves in a sheath (Search, Medscape, Stingray Envenomation): https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/772683-overview
- 23 November 1998: a sub-adult female orca found dead near the Noises Islands, Hauraki Gulf; necropsy concluded she had probably died from penetration of a stingray spine (Fetched, Whale Rescue / Orca Research Trust): https://www.whale-rescue.org/rescues/entry/1998-november-23-orca-noises-islands-hauraki-gulf ; Duignan et al. 2000, Aquatic Mammals 26(2):143-147.
- The orcas kept hunting rays after 1998: Visser 2005 reports ray foraging observed in 2002 (Search, same Visser 2005 link).

## 6. Moose
- ADF&G: adult bulls in prime condition 1,200-1,600 lb (542-725 kg); cows 800-1,300 lb (Search, ADF&G moose PDF): https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/moose.pdf
  - Comparison: 725 / 1,983 = 0.37, "more than a third of an average new car".
- Moose swim across big water such as coves and between islands; researchers say they swim for aquatic vegetation for its sodium (Fetched, Cowboy State Daily, quoting ADF&G spokesman Riley Woodford and researchers): https://cowboystatedaily.com/2024/12/30/wyoming-moose-fear-wolves-but-coastal-moose-have-to-watch-out-for-killer-whales
- 1993, Icy Strait, southeast Alaska: two fishermen saw three or four killer whales attack and kill one of a pair of moose; the second later drowned (Fetched, quoting Ford & Ellis 1999, Transients: Mammal-Hunting Killer Whales, UBC Press): https://themarinedetective.com/2013/03/02/oh-deer-a-rare-meal-for-mammal-eating-killer-whales/ . Note: Cowboy State Daily gives 1992 for what appears to be the same event; the script uses the book's 1993.
- ADF&G spokesman: killer whale predation on moose "is rare, but it does happen, and it's been documented"; transients, not fish-eating residents, are the likely hunters (Fetched, Cowboy State Daily, same link).
- Off the east coast of Vancouver Island, several killer whales were seen circling a small rock where two deer had taken refuge (Fetched, Marine Detective quoting Ford & Ellis 1999, same link).

## 7. Great White Shark
- Adult white sharks generally weigh 520-770 kg (Search, Guinness World Records): https://GuinnessWorldRecords.com/world-records/largest-predatory-fish
- A big female can top a tonne: a 4.9 m female killed by orcas near Gansbaai in May 2017 was estimated at 1,110 kg (Fetched, DIVE Magazine): https://divemagazine.com/scuba-diving-news/orcas-kill-great-whites-in-south-africa . (The Gansbaai event itself is not narrated; 008 used it.)
  - Comparison: 1,110 / 1,983 = 0.56, "more than half the weight of an average new car".
- Port and Starboard, named for their collapsed dorsal fins, linked to white shark kills off South Africa (Fetched, ScienceDaily, March 2024): https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/03/240302171519.htm
- All recorded incidents before June 2023 involved two or more orcas (Search, Daily Maverick): https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2024-03-24-scientists-detail-first-recording-of-single-orca-killing-white-shark
- 18 June 2023, south of Seal Island, Mossel Bay: Starboard alone killed a juvenile white shark about 2.5 m long (Fetched, Daily Maverick, same link); within about two minutes; first recorded single-orca kill of a white shark (Fetched, ScienceDaily, same link). Filmed by researchers and tourists on two vessels (Search, Towner et al. 2024, African Journal of Marine Science 46(1):1-5, DOI 10.2989/1814232X.2024.2311272): https://www.nisc.co.za/products/abstracts/37235/further-insights-into-killer-whales-orcinus-orca-preying-on-white-sharks-carcharodon-carcharias-in-south-africa
- 1997, Farallon Islands: an orca held a white shark upside down for 15 minutes, likely inducing tonic immobility, "whether intentional or not" (Search, Shark Trust): https://www.sharktrust.org/tonic-immobility ; Farallon 1997 event context (Search, National Wildlife Federation): https://www.nwf.org/Magazines/National-Wildlife/2004/Showdown-at-Sea . ## 9. Gray Whale Calf
- Newborn calves about 14-16 ft and about 2,000 lb (907 kg); adults about 49 ft and about 90,000 lb (40.8 t); about 10,000 miles (16,000 km) round trip, one of the longest migrations of any mammal; killer whales prey on gray whales (Fetched, NOAA): https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/gray-whale
  - Comparison: 907 / 1,983 = 0.46, "roughly half an average new car"; 40,800 / 1,983 = 20.6, "twenty cars".
- Unimak Island, Alaska: more than 150 transient killer whales gather during the gray whale northbound migration; most attacks on calves or yearlings, by groups of 3-4; attackers broke off when mothers defended aggressively; gray whales moved toward shallow water along the shore, and attacks ended when whales reached water 3 m deep or less (Search, Barrett-Lennard et al. 2011, Marine Ecology Progress Series 421:229-241, abstract): https://int-res.com/abstracts/meps/v421/p229-241
- May 2012, Monterey Bay: orcas attacked a gray whale mother and calf; two humpbacks were already there and about 14 more arrived (about 16 in all); the calf died; the humpbacks stayed six and a half hours, slashing at the orcas with flippers and tails (Fetched, National Geographic): https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/humpback-whales-save-animals-killer-whales-explained
- Review of 115 humpback-killer whale interactions; when humpbacks interfered, 89% of the prey were other species (Search, Pitman et al. 2017, Marine Mammal Science 33(1):7-58): https://research.usfq.edu.ec/en/publications/humpback-whales-interfering-when-mammal-eating-killer-whales-atta/
- "For the first time on this list, something made the orcas back off": narrator framing of the facts above (mothers made attackers break off; humpbacks harassed them).

## 10. The Law of the Tongue
- Killer whales seen near Eden between 1840 and 1930; they herded baleen whales into Twofold Bay and alerted the whalers (Fetched, Wikipedia, citing Clode 2002 and the ABC documentary): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killer_whales_of_Eden,_New_South_Wales . Partnership from the 1840s to the station's closure in 1929 (Search, Lit Hub): https://lithub.com/law-of-the-tongue-when-humans-and-killer-whales-hunt-together
- Old Tom alerted whalers by tail slapping at the mouth of the Kiah River; sometimes took the line in his jaws to tow boats; wear on his lower teeth shows this; died 17 September 1930; skeleton prepared by George Davidson and his son Wallace, which led to the Eden Killer Whale Museum (Fetched, Eden Killer Whale Museum): https://killerwhalemuseum.com.au/old-tom/
- The Davidsons' cottages were near the mouth of the Kiah River (Fetched, Wikipedia, same page).
- Law of the tongue: whalers left the carcass overnight so the orcas could eat the tongue and lips (Fetched, Wikipedia; Eden Magnet: carcass tied to a buoy overnight): https://www.edenmagnet.com.au/story/2563131/old-tom-anniversary-of-the-death-of-a-legend/
- Skeleton on display at the Eden Killer Whale Museum, with wear marks from ropes on his teeth (Fetched, Wikipedia, same page; museum page).
- George Davidson was a third-generation whaler (Fetched, Wikipedia, citing SMH 2010). "Roughly ninety years" = 1840 to 1930.
- "They treated us as partners, not prey": Wikipedia calls it a rare example of mutualism between humans and killer whales (Fetched).
