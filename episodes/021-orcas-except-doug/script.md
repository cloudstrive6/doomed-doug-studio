## Chinook Salmon

Chinook Salmon.

Off the coasts of Washington and British Columbia lives a family of orcas that eats almost nothing but fish, and it has a favorite.

[A big silver salmon, side-on. Label: "CHINOOK". Beside it, two bowling balls on a scale. THE MENU gets its first line: "CHINOOK SALMON, 14 kg".]

The Chinook is the biggest salmon in the Pacific, and a typical adult is about a meter long and weighs around fourteen kilograms, roughly the weight of two bowling balls.

[A pod of orcas, tall straight fins, cruising past a crowd of smaller salmon. Red arrow to one big Chinook.]

The orcas here are called residents, and researchers who followed them for years found that they hunt Chinook on purpose. Smaller salmon pass through the same water in far greater numbers, and the orcas mostly let them go.

[Doug, zipped into a salmon costume, red cap on top, floating very upright.]

Orcas don't hunt people. They hunt salmon. Which is exactly why Doug is dressed as one.

Here's the strange part.

[Map of the Salish Sea. Label: "ENDANGERED". A thin, tired orca icon.]

The southern population of these orcas is endangered, and United States government scientists name a shortage of Chinook as one of its main threats. A University of British Columbia study found that in six of the last forty years, they simply didn't get enough to eat. Canadian government scientists think they favor Chinook for its size, its fat and the fact that it is around all year.

[Harbor seals bob on the same map. A second orca icon with a different fin shape swims among them. Label: "SEAL EATERS".]

Meanwhile, harbor seals swim through the same waters, and a different population of orcas eats them all the time. Researchers have never confirmed a resident orca eating a seal, or any other marine mammal.

They would rather go hungry than change the menu.

[Doug in the salmon costume waves at the resident pod, hopeful smile. The pod turns. Splash. The cap floats alone. DOUG DEATHS: 171.]

Doug waved at the pod, hoping to be ignored, and he was not ignored.

Doug did not agree to this.

They took the salmon, and they had no particular opinion about Doug.

## Sea Otter

Sea Otter.

A sea otter spends much of its life floating on its back, cracking shellfish open with a rock it keeps tucked under its arm.

[An otter on its back, rock on its chest. Label: "DENSEST FUR". A close-up circle: "1,000,000 HAIRS PER SQUARE INCH". THE MENU: "SEA OTTER, 45 kg".]

It has the densest fur of any mammal, up to about a million hairs in a single square inch, and a big male weighs around forty-five kilograms, roughly six bowling balls.

[Map of the Aleutian Islands, a long chain. Otter icons disappear one by one. Label: "MINUS 25% A YEAR".]

In the nineteen nineties, sea otters around Alaska's Aleutian Islands started to vanish. Across large parts of western Alaska, their numbers fell by about a quarter every year. In one bay on Adak Island, about three out of every four otters disappeared in four years.

But this is the odd part.

[A scientist stick figure with glasses and a clipboard. A big red "?" next to an orca fin.]

The team that studied it, led by the ecologist James Estes, proposed a suspect nobody expected. Orcas. Before nineteen ninety-one, the researchers had never seen orcas eating otters there, and it took Estes about two years to become convinced.

[Seal and sea lion icons fade out. A red arrow from the orca swings across to the otters.]

Estes linked the change to a collapse in the orcas' usual prey, seals and sea lions, and when that food ran short, it seems the orcas looked elsewhere. Meanwhile, in a lagoon the orcas couldn't reach, the otters stayed perfectly stable.

So orcas will change the menu, when the ocean changes it for them.

[Doug in an otter costume, on his back, a rock on his chest, eyes closed, deeply relaxed. A fin passes. The rock floats alone with the cap on it. DOUG DEATHS: 172.]

Orcas here had started eating otters, so naturally, Doug is an otter. He floated there, perfectly at peace, until a fin went by and the rock was wearing the cap.

The researchers calculated that as few as four orcas eating nothing but otters could explain the decline across an enormous stretch of coastline.

Doug-sized.

## Harbor Seal

Harbor Seal.

From here, the animals on the menu are roughly the size of Doug, and the orcas that hunt them are a different kind entirely.

[A harbor seal on a bathroom scale. Doug on a second scale beside it. Both read about "80". THE MENU: "HARBOR SEAL, 82-130 kg".]

An adult harbor seal can weigh up to about one hundred and thirty kilograms, nearly eighteen bowling balls, and a small adult weighs about the same as Doug.

[Orcas with tall, pointed fins slipping past a rocky shore. Label: "BIGG'S ORCAS".]

Sharing the coast with the salmon eaters are Bigg's orcas, and they hunt marine mammals. In one long study off British Columbia, more than half of the attacks researchers saw were on harbor seals.

[A seal underwater with huge cartoon ear rings. Sound waves arrive from far off.]

Seals have excellent underwater hearing and can pick up orca calls from kilometers away. In playback experiments, harbor seals reacted strongly to the calls of mammal-eating orcas, but ignored the familiar calls of the local fish eaters.

[The Bigg's orcas swim with a big red "SHH" label above them.]

So the seal hunters mostly keep quiet. Researchers found that they rarely call while hunting and save most of their noise for after a kill.

Here's the problem with Doug's plan.

[Sober card: plain dark blue, white text only. "POINT SUR, CALIFORNIA, 1972". No Doug.]

In nineteen seventy-two, an orca bit a surfer off Point Sur, California, but it let go quickly, and he survived. The researcher Erich Hoyt, who interviewed him, thought the whale may have mistaken him for a seal.

It's still described as the only fairly well-documented case of a wild orca biting a person, and even then, the orca let go. So it isn't that we're the wrong size.

[Back to the sea. Doug, in the seal costume, paddles out on a surfboard, hopeful smile. A large orca rises, takes the surfboard in its mouth, pauses, and spits it out. Doug floats beside a board with a neat curved bite outline. DOUG DEATHS: 172, unchanged.]

Doug paddled out in the seal costume, and an orca took the surfboard, considered it for a moment, and gave it back.

Doug is fine.

We had been very confident about this one. The orca, apparently, had not.

## Dolphin

Dolphin.

Dolphins are fast, social and clever, which sounds like exactly the kind of animal that shouldn't end up on anyone's menu.

[A huge orca beside a small car, then five small cars in a row. Label: "11 TONS". THE MENU: "DOLPHIN, 180 kg".]

Technically, the orca is a dolphin, the largest member of the whole family. It can grow to almost ten meters long and weigh up to eleven tons, about as much as five average new cars.

[A Pacific white-sided dolphin, sleek, with grey and white stripes. Label: "PACIFIC WHITE-SIDED".]

Pacific white-sided dolphins weigh up to about one hundred and eighty kilograms, and they share the coast with Bigg's orcas. Off British Columbia, orcas are the only predators known to kill them regularly.

[Photo-ID style grid of dolphin fins. Some fins have little red tick marks. Label: "ORCA SCARS".]

Researchers who photographed more than four hundred of these dolphins found healed orca scars about eight times more often than scars from fishing gear. Actual kills are rarely seen, and between nineteen seventy-three and nineteen ninety-six, only one was reported off British Columbia, although fourteen more were seen over the next fifteen years.

[A bay mouth. Three orcas line up across it, blocking the exit. Porpoises circle inside.]

Dall's porpoises are possibly the fastest of all dolphins and porpoises. So Bigg's orcas hunt them in bays, working together to block their escape into open water.

The unsettling part is this.

The largest dolphin in the ocean eats dolphins, and in one long study, Bigg's orcas were seen taking six different species of marine mammal. Being clever doesn't protect you, and neither does being family.

[Doug, in a dolphin costume, swims up to an orca. Speech bubble: "hi cousin?". Hard cut to empty blue water. The cap drifts. DOUG DEATHS: 173.]

Doug swam up in the dolphin costume and greeted the orca as family, but the orca did not see the resemblance.

Doug has died. Again.

Off British Columbia, roughly one in every twenty-five of these dolphins is carrying orca scars.

## Stingray

Stingray.

You'd think a predator would skip any animal that comes with its own built-in weapon.

[Shallow turquoise water over sand, right by a beach. Orca fins in water barely deeper than they are. Label: "NEW ZEALAND".]

New Zealand's orcas don't. They hunt rays in very shallow water, close to sandy beaches, including eagle rays and short-tail stingrays. Elsewhere in the world, orcas eat fish, seals and dolphins, but according to Te Ara, New Zealand's government encyclopedia, these orcas are the only group known to eat rays as a staple food.

[A short-tail stingray, huge and flat, next to a standard door. The ray is wider than the door is tall. THE MENU: "STINGRAY, 350 kg".]

The short-tail stingray is the largest stingray species. It can measure about two point one meters across, wider than a standard door is tall, and weigh up to three hundred and fifty kilograms.

[Close-up of the ray's tail. A yellow warning triangle next to the spine. Label: "VENOM".]

Its tail carries a barbed spine, with venom held in grooves along it.

The worst part is what happened in nineteen ninety-eight.

[Plain sea-blue card. A small orca silhouette lying on its side. Label: "HAURAKI GULF, 1998".]

A young female orca was found dead near the Noises Islands in the Hauraki Gulf. At the necropsy, vets concluded she had probably died after a stingray spine penetrated her.

[Back to the shallows. A beached orca outline, then a red arrow showing it wriggling back out.]

The orcas kept hunting rays anyway, even though hunting this close to shore is risky enough that New Zealand orcas sometimes strand themselves doing it. Researchers have watched the same orcas get stuck, then work themselves free.

So it isn't that we're too dangerous to bother with.

[Doug buried in the sand in a stingray costume, only his eyes and cap showing, perfectly still. The sand ripples. Then only the cap is showing. DOUG DEATHS: 174.]

Doug buried himself in the sand, with only his eyes and the cap showing, and waited. Then the sand moved, and only the cap was showing.

The ray brings venom to the fight, and the orcas treat it as packaging.

Bigger.

## Moose

Moose.

A moose is a land animal, which ought to keep it well away from anything that hunts at sea.

[A bull moose on a scale, next to a third of a car. Label: "UP TO 725 kg". THE MENU: "MOOSE, 725 kg".]

A big bull moose in Alaska can weigh up to about seven hundred and twenty-five kilograms, more than a third of an average new car.

[Map of southeast Alaska, islands and narrow channels. A moose icon swimming across, antlers above the water.]

Moose are strong swimmers, and along Alaska's coast they cross big stretches of water, between islands and across coves. Researchers think part of the draw is water plants, which give moose the sodium they crave.

Here's where it goes wrong.

[Same map. Label: "ICY STRAIT, 1993". Two moose icons mid-channel. Three dorsal fins approaching. Cut away before contact.]

In nineteen ninety-three, in Icy Strait in southeast Alaska, two fishermen watched three or four orcas attack a pair of swimming moose. The orcas killed one of them, and the other didn't survive either. Two leading researchers of these orcas, John Ford and Graeme Ellis, describe it in their book on the mammal hunters.

[An official stick figure in a peaked cap, holding a clipboard. Label: "RARE, BUT DOCUMENTED".]

An Alaska Department of Fish and Game spokesman described this kind of predation as rare, but documented. He said the roving, mammal-hunting orcas were the likely culprits, not the local fish eaters.

[A tiny rock off Vancouver Island. Two deer huddled on it. Orca fins circling.]

Off Vancouver Island, orcas have also been seen circling a small rock where two deer had taken refuge.

So being a land animal stops counting the moment you get in the water.

[Doug in a moose costume swimming between two islands, antlers held high, chin up, looking proud. A fin appears behind him. Then the antlers float alone, with the cap resting between them. DOUG DEATHS: 175.]

Doug swam between two islands in the moose costume, looking more dignified than he ever has, right up until the antlers floated on without him.

Two fishermen, one channel, and a moose that only wanted to reach the next island.

## Great White Shark

Great White Shark.

Ask most people to name the most dangerous animal in the sea, and this is the one they'll pick.

[A great white, side-on, filling the frame. Label: "OVER 1 TONNE". A small car outline behind it, half shaded. THE MENU: "GREAT WHITE, 1,100 kg".]

Adult great whites typically weigh between five hundred and seven hundred and seventy kilograms. A big female can top a tonne, more than half the weight of an average new car.

[Map of South Africa. Two orcas with flopped-over dorsal fins. Labels: "PORT" and "STARBOARD".]

Off South Africa, a pair of orcas known as Port and Starboard, named for their collapsed fins, has been linked to killing great whites. Every recorded kill there had involved two or more orcas.

[Doug, in the shark costume, swimming a slow, serious patrol.]

Since orcas now eat great whites, Doug is, of course, a great white.

But here's the unbelievable part.

[Mossel Bay map, a pin south of Seal Island. Label: "18 JUNE 2023". A timer reads "2:00".]

On the eighteenth of June, two thousand twenty-three, near Seal Island in Mossel Bay, Starboard did it on his own. Researchers and tourists on two boats filmed him take a juvenile white shark about two and a half meters long, in roughly two minutes. It was the first recorded case of a single orca killing a great white.

[A Farallon Islands map. An orca holding a shark upside down. Label: "TONIC IMMOBILITY".]

There's an older trick as well, from nineteen ninety-seven, when an orca off California's Farallon Islands was seen holding a white shark upside down for about fifteen minutes. Flipping a shark over can put it into a trance-like state called tonic immobility, and that is likely what happened.

So the most feared predator most people can name lasted about two minutes against one orca.

[An orca nudges Doug's shark costume and flips it upside down. Doug goes very still, X eyes. The flat costume drifts, cap still on top. DOUG DEATHS: 176.]

An orca turned Doug upside down, and he went very still and stayed that way.

One orca. One shark. About two minutes. Starboard didn't even need his partner.

## Whale Shark

Whale Shark.

The whale shark is the biggest fish alive, a slow, gentle filter feeder that cruises along swallowing plankton.

[A whale shark covered in white spots, next to an orca outline. The shark is nearly twice as long. Label: "UP TO 18 m". THE MENU: "WHALE SHARK, 6 m".]

Adults can reach around eighteen meters, almost twice the length of the biggest orca.

[Map of Mexico, the Gulf of California highlighted. Four small camera icons. Label: "2018 TO 2024".]

Between two thousand eighteen and two thousand twenty-four, researchers documented four orca attacks on whale sharks in the southern Gulf of California, using photos and video from scientists and members of the public. One of the sharks was a juvenile about six meters long, three doors laid end to end.

[Three orcas around a whale shark. A red curved arrow shows the shark being rolled belly up. Cut away.]

The orcas worked as a team, striking the shark until it turned upside down. The researchers point out that young whale sharks are more vulnerable to predators than adults are.

Here's what makes this one different.

[An orca with a distinctive fin. Label: "MOCTEZUMA". A second orca beside him, labelled "?".]

An adult male the researchers call Moctezuma appears in three of the four attacks. A female seen with him before joined one of them. The researchers suggest there may be a specialized group of shark hunters living in the region.

So a brand-new prey can end up on the menu. Somebody just has to work it out first.

[Doug in a polka-dot whale shark costume, mouth wide open, contentedly filtering plankton. The costume slowly rolls belly up. X eyes. Cap on top. DOUG DEATHS: 177.]

Doug cruised along with his mouth open, minding his own business and filtering plankton, until the costume rolled over and Doug stopped minding anything.

The biggest fish in the ocean, and only a handful of orcas seem to know it's food.

Giants.

## Gray Whale Calf

Gray Whale Calf.

From here on, everything on the list is a whale, and whales are much harder to push around.

[Colder, deeper blue. A gray whale mother and calf swimming together. Labels: "CALF: 900 kg" and "MOTHER: 40 TONNES". Car outlines: half a car next to the calf, twenty cars next to the mother. THE MENU: "GRAY WHALE CALF, 900 kg".]

A newborn gray whale weighs about nine hundred kilograms, roughly half an average new car. Its mother can weigh about forty tonnes, the same as twenty cars.

[Map of the west coast of North America. A dotted line from Mexico to Alaska. Label: "16,000 km ROUND TRIP".]

Gray whales make one of the longest migrations of any mammal, about sixteen thousand kilometers there and back. Each spring, mothers and calves head north along the coast, and Bigg's orcas wait for them.

[Map of Unimak Island, Alaska. A crowd of orca fins. Label: "150+".]

At Unimak Island in Alaska, researchers watched more than one hundred and fifty of these orcas gather during the migration. Most attacks were on calves or yearlings, by small groups of three or four orcas.

Here's where the list changes.

[The mother gray whale swings her tail between the calf and the orcas. A red "!" above her.]

The mothers fight back, and when they defended their young aggressively, the orcas broke off. Gray whales also run for the shoreline, and once a whale reached water about three meters deep, the attack ended.

[Monterey Bay map. Label: "MAY 2012". Humpback icons arrive one after another until there are about sixteen.]

Sometimes, strangers turn up. In May two thousand twelve, in Monterey Bay, California, orcas attacked a gray whale mother and her calf. Humpback whales arrived, around sixteen in total, and although they were too late for the calf, they spent six and a half hours harassing the orcas anyway, slashing at them with flippers and tails.

[A field-notes page: "115 ENCOUNTERS". A tick next to "OTHER SPECIES".]

A review of over a hundred encounters found that humpbacks often charge in to break up orca attacks, even when the target is a completely different species.

[Doug in a gray whale calf costume, tucked beside a very large mother. Orca fins approach. Two humpbacks crash in, splashing. The orcas leave. Doug, hopeful smile. DOUG DEATHS: 177, unchanged. The narrator's pencil writes at the bottom of THE MENU: "DOUG, 80 kg".]

Doug stayed close to a very large mother. The orcas came, two humpbacks crashed in, and the orcas left. Doug is fine. We are slightly disappointed, and for completeness, we have added Doug to the menu.

For the first time on this list, something made the orcas back off, and some of it came to help a stranger.

## The Law of the Tongue

The Law of the Tongue.

This is the closest any orca has come to treating people as anything other than background.

[An old sepia-style map of the New South Wales coast. Pin on Twofold Bay. Label: "EDEN, 1840s TO 1930".]

From around the eighteen forties until about nineteen thirty, at Twofold Bay near Eden in New South Wales, Australia, a group of orcas worked with shore whalers.

[Orca fins herding a large whale silhouette into a bay. Then one orca at a river mouth, slapping the water with its tail. Label: "ALARM".]

The orcas herded baleen whales into the bay. Then one of them would swim to the mouth of the Kiah River, where the whalers lived, and slap the water with its tail to call the boats out.

[A big male orca with a rope in his teeth, towing a wooden boat. Label: "OLD TOM".]

The best known of them, a male called Old Tom, would sometimes grab a boat's rope in his teeth and tow it. The rope wear is still visible on his teeth.

[A calm bay at night. A buoy in the water. Label: "THE LAW OF THE TONGUE".]

In return, the whalers kept what became known as the law of the tongue. They left the catch in the water overnight, and the orcas took the tongue and lips.

[Three stick figures in a row, each a little older-looking. Label: "3 GENERATIONS".]

The arrangement lasted roughly ninety years, long enough for three generations of one whaling family, the Davidsons. Old Tom was found dead in the bay in September nineteen thirty, and the whalers preserved his skeleton.

And this is the important part.

Orcas have hunted alongside people before, and for close to a century, they treated us as partners, not prey.

[Doug in a wooden whaleboat, oar in hand, no costume, red cap. An orca surfaces, picks up the trailing rope in its teeth, and tows him along helpfully. Doug, hopeful smile. DOUG DEATHS: 177, unchanged.]

The last time Doug met orcas in a boat, they took the rudder. This time, one picked up his rope in its teeth and towed him across the bay, very helpfully.

Doug is fine.

Old Tom's skeleton is still on display at the Eden Killer Whale Museum, rope marks and all.

## Blue Whale

Blue Whale.

This is the largest animal that has ever lived, and there is nothing bigger left to hunt.

[Open ocean at dusk, deep navy, one shaft of light. A blue whale so long it runs off both edges of the frame. Label: "150 TONNES". A row of car outlines underneath, then a crowd of tiny Dougs. THE MENU: "BLUE WHALE, 150,000 kg".]

A blue whale can weigh up to about one hundred and fifty tonnes, the same as roughly seventy-five average new cars, or nearly nineteen hundred Dougs.

[Map of southwestern Australia. Three pins. Labels: "2019 ADULT", "2019 CALF", "2021 JUVENILE".]

Off southwestern Australia, researchers recorded orcas killing blue whales three times between two thousand nineteen and two thousand twenty-one. The first was a healthy adult, two weeks later came a calf, and in two thousand twenty-one, a juvenile.

[Orca fins swarming around the giant. Label: "FEMALES LEAD". Then a wide shot: the sea full of fins. Label: "ABOUT 50".]

When the researchers reached the first hunt, around fourteen orcas were attacking, with females leading. Around fifty orcas ended up in the area, sharing the meal. At least sixteen of the same orcas took part in all three hunts.

[A small inset of the Eden bay map from the last item, with a dotted line to this one.]

The researchers saw the orcas go for the tongue first, the same share the whalers of Eden used to leave them.

So here is the last clue.

[THE MENU card fills the screen. Every line so far, in order, with "DOUG, 80 kg" in pencil at the bottom.]

It isn't size, because they'll take the largest animal that has ever lived. It isn't danger, because venomous rays and great whites don't stop them. And it isn't hunger, because some of them would rather go hungry than change the menu.

[A mother orca with a younger orca beside her, a line of small fish icons between them. Label: "LEARNED".]

The leading explanation is about families. United States government scientists say each orca population's diet is driven mainly by hunting tactics that are learned. The researcher Erich Hoyt calls orcas fussy eaters, very conservative about whatever they learned from their mothers. In his view, people were simply never part of anyone's learned diet.

[Doug in a blue whale costume so enormous that only the red cap pokes out of the blowhole. Orca fins arrive from every direction. Cut to open ocean. The cap floats alone in the shaft of light. DOUG DEATHS: 178.]

Doug wore the biggest costume we have ever made, so big that only the cap stuck out of the blowhole, and then the orcas arrived.

Doug has died. Again.

[Dusk. Doug, no costume, in the tiny boat from the thumbnail, wearing a spare cap. An orca surfaces beside him, looks, and swims away. It turns, comes back, and drops a fish into the boat. Doug looks at the fish. The orca waits.]

Later, Doug went back out in a tiny boat, with no costume at all. An orca surfaced, looked at him and swam off, then came back and dropped a fish into his boat.

[Inset: a world map with pins in California, New Zealand, Norway and Patagonia. Label: "34 OFFERS".]

That part is real. A study published in two thousand twenty-five collected thirty-four cases from the past two decades of wild orcas offering food to people, from California to New Zealand, Norway and Patagonia. In all but one, the orcas waited to see what the people would do.

[A single fin crosses THE MENU, striking through "DOUG, 80 kg" in one stroke. Cut to the field guide ORCA page. A red "MAYBE" is stamped in the FRIEND? box.]

They hunt everything in the ocean. Doug, they tried to feed.

[DOUG DEATHS: 178 holds on screen.]

Doug deaths: one hundred and seventy-eight. He still hasn't eaten the fish. What should Doug dress up as next? Tell us in the comments.
