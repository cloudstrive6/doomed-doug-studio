## 2. Escalation axis and items
**Axis: prey size, from a fish lighter than Doug to the largest animal that has ever lived.** Each item is ordered by the typical
size of the animal the orcas actually take, which may be a calf or a juvenile. The writer sources a weight for every item and puts it
in the script header table. If two items swap order, reorder them and report it. The boss stays the blue whale. The spoken section
headers are new to the channel:
- **"Snacks."** (opener, items 1-2)
- **"Doug-sized."** (before item 3)
- **"Bigger."** (before item 6)
- **"Giants."** (before item 9, at about 70%)

**The why-thread (the title promise).** The question is why the predator that kills all of these has no record of ever killing a person
in the wild. Each item's twist rules out one easy answer or adds one clue, so the viewer collects the answer as the list goes on:
- it isn't hunger (item 1);
- it isn't that they never change their menu (item 2);
- it isn't that we're the wrong size (item 3);
- it isn't that we're clever or related (item 4);
- it isn't that we're dangerous (items 5 and 7);
- it isn't that we're land animals (item 6);
- they can learn new prey (item 8);
- other animals stand up to them (item 9);
- they have worked with people before (item 10);
- size is no limit at all (item 11).

The answer the scientists give (item 11 and the closing) is attributed, never asserted: orcas eat what their family taught them to eat,
and no family has taught a taste for people. Every "why" hypothesis must be attributed to a named researcher or a paper, in words
such as "the leading explanation" or "researchers think". If it can't be attributed, cut it.

**Rules:**
- Every number needs a source. The writer verifies each one; if a fact fails, cut it, don't hedge it.
- Predation is always off-screen, with no blood (not even cartoon red on prey animals), no bite descriptions and no carcass. Deaths are
  Doug in costume only: splash, X eyes, flat costume, ghost Doug, floating cap. The orca never visibly bites Doug.
- Deaths: 8, numbered 171 to 178 (the counter starts at 170). No milestone bits.
- Costume rule: Doug wears the costume of the item's prey and plays it straight, with the red cap on top. The narration says why once,
  in item 1 ("Orcas don't hunt people. They hunt salmon. Which is exactly why Doug is dressed as one."). After that, vary the line and
  use it at most twice more.

Background ramp: the North Pacific in grey-green, then kelp green, open blue, colder southern blue and deep navy; the boss is the open
ocean at dusk with one shaft of light. The art director defines it. **Episode-only device, proposed: "THE MENU"**, a crude
restaurant-style menu card in the corner. Each prey animal is added as a line when its item starts (handwritten, with its weight). At
the start of "Giants." the narrator writes "DOUG, 80 kg" at the bottom in pencil. At the closing an orca's fin "crosses it out" (one
stroke). It must be monotonic (lines are only ever added). The art director may cut it if the screen gets busy; the death counter
always stays.

### Snacks.

**1. Chinook Salmon** (hook item; death 171; "Doug did not agree to this.")
- Facts:
  - The fish-eating "resident" orcas of the northeast Pacific feed mostly on salmon and strongly prefer Chinook, the largest salmon
    (Ford & Ellis 2006, *Marine Ecology Progress Series*; verify the share).
  - The Southern Residents are endangered, and the main threat named is a shortage of Chinook (NOAA Fisheries; verify the status
    and the wording).
  - Mammal-eating Bigg's orcas hunt seals in the same waters, and residents have not been seen eating marine mammals (Ford et al.
    1998, *Canadian Journal of Zoology*; verify that it is "not recorded", not "never").
- Analogy idea: a big Chinook against a sourced everyday weight (a small dog, a car tyre). Verify the typical weight.
- Twist (must land by 0:45, about 145 words): **some orca families would rather go hungry on salmon than eat the seals swimming past
  them.** The first clue is that it isn't hunger.
- Doug beat: Doug in the salmon costume, waving at a resident pod ("Doug did not agree to this."). Splash, cap floats. Death 171.
  Kicker: they took the salmon. They had no opinion about Doug.

**2. Sea Otter** (death 172)
- Facts:
  - In the Aleutian Islands, sea otter numbers fell sharply in the 1990s, and researchers proposed orca predation as the cause
    (Estes et al. 1998, *Science*; verify the percentage and that it is framed as a hypothesis).
  - The proposed reason is that the orcas' usual prey had declined, so they switched to otters (same paper; later debate is fine in one
    clause if sourced).
  - Sea otters float on their backs, use stones as tools and have very dense fur (verify the fur claim if it is used).
- Analogy idea: an adult otter's weight against a sourced everyday object (a large suitcase, a child's bike is not allowed: no child
  references). Verify it.
- Twist: **orcas will change the menu when the ocean changes.** So it isn't that they never try anything new.
- Doug beat: Doug floats on his back in the otter costume with a rock on his chest, very relaxed. A fin passes. Cap on the rock. Death
  172. Kicker: an animal that weighs 30 kg (verify) was enough to change one pod's whole diet.

### Doug-sized.

**3. Harbor Seal** (Doug survives; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - Bigg's orcas hunt harbor seals and keep quiet while they hunt, which researchers link to seals being able to hear their calls
    (Deecke, Ford & Slater 2005, *Animal Behaviour*; verify).
  - A harbor seal weighs about as much as an adult man (sourced weight range).
  - The one well-documented case of a wild orca biting a person: a surfer at Point Sur, California, in 1972, who was bitten and then
    released. The usual explanation is that he was mistaken for a seal (verify with a strong source; **no name, no injury details**, one
    flat sentence).
- Analogy idea: seal and Doug on a bathroom-scale style display (both about 80 kg). Verify the seal range.
- Twist: **the one time an orca grabbed a person, it seems to have let go.** So it isn't that we're the wrong size.
- Doug beat: Doug in the seal costume on a surfboard. The orca mouths the surfboard, pauses, and spits out the board. Doug floats there
  with a bitten-board outline. "Doug is fine." The counter stays at 172. The narrator: one dry line about having been sure about this
  one. Don't reuse "had the death drawn already" or "sharpened the pencils".

**4. Dolphin** (death 173)
- Facts:
  - The orca is the largest member of the dolphin family (Delphinidae; NOAA or the Smithsonian).
  - Mammal-eating orcas hunt other dolphins and porpoises, for example Dall's porpoises in the Pacific Northwest (Ford et al. 1998;
    verify the species).
  - Orcas have large brains and complex social learning (a sourced comparative figure if used; otherwise state it with no number).
- Analogy idea: the orca's length against Doug's dolphin costume and a sourced everyday object (a small car).
- Twist: **the ocean's biggest dolphin eats dolphins.** So being clever, or being a relative, doesn't protect you.
- Doug beat: Doug in the dolphin costume clicks "hi cousin?" at the orca. Cut away. Death 173. Don't reuse 019's "cousin?" family-photo
  shot; the speech bubble only.

**5. Stingray** (death 174)
- Facts:
  - New Zealand orcas specialise in hunting rays, including stingrays and eagle rays, sometimes digging them out of the sand in shallow
    water (Visser 1999, *Marine Mammal Science*; verify).
  - A stingray's tail spine is venomous (sourced).
  - At least one New Zealand orca was found dead with stingray barbs embedded in it (Duignan et al. 2000, or Visser's work; verify, cut
    if unsourced). One flat line, no wound detail.
- Analogy idea: the width of a large short-tail stingray against a sourced object (a dining table, a door). Verify the size, and keep
  this item after the dolphin only if the sourced weight supports it.
- Twist: **it carries a venomous spine, and they hunt it anyway.** So it isn't that we're dangerous.
- Doug beat: Doug buried in sand in the stingray costume, with only the eyes and the cap showing. The sand moves. Death 174. Cap on the
  sand.

### Bigger.

**6. Moose** (death 175)
- Facts:
  - Orcas have been recorded killing land mammals swimming in the sea. Moose (Alaska) and deer (British Columbia) are the examples to
    verify. The writer finds a primary or agency source; if the moose isn't sourced, swap the item to "Deer" and change the weight
    and ordering.
  - Moose swim well and cross water between islands (sourced).
- Analogy idea: a moose's weight against a sourced everyday weight (a grand piano, a small car).
- Twist: **being a land animal stops counting the moment you swim.** So it isn't that we live on land.
- Doug beat: Doug in a moose costume swims between two islands, antlers above the water, looking proud. A fin. Antlers float. Death 175.

**7. Great White Shark** (death 176)
- Facts (none of them used in 008):
  - In 2023 off Mossel Bay, South Africa, a single orca known as Starboard was filmed killing a juvenile white shark on its own, in
    under two minutes (Towner et al. 2024, *African Journal of Marine Science*; verify the time and the location).
  - Orcas have been seen holding sharks upside down, which can put sharks into a still state called tonic immobility (verify that the
    link to orcas is sourced, e.g. Pyle et al. 1999 Farallon account, otherwise cut).
  - A great white can weigh more than a tonne (sourced).
- Analogy idea: the shark's weight against a sourced everyday object (a small car, a cow). Verify it.
- Twist: **the ocean's most famous predator, taken by one orca, faster than Doug can make toast.** (Only if the time is verified.)
- Doug beat: Doug in the 008 shark costume (`shark_costume.json`, played as the same costume) patrolling, very serious. The orca flips
  him upside down. He goes still with X eyes. Death 176. No contact with the costume shown beyond the flip, no red. Shorts note: this
  is the follow-up to our best Short.

**8. Whale Shark** (death 177)
- Facts:
  - In the Gulf of California, orcas have been documented hunting whale sharks, the largest fish alive, by working together and flipping
    them (Higuera-Rivas et al. 2024, *Frontiers in Marine Science*; verify the number of events and the method).
  - The records involve young whale sharks. Adults can be about as long as a bus (sourced length for juveniles and adults).
  - Researchers linked the behaviour to one particular group of orcas (verify; cut if the paper doesn't say so).
- Analogy idea: the juvenile length against a sourced everyday length (a minivan, a school bus).
- Twist: **a single pod worked out how to hunt the biggest fish in the sea.** So orcas can learn a brand-new prey, and someone has to
  teach it first. This is the key clue for the boss.
- Doug beat: Doug in a polka-dot whale shark costume, mouth open, filter-feeding contentedly. The costume rolls over. Death 177.

### Giants.

**9. Gray Whale Calf** (Doug survives; narrator quietly disappointed)
- Facts:
  - Bigg's orcas hunt gray whale calves during the spring migration, for example in Monterey Bay and off Unimak Island, Alaska
    (verify the sources, e.g. Barrett-Lennard et al. 2011, *Marine Ecology Progress Series*).
  - Gray whale mothers defend their calves and may lead them into shallow water (verify).
  - Humpback whales have been recorded interrupting orca attacks on other species, including gray whale calves (Pitman et al. 2017,
    *Marine Mammal Science*; verify that gray whales are included).
- Analogy idea: a newborn gray whale against a sourced everyday weight (a car, a hippo would be a self-callback, don't use it).
- Twist: **the giants fight back, and some of them come to help strangers.** The first time anything stops the orcas.
- Doug beat: Doug in a gray whale calf costume, a mother whale beside him. The orcas come. Two humpbacks arrive, splashing. The orcas
  leave. Doug survives. The counter stays at 177. The narrator writes "DOUG, 80 kg" on THE MENU right after ("for completeness").

**10. The Law of the Tongue** (Doug survives; the one 008 callback)
- Facts:
  - At Twofold Bay, Eden, New South Wales, from the 1800s to the early 1900s, a group of orcas worked with shore whalers: they herded
    baleen whales into the bay and alerted the whalers (Eden Killer Whale Museum; Australian Museum or NSW sources; verify the dates).
  - In return, the whalers left the dead whale's tongue and lips for the orcas, the "law of the tongue" (verify). One clinical clause;
    no description of the whaling itself.
  - The skeleton of the best-known orca, Old Tom, is in the Eden Killer Whale Museum (verify). The orca's name is fine because it is a
    wild animal, not a captive one.
- Analogy idea: the length of the partnership against a sourced everyday span (a human lifetime, four generations).
- Twist: **orcas have hunted with people before. They treated us as partners, not food.** It is the strongest clue so far.
- Doug beat: no costume. Doug in a wooden whaleboat with an oar. The 008 callback: "The last time Doug met orcas in a boat, they took
  the rudder." This time an orca tows his rope, helpfully. Doug survives. The counter stays at 177. Keep the whaling neutral and
  historical. No hunting of whales by Doug, no harpoon on screen, and no moral.

**11. Blue Whale** (the boss; death 178)
- Facts:
  - Off Western Australia (Bremer Bay region), orcas have killed blue whales, the largest animals that have ever lived, three times
    between 2019 and 2021, with dozens of orcas involved (Totterdell et al. 2022, *Marine Mammal Science*; verify the number of orcas
    and the year range). State it once as a record. Don't present it as new news (008 did that).
  - Orcas that hunt baleen whales often eat the tongue first (same paper or a sourced review; this ties back to item 10; verify, cut if
    unsourced). One clinical clause.
  - A blue whale can weigh more than 100 tonnes (sourced).
- Analogy idea: a blue whale's weight against a sourced everyday object (about N school buses, using a sourced bus weight; or "as heavy
  as 1,000+ Dougs" if the arithmetic is from sourced numbers).
- Twist (the episode's thesis, attributed): **they will hunt the largest animal that has ever lived, and they ignore an 80 kg man in a
  boat. The leading explanation is not size, danger or hunger. Each orca eats what its family taught it, and no family has ever been
  taught a taste for people.** Use the researchers' words and cite them (Ford, Visser, Giles or the papers above, whichever supports
  it). Hard rule: don't claim anything stronger than the source.
- Doug beat: Doug in a blue whale costume so big that only the cap pokes out of the blowhole. The orcas arrive. Cut to the open ocean.
  Death 178. The cap floats.
- **Closing image:** dusk. Doug, no costume, in the tiny boat from the thumbnail. An orca surfaces next to him, ignores him, then turns

  feed. The field guide ORCA page: FRIEND? gets a red "MAYBE" (the survival stamp from 010).

## 7. Risks
- **Kid-appeal risk (MEDIUM-HIGH):** orcas are a classic kids' animal (aquariums, cartoons, plush toys). Countermeasures:
  - Dry adult narrator. The jokes are about Doug's choices and the narrator's indifference.
  - Orcas are drawn powerful and never cute: no smiles, no babies on screen, no squeaky sound effects.
  - No captivity anywhere, which also removes the aquarium associations.
  - Costumes are played deadpan (series bible: no mascot voices, no cute framing).
  - Metadata never targets kids.
- **Safety advice (HIGH):** no "what to do if orcas approach your boat", no "stay calm", no "orcas are safe to swim with". Never imply
  orcas are harmless to people. The claim is always the narrow sourced one ("no record of killing a person in the wild"), and the 1972
  bite is in the script, so the video doesn't overclaim. Description line: "Entertainment and education only. Do not approach wild
  animals." (the titler owns it).
- **Gore (HIGH for this topic):** predation is the subject. **No blood, no wounds, no carcasses, no feeding scenes**, including on real
  photos. The tongue appears twice as one clinical clause each (items 10 and 11). The great white on the thumbnail is held whole with no
  red. Doug's deaths are off-screen or a splash cut. No real attack photos. Animal photos only with a verified licence.
- **Human incidents (MEDIUM):** only the 1972 Point Sur bite, with no name, no injuries, and no joke on that line (a sober
  card, no Doug, as in 012/017/018). Doug's seal beat comes after the sober card and is clearly separate.
- **Whaling history (MEDIUM):** Eden is history and is told neutrally. No whaling imagery beyond a boat and a rope, no moral, no joke
  about the whales' deaths.
- **Over-claiming the "why" (HIGH):** every explanation is attributed and hedged as the researchers hedge it. Banned claims: "orcas
  know humans are intelligent", "orcas respect humans", "orcas have a rule against eating people", "humans taste bad to orcas"
  (unsourced pop explanations; they may appear only as an idea being rejected, if it is sourced as a popular claim).
- **Assets:** the director files these in `asset_requests.md`.
  - costumes (each with a `_flat` variant): salmon, sea otter, dolphin, stingray, moose, whale shark (polka dot), gray whale calf, blue
    whale (huge, blowhole opening)
  - a resident and a Bigg's orca pose (if `orca.json` lacks them): breaching, surfacing with a fish, fin only
  - surfboard and bitten-board outline (cartoon, no gore)
  - a wooden whaleboat with an oar and a rope
  - a mother gray whale and two humpbacks (check `blue_whale.json` / `whale_skeleton.json` for reuse)
  - an otter rock, the THE MENU card, and an orca with a great white held whole (for the thumbnail)
- **Facts that need strong sources (cut if unsourced):**
  - Chinook Salmon: Ford & Ellis 2006 preference, NOAA status and threat, residents not eating mammals.
  - Sea Otter: Estes et al. 1998 decline figure and hypothesis.
  - Harbor Seal: Deecke et al. 2005 silent hunting, the seal weight, the 1972 Point Sur bite and its explanation.
  - Dolphin: Delphinidae, porpoise and dolphin prey.
  - Stingray: Visser 1999, venomous spine, the barbs case.
  - Moose: a swimming-moose (or deer) predation record.
  - Great White Shark: Towner et al. 2024 Starboard kill and time, tonic immobility link.
  - Whale Shark: Higuera-Rivas et al. 2024.
  - Gray Whale Calf: Bigg's predation sites, mother defence, Pitman et al. 2017 humpback interference.
