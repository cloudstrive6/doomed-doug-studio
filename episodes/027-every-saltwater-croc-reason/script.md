The Dive.

[Bright tidal river, brown-green water, mudbanks, a few mangroves. The surface is perfectly flat. Doug on the bank.]

## The Skin

The Skin.

[Mangrove shade, darker green. Close on a crocodile's jaw. Tiny dark dots cover the scales. Red arrow: "SENSORS".]

Look closely at a crocodile's jaw and you'll see that it's covered in tiny dark dots, which look like freckles but are something much more useful.

[The dots light up one by one along the jaw, then along the body.]

Each dot is a small dome packed with touch sensors. A two thousand twelve study counted around four thousand of them on an alligator, mostly on its jaws and inside its mouth. Nile crocodiles had about nine thousand, because in true crocodiles the domes cover almost every scale on the body.

[A stick-figure fingertip beside a crocodile jaw. Label on the fingertip: "YOU". Label on the jaw: "10 TIMES MORE SENSITIVE".]

The most sensitive domes could pick up forces about ten times smaller than the most sensitive parts of a human fingertip. Your fingertip is the most sensitive tool you own, and a crocodile's face beats it.

[A dark tank, almost black. A single drop falls. Ripples spread. An alligator's head turns toward them.]

Here's the unsettling part. In an earlier experiment, alligators sat in a tank in complete darkness, and when a single drop of water hit the surface, they turned to face the ripples.

[The sensors on the jaw flash as a small fish brushes against them. Snap. Label: "50 TO 70 MILLISECONDS".]

In the newer study, once something touched the skin around their jaws, the animals snapped at it within fifty to seventy milliseconds, which is about a twentieth of a second. It doesn't need to see Doug. It can feel him.

[Night on the river. Doug sits perfectly still in a canoe, very pleased with himself, paddle across his knees.]

Doug has decided the danger is at the water's edge, so he's sitting perfectly still in a canoe, in the dark, in the middle of the river.

[Close-up: one drop gathers on the tip of the paddle, and falls. Ripples spread from it. Cut to a splash. The canoe is empty. DOUG DEATHS: 220.]

One drop falls from his paddle.

## The Tongue

The Tongue.

[A crocodile with its mouth open, side view, nothing in it. Red arrow to the tongue: "SALT GLANDS".]

A crocodile that spends time in the sea has a problem, because living in salt water means taking in salt, and the extra has to go somewhere.

[The tongue up close: a row of tiny pores. Small white specks leave them.]

In nineteen eighty-one, two Australian scientists found the answer on the saltwater crocodile's tongue. It's covered in salt glands that push the extra salt back out of its body, and alligators don't have them.

[A map: a red band from southern India and Sri Lanka, across Southeast Asia and the Philippines, out to Micronesia, and down to northern Australia.]

That's part of the reason the saltwater crocodile is one of the most widely spread crocodiles on Earth. It's found from southern India and Sri Lanka, across Southeast Asia and the Philippines, east to Micronesia and south to northern Australia. The Queensland Museum calls it the only crocodile that can survive long journeys at sea.

[Map zoom out to open ocean. A lone crocodile far from any coast. Dotted line: "1,000 KM+". Beside it, the Great Barrier Reef as a long thin strip for scale.]

A United Nations fisheries guide notes that single saltwater crocodiles have turned up around islands more than a thousand kilometers from the nearest population. That's nearly half the length of the Great Barrier Reef, crossed by one reptile, alone, in the open sea.

But here's the twist. Crocodile experts say it also thrives in fresh water, in rivers, lakes and swamps as far as hundreds of kilometers inland.

So "saltwater" isn't a nickname. It's an incomplete list.

[Doug sprints from the river across the sand and paddles out to sea on an inflatable ring. He floats there, relieved, eyes closed.]

Doug has run from the river to the beach and paddled out on an inflatable ring, because the sea is where he feels safe.

[A crocodile swims calmly past Doug's ring and heads out toward the horizon. It doesn't look at him. The cap is still on Doug's head.]

A crocodile swims calmly past him on its way out to sea, and it doesn't even look at him.

Doug is fine.

He ran from its river straight into its ocean, and the only reason he's alive is that it had somewhere else to be.

## The Egg

The Egg.

[Mangrove shade, a little darker. A tall mound of mud and leaves on a riverbank. A large female crocodile lies beside it.]

On a riverbank in the wet season, there's a large pile of mud and dead leaves that looks like garden waste. It's a nest.

[Cutaway of the mound: rows of white eggs inside. Beside it, four egg cartons. Label: "ABOUT 50 EGGS".]

The female saltwater crocodile builds the mound herself out of plants, and in Australia she lays around fifty eggs inside it, more than four cartons' worth. Each one weighs about a hundred and thirteen grams, roughly as much as two large chicken eggs.

[Thermometer pushed into the mound. Two labels: "MALE" and "FEMALE".]

The temperature inside the mound decides whether each baby is male or female. Then the mother stays close, and the same United Nations fisheries guide says she aggressively protects the nest.

[A dotted chart: 100 hatchlings in a row. 99 fade out. One stays.]

She has reason to worry. Because nesting happens in the wet season, floods destroy a lot of nests. Even for the babies that hatch, the Territory Wildlife Park in northern Australia says only about one percent survive to adulthood.

[Close on the mound. Small sound marks come from inside: "chirp".]

The strange part comes at the end, about three months after the eggs are laid, when the babies start calling from inside their eggs. When the mother hears them, she digs them out, and she may gently carry them down to the water in her mouth.

[The mother's head in profile, jaws closed, a small hatchling just visible behind her teeth. Drawn at the detailed tier, small, not cute.]

The same jaws that hunt in this river carry her babies so carefully that they reach the water unhurt.

[Doug kneels beside the mound, ear to the mud, smiling. Speech bubble: "hi friends?". Behind him, the mother's head rises from the water.]

Doug hears chirping coming from the mound and leans in close to say hello.

Doug has died.

She can be gentle with that mouth. She just saves it for family, and Doug was kneeling on the nursery.

The Size.

Saltwater crocodiles are the largest reptiles alive today, and the true giants are the males.

Cassius was thought to be more than one hundred and ten years old. Nobody knew for sure, but if that estimate was right, he hatched around the start of the First World War.

## The Home

The Home.

[Dusk estuary. A helicopter lifts a large crocodile in a sling over the bush of Cape York.]

When a crocodile becomes a problem, one obvious answer is to take it somewhere far away, and a study published in two thousand seven tested how well that works.

[Map of Cape York Peninsula. A dotted line jumps by helicopter from the west coast to the east coast.]

Australian scientists caught three large males, fitted each one with a satellite tag, and moved them away from home. The biggest, a four and a half meter male, was flown by helicopter across Cape York, from the west coast to the east, and released into the sea.

[On the map, a red line starts at the release point, travels north up the east coast, rounds the tip of Cape York, and runs back down the west coast to the start. Label: "411 KM, 20 DAYS".]

He didn't take the short way back over land. He swam north, rounded the tip of Cape York, and came back down the other side, covering four hundred and eleven kilometers of coastline in twenty days. That's about twenty kilometers a day, close to a half marathon, every day for almost three weeks. He arrived home on Christmas Eve.

The worst part is that he wasn't special.

[Three small crocodile icons, each with a tick beside a home symbol.]

All three crocodiles found their way back to where they had been caught. The scientists concluded that moving a problem crocodile to a remote place is very likely to be ineffective, and they called the animals' navigation skills remarkable.

Doug has driven a crocodile a very long way from home, waved goodbye, and driven all the way back.

He goes to run a bath.

The study tracked crocodiles going home. It did not say whose home, and this one has apparently picked Doug's.

## The Lunge

The Lunge.

[Dusk. A crocodile on a riverbank, walking slowly. Doug watches from a tree.]

On land, a big crocodile is not much of an athlete. A two thousand nineteen study filmed fifteen species of crocodiles and their relatives moving at different speeds, and found that the bigger the animal, the less athletic its movement on land.

[A stick-figure chart: small crocodile running, medium crocodile trotting, large crocodile plodding. A red arrow points down along the row.]

Some crocodiles can gallop, but for this species, the only report of bounding that the scientists cite was in young ones under two meters long.

[Doug climbs onto a low branch hanging over the river, about as high above the water as a kitchen counter is above the floor. He unwraps a sandwich.]

Doug has read all of this and made a plan. He's climbed onto a branch about as high above the water as a kitchen counter, where he can eat his lunch in peace.

But that's not where the danger is.

[Cross-section of the water. A crocodile below the branch, body bent, tail curled. Red arrow along the tail: "SOLID MUSCLE".]

The Northern Territory Government describes the saltwater crocodile's tail as solid muscle and a major source of power. It makes the crocodile a strong swimmer, and it lets it make sudden lunges out of the water to grab its prey.

[Diagram: the tail sweeps, the body rises out of the water, jaws closed, toward the branch. Dotted line: "OUT OF REACH?" with a red question mark.]

So the slow animal on the bank and the animal under Doug's branch are not the same problem. On land, it plods. In the water, it has the tail, and Doug has chosen to sit directly above the water.

Doug climbed out of reach. "Out of reach" was a guess.

[Doug takes a bite of his sandwich. Below him, the water bulges. Cut to a splash. The branch is empty. The red cap hangs from a twig. DOUG DEATHS: 223.]

Doug takes the first bite of his sandwich.

Doug has died.

The branch was the right height for a picnic, but nobody had told the crocodile that it was a picnic.

## The Roll

The Roll.

[Night falling. Dark river. A crocodile at the surface holds something too big to swallow, shown only as a grey shape.]

Crocodiles can't chew. Their teeth are made for gripping, not cutting, so a meal that's too big to swallow whole is a real problem.

[Diagram: a crocodile from above, bending into a C. Red arrows: head bends one way, tail the other. Legs folded flat against the body.]

The solution is the death roll. In a two thousand seven study, scientists filmed young alligators doing it in slow motion. Each one folded its legs flat against its body, then bent its head and tail into a curve, like a banana.

[The C-shaped body begins to turn along its long axis. Motion lines.]

Then the whole animal spun along its length, and when the researchers held the tail still, the alligators couldn't roll at all, so the tail is doing most of the work.

[Label: "ABOUT 1 TURN PER SECOND".]

Large Nile crocodiles have been measured spinning at somewhere between half a turn and one full turn every second. A two thousand nineteen survey tested crocodiles from every living species, including thin-snouted species that had been thought unable to do it, and all but one species rolled.

Here's where it gets worse.

[The grey shape stays fixed in the jaws while the whole crocodile turns around it. Red arrow: "THE CROCODILE SPINS".]

The crocodile isn't spinning its prey. It's spinning itself, with its jaws locked shut, and whatever is in them has no choice but to come along.

[A clean diagram only: a spinning top. Doug stands on the bank, curious, leaning over the water.]

Doug has heard of the death roll and assumes it's a dance, so he's leaning out over the water to get a better look.

[Doug becomes a spinning-top blur on the surface. The red cap flies off in a clean arc and lands on the bank. Calm water. DOUG DEATHS: 224.]

He leans a little too far.

Doug has died.

The survey also found that crocodiles roll to escape, not just to feed. It isn't a dance, although it does need a partner.

Chosen.

## The Legend

The Legend.

[Sober card. Near-black. A plain map of the coast of Myanmar, Ramree Island outlined, mangrove swamp shaded. No soldiers, no Doug, no crocodiles drawn. A closed record book beside the map.]

For decades, a famous record book listed one night in nineteen forty-five as the worst crocodile attack in history.

[Label on the map: "RAMREE ISLAND, 1945".]

During the Second World War, around a thousand Japanese soldiers retreated into the mangrove swamps of Ramree Island, off the coast of what is now Myanmar, and very few came out. A naturalist's book from nineteen sixty-two described crocodiles in that swamp at night. Later writers claimed the crocodiles killed most of the soldiers, and Guinness repeated the story for years.

[A notebook icon and the words "WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS".]

The evidence doesn't support it. In two thousand one, a team of crocodile scientists went back over the records, and they found that the book's author was apparently not on the island at the time. Most military histories of the campaign never mention crocodiles.

[Interview icon: a speech bubble with a question mark. Label: "10 TO 15".]

The team also interviewed older residents who lived there during the war. They described soldiers dying from combat, disease, hunger and a lack of fresh water, and the only crocodile deaths they knew of were ten to fifteen soldiers crossing a tidal creek.

[The record book opens. A small note appears in the margin: "DOUBT".]

Years later, a British historian looked at military records for a documentary and found that most soldiers had died in combat or drowned. In late two thousand sixteen, Guinness took the record out of circulation.

Here's the uncomfortable part. The most famous crocodile attack in history probably didn't happen the way the record book said. The real danger is quieter: a few people at a time, every year, in many countries.

[End sober card. Day again. Doug sits on a riverbank rock, a rock, reading the record book. He closes it, reassured. Nothing happens. DOUG DEATHS: 224.]

Doug reads the record book on a rock, closes it, and feels much better.

Doug is fine. The legend is not.

The book was wrong about the biggest attack in history. That isn't good news for Doug, because the real danger was never one big night.

## The Menu

The Menu.

[Night. Near-black river, one torch beam. A crocodile's eyes in the beam.]

Plenty of large animals can kill a person, but this one belongs on a much shorter list.

[Sober card. Plain dark background, a map of Indonesia with one province highlighted. No Doug, no drawings of people.]

Crocodile scientists say that of all the world's crocodiles, only two species regularly prey on people: the Nile crocodile and the saltwater crocodile. The international group of crocodile experts puts it plainly, saying that saltwater crocodiles prey on people when they get the chance. The Nile crocodile actually causes more deaths, so this isn't the deadliest crocodile. It's just the other one on the list.

[Label beside the highlighted province: "100 ATTACKS, 10 YEARS". A coin beside it.]

A two thousand twenty-one study looked at one province of Indonesia, East Nusa Tenggara, over ten years. It counted one hundred saltwater crocodile attacks, and sixty of them were fatal, which is worse odds than a coin flip. Most of the people attacked were men, and most of them were fishing.

[A second highlighted map: Queensland. Label: "35 ATTACKS, 1971 TO 2015".]

Even in Queensland, crocodile experts counted thirty-five attacks between nineteen seventy-one and twenty fifteen.

Here's the disturbing part. To a large saltwater crocodile, a person isn't a threat to chase off, and it isn't a case of mistaken identity.

It sees a regular meal.

[End sober card. Back to the river at night. A wooden jetty. Doug stands at the end with the field guide open. He writes "FRIEND?" in big letters and holds out his hand toward the water.]

Doug has written "friend" in his field guide in big letters. He's offering a handshake from the end of a jetty, about as close to the water as you can get without swimming.

[Cut to spray over the end of the jetty. The field guide lies open on the planks. The cap rests beside it. DOUG DEATHS: 225.]

Doug has died.

He offered his hand to an animal that had already decided what he was, and it wasn't a friend.

## The Comeback

The Comeback.

[Near-black river at night, one torch beam. Then a sepia map of the Northern Territory, crossed rifles stamped on it, and a falling graph line.]

After the Second World War, crocodile skins were worth a lot of money, and in northern Australia, hunters shot saltwater crocodiles for their skins, almost without control, for twenty-six years.

[The graph line drops to a small number. Label: "ABOUT 3,000".]

By the time hunting was banned in the Northern Territory, in nineteen seventy-one, the population there had dropped to about three thousand animals.

[A stamp: "PROTECTED 1971". The graph line starts to climb.]

Then the crocodiles were protected, and scientists counted them along the same rivers, year after year, for decades, and the numbers climbed while the crocodiles got bigger.

[The line climbs steeply. Label: "ABOUT 100,000".]

Today, crocodile experts estimate there are between eighty and one hundred thousand saltwater crocodiles in the Northern Territory, close to where the population was before the hunting started. It's as if a town of three thousand people grew into a city of a hundred thousand within one lifetime.

[A small badge: "IUCN: LEAST CONCERN".]

Worldwide, the species is listed as Least Concern. Protection worked. That's the honest part of this story, and it's a real conservation success.

Here's the thing nobody planned for.

[Map of Darwin. The harbor. Floating traps, cylinders on floats, dotted around the water. Label: "215 IN 2012".]

The crocodiles have moved back into their old waters, and that includes the harbor of Darwin, the capital of the Northern Territory. In two thousand twelve alone, rangers took two hundred and fifteen saltwater crocodiles out of Darwin Harbour. The Territory's current management plan allows up to one thousand two hundred crocodiles a year to be removed in set areas, and the traps are still out there.
