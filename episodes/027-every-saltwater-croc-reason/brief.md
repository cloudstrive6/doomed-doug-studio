### 1. The Dive (hook item, second-strongest; death 219; "Doug did not agree to this.")
- **Facts:**
  - Tagged wild saltwater crocodiles have made resting dives of **several hours**. Campbell, Dwyer, Irwin and Franklin (2010) recorded dives of up to about 7 hours in a Queensland river (verify the journal, the figure and that these were resting dives). Most dives are short.
  - The longest dives happen in cool water, when the croc is resting and its metabolism is low (same source; verify).
  - The croc heart can divert blood away from the lungs during dives, using an actively controlled valve (the cog-tooth valve) and the foramen of Panizza (Axelsson, Franklin and colleagues, *J Exp Biol*; verify the paper and the wording; keep it to one sentence).
- **Analogy idea:** about 7 hours set against something everyday and sourced: a full working day, or a flight from Sydney to Singapore (verify the duration).
- **Twist (by 0:45):** **The river Doug checked and found empty has been occupied the whole time.** Doug watched the water for an hour. That is a short dive.
- **Doug beat:** the narrator says "Doug did not agree to this." as he hands Doug a fishing rod at the bank. Doug watches the flat water for an hour (an on-screen clock), declares "empty", and wades in. Cut to a splash. Death 219.
  The cap floats. Field guide page SALTWATER CROCODILE (already NO from 011) gets a second NO.

### 2. The Skin (death 220, flat)
- **Facts:**
  - Crocodiles have thousands of tiny dome pressure receptors, called integumentary sensory organs (ISOs), around their jaws, and in crocodiles (true crocs, not alligators) over the body too (verify).
  - Leitch and Catania (2012, *J Exp Biol*) found the face receptors are more sensitive than human fingertips, and that crocs turn toward a single drop hitting the water in the dark (verify both claims and the species tested).
- **Analogy idea:** fingertip sensitivity. A single drop of water is enough. Make it concrete: one drip from a paddle.
- **Twist:** **It doesn't need to see Doug. It feels him.**
- **Doug beat:** at night Doug sits perfectly still in a canoe, very proud of himself. One drop falls from his paddle (a close-up drip). Splash. Death 220, said flat ("Doug has died. Again.").

### 3. The Tongue (survival: "Doug is fine.")
- **Facts:**
  - Saltwater crocodiles have **salt glands on the tongue** that let them get rid of excess salt (Taplin and Grigg 1981, *Science*; verify).
  - That is one reason they can live in estuaries and spend time at sea. They have turned up on remote islands, hundreds of kilometres from the nearest population (verify one named, dated record, such as a Pacific island sighting reported by a government or a peer-reviewed note. Cut the island if it isn't solid).
  - The species is found from eastern India across Southeast Asia to northern Australia (verify with IUCN / CSG).
- **Analogy idea:** the range, set against a sourced distance (wider than the continental US, if that holds up).
- **Twist:** **"Saltwater" isn't a nickname. Doug's escape to the beach was a move into its other territory.** (Don't mention the tide.)
- **Doug beat:** Doug runs from the river to the beach and floats on an inflatable ring offshore, safe at last. A croc swims calmly past him, out toward the horizon, and ignores him completely. "Doug is fine." The narrator doesn't sound pleased.

### 4. The Egg (death 221; "hi friends?")
- **Facts:**
  - The female builds a mound nest of vegetation and mud and guards it (verify for *C. porosus*).
  - The nest temperature decides whether hatchlings are male or female (temperature-dependent sex determination; Webb, Lang and colleagues; verify the pattern for this species and keep it to one sentence).
  - Hatchlings call from inside the egg, and the mother digs the nest open and carries the young to the water in her mouth (verify both for this species).
  - Very few hatchlings survive to adulthood (verify a figure; cut it if there isn't a primary or government number).
- **Analogy idea:** egg size or clutch size against a sourced everyday object (an egg carton). Verify it.
- **Twist:** **The jaws that kill Doug carry the babies so gently that they come out unhurt.** The same mouth does both. (No bite number; that was used in 008/011.)
- **Doug beat:** Doug hears chirping from a mound. "hi friends?" The mother comes. Death 221. The hatchlings stay small, in the background and drawn at the detailed tier, never big-eyed or cute.

### Followed.

### 5. The Size (survival; the narrator quietly disappointed)
- **Facts:**
  - Lolong, captured in Bunawan, Philippines in 2011, was measured at **6.17 m** and listed by Guinness as the longest crocodile in captivity. He died in 2013 (verify).
  - Cassius, a captive croc in Queensland, measured about 5.48 m. He was thought to be **more than 110 years old** when he died in 2024 (verify the age estimate and attribute it).
  - Males are much bigger than females (verify the ratio). **Don't restate "over 1,000 kg" or "6 m longer than a car" as the twist** (008/011).
- **Analogy idea:** age, not length. Cassius was probably hatched before the Titanic sailed (verify that the date estimate allows this; otherwise use another sourced year).
- **Twist:** **The crocodile Doug is measuring may have been alive longer than anyone Doug has ever met.**
- **Doug beat:** Doug tries to measure a sleeping male with a tape measure and runs out of tape. The croc doesn't wake up. "hi friend?" Survival. The narrator says one dry line about the tape (don't reuse 025's tape-measure device wording).

### 6. The Home (death 222)
- **Facts:**
  - Read, Grigg, Irwin, Shanahan and Franklin (2007, *PLoS ONE*) satellite-tracked three large males that had been moved away from their capture sites. All three went home. One travelled about **411 km in about 20 days**, most of it by sea (verify the numbers and the route).
  - Relocation is therefore not a reliable way to remove a problem croc (state the paper's conclusion, attributed, not as advice).
- **Analogy idea:** 411 km against a sourced everyday trip (Sydney to Canberra is about 280 km by road; find a better match).
- **Twist:** **You can move it away. It comes home, and sometimes it gets there first.**
- **Doug beat:** Doug drives a croc far away in a trailer, waves goodbye and drives home. A croc is in his bathtub. Death 222.

### 7. The Jump (death 223)
- **Facts:**
  - Saltwater crocodiles can drive themselves almost vertically out of the water with their tail, lifting most of their body length to take prey from branches above (verify with a primary or textbook source such as Grigg and Kirshner, *Biology and Evolution of Crocodylians*, 2015. Tour-boat footage alone isn't enough).
  - **If the jump can't be sourced in strong terms, swap in "The Gallop":** some crocodylids can gallop or bound on land (Hutchinson et al. 2019, *Scientific Reports*). Use it only if *C. porosus* is in their sample, and say what was measured, not a "faster than a horse" myth.
- **Analogy idea:** height reached against a door frame or a basketball hoop (verify).
- **Twist:** **Doug climbed out of reach. "Out of reach" was a guess.**
- **Doug beat:** Doug sits on a branch over the river eating a sandwich. Splash, the branch is empty, and the cap is left hanging on a twig. Death 223.

### 8. The Roll (death 224)
- **Facts:**
  - The "death roll" is a fast spin about the body's long axis, used to break apart prey too big to swallow whole (Fish et al. 2007, *J Exp Biol*, on the mechanics; Drumheller et al. 2019, *Ecology and Evolution*, showed that most living crocodylian species do it; verify both).
  - Several crocs can feed on the same carcass, each rolling in turn (verify; cut if it's only anecdotal).
- **Analogy idea:** spin speed or turns per second against a sourced everyday spin (a washing machine). Verify it. If there is no number, skip the analogy.
- **Twist:** **The croc isn't spinning the prey. It's spinning itself, and the prey goes along.** Say it clinically. No outcome detail.
- **Doug beat:** a cartoon spin only. Doug turns into a spinning-top blur, and the cap is flung off in a clean arc onto the bank. Death 224. **Never show or describe what the roll does to a body.**

### 9. The Legend (survival: "Doug is fine. The legend is not.")
- **Facts:**
  - Guinness lists the 1945 Battle of Ramree Island, with hundreds of Japanese soldiers said to have been killed by crocodiles, as the record for crocodile attack deaths (verify the current Guinness wording).
  - Historians and crocodile scientists doubt the story. Accounts from the time don't support the large numbers, and the main source was a single naturalist's book (attribute it: Platt et al. in the *Crocodile Specialist Group Newsletter*, or Frank McLynn, *The Burma Campaign*, 2011. Verify who said what).
  - **Sober card**: no jokes on the war lines, no Doug in the shot, no description of deaths, no names.
- **Analogy idea:** none needed. The beat is the record book against the evidence.
- **Twist:** **The most famous crocodile attack in history probably didn't happen the way the record book says.** The real danger sounds less dramatic: a few people at a time, every year, in many countries. That leads into item 10.
- **Doug beat:** after the sober card, Doug reads the record book on a riverbank rock (a rock, not a log) and closes it, reassured. Nothing happens. "Doug is fine. The legend is not."
- **Cut rule:** if the doubt can't be attributed to two credible sources, cut the item and don't replace it (10 items).

### 10. The Menu (death 225)
- **Facts:**
  - Large saltwater crocodiles are among the few animals that regularly treat people as prey (attribute to a crocodile specialist or the IUCN/CSG; verify the wording).
  - The CrocBITE database (Sideleau and Britton) records hundreds of saltwater crocodile attacks, and about half are fatal. Indonesia has the most recorded attacks (verify the counts, the period and the fatality share. Use database wording, not a tabloid figure).
  - **Not Fukuda 2015** (011 used it).
- **Analogy idea:** none, or the share fatal against a coin flip ("about half"), only if the figure supports it.
- **Twist:** **Most predators see a person as a threat or a mistake. This one sees a regular meal.**
- **Doug beat:** Doug writes "FRIEND?" in the field guide and offers a handshake from a jetty. Cut to spray. Death 225. **Sober card** before the beat for the real-attack numbers: no names, no individual stories, no Doug on that card.

### 11. The Comeback (boss; death 226)
- **Facts:**
  - Commercial hunting had cut the Northern Territory population to about **3,000** by the time protection began in 1971 (verify the year and the estimate).
  - By the 2010s it had grown to roughly **100,000** non-hatchling crocs in the NT (Fukuda et al. 2011, *Journal of Wildlife Management*; verify the figure and the class counted).
  - Payoff of the cold open: Darwin Harbour has a standing trapping program because crocs keep moving back into the city's waters (NT Government; same source as the cold open).
  - The IUCN lists the species as Least Concern (verify).
- **Analogy idea:** 3,000 to 100,000, against a sourced everyday figure (a small town growing into a city). Verify any comparison.
- **Twist (the episode's thesis, played flat with no moral):** **The most terrifying thing about the saltwater crocodile is that people nearly wiped it out, decided not to, and it came back to everywhere it used to live, including the harbour.**
  No lecture and no "they were here first". One honest line: protection worked.
- **Doug beat:** a night swim in the "harbour" (a city skyline drawn in the background). Torch beam, then a row of eyeshine dots, then more and more. "hi friends?" Death 226.
- **Closing image:** morning in the harbour. A rangers' floating croc trap (a real NT design: a cylindrical cage on floats) is pulled up. Inside is only the red cap. Outside, eyes in the water watch.
  Kicker: "The rangers caught the cap." Field guide closes on SALTWATER CROCODILE with three red NOs. **Outro:** "DOUG DEATHS: 226" and one dry line. No recap and no subscribe pitch.
