## 1. Working title and proven outlier
- **Working title:** **When Earth Had So Much Oxygen That Bugs Could Kill Doug** (55 characters, 11 words). "Kill" is the death verb.
  "When Earth Had…" is the engine's frame. "Doug" makes it clear this is our cartoon premise, not a claim that giant bugs hunted people.
- **Borrowed from:** Mr. Science's "When Earth Had Supermountains" 3.39M and "When Antarctica Was a Jungle" 4.82M (the "When Earth
  Had/Was [vanished extreme state]" walk-through). We borrow the frame: a strange vanished Earth, with the extreme cause named in the title.
  We do **not** borrow their wording, their pacing (122 wpm, slow cold open), their structure or their photoreal art.
- **Delivers what it promises:** item 1 is the oxygen itself, every item after it is a giant of that world, getting bigger toward Doug's
  size and then past it, and the boss is the biggest bug that ever lived.
- **Alternates for the titler (leads; 5 are required):**
  - "Why Bugs Got Deadlier the More Oxygen Earth Had" (T6 shape, 47 chars)
  - "The Deadliest Giant Bugs From When Earth Had Too Much Air"
  - "When Bugs Were Big Enough to Kill Doug" (38 chars)
  - "The Scariest Bugs That Ever Lived Were the Size of Doug"
  - **Banned:** "Weirdest"/"Craziest", "What… Would Be Like" (T1), "How Every…" (T2), "the Deeper Doug Goes", ALL CAPS, "TERRIFYING",
    "?!", "for kids", any claim of an exact oxygen percentage in the title, and Mr. Science's exact phrasing.
- **"Bugs":** the narrator says once, early, that "bug" is loose here: some were insects and some were sea scorpions and millipede
  relatives. One item (Crassigyrinus) isn't a bug at all, and the script says so ("Not everything that got big was a bug.").

## 2. Escalation axis and items
**Axis: body size, longest dimension (wingspan or body length), smallest to largest,** inside the high-oxygen world of the late
Carboniferous and earliest Permian (about 340-290 million years ago). Item 1 is the oxygen molecule, the smallest thing in the episode
and the cause of everything after it. The boss is the largest land arthropod known.

**Episode-only device, proposed: DOUG FOR SCALE.** A crude tape measure (`tape_measure.json` exists) next to the death counter,
showing each item's size beside a Doug silhouette. It is monotonic: "0.0000000003 M" (or "SMALLER THAN THIS DOT") at item 1, then
about 45 cm up to about 2.6 m. The Doug silhouette is passed at "Doug-sized." Every number is "about" or "estimated" and sourced.
The art director may simplify it, but the death counter always stays.

**Time machine** (standard `prehistoric` vehicle). Every item opens with its door. The dial shows the site's age (in millions of years)
for flavour, but the age is **not** the axis. The dial jumps around, and the narrator doesn't comment.

Spoken section headers (two-word style):
- *(no header before item 1; the opener leads straight into "The Oxygen.")*
- **"Arm-sized."** (before item 2)
- **"Doug-sized."** (before item 7, about 65%)

Every number below is a lead. The writer verifies each one in facts.md. **If a fact fails, cut it; don't hedge it.** If a size
estimate changes the order, reorder within the band. The boss stays last.

### Item 1 (no band)

**1. The Oxygen** (hook item; death 204)
- Facts:
  - Today's air is about 21% oxygen (NOAA or any standard source).
  - Late Carboniferous and early Permian oxygen is estimated well above that. Estimates range from the high 20s to about 35% depending
    on the model (Berner 2006 GEOCARBSULF; Glasspool & Scott 2010, *Nature Geoscience*, from charcoal in coal). Give a range and
    "estimated", never one exact figure.
  - Why there was so much: vast swamp forests made oxygen, and their buried wood became coal (keep it to one line; 007 spent the
    freezing angle).
  - Fire: experiments show that in higher-oxygen air even damp plants burn more easily (Belcher & McElwain 2008, *Science*), and
    Carboniferous coals are full of fossil charcoal (Scott & Glasspool 2006, *PNAS*).
- Analogy idea: today, one breath in five is oxygen; then, possibly one in three.
- Twist (must land by 0:45): **more oxygen didn't only grow bugs. It made wet forests burn.**
- Doug beat: Doug steps out of the time machine with a cupcake and one birthday candle to mark the trip. He lights it. The flame jumps far
  too high. Death 204: a cartoon soot-black Doug with X eyes, smoke curling, **no burns or injury detail**. The cap, smoking slightly, on a
  fern.
- Put the "bug" line here: "Everything on this list grew up breathing this air."

### Arm-sized.

**2. The Mayfly** (death 205)
- Facts:
  - *Bojophlebia prokopi*, about 300+ million years old, from the Czech Republic, with a wingspan estimated at about 45 cm
    (Kukalová-Peck 1985). **Check its current classification.** If it is no longer treated as a mayfly relative, say "an early
    winged insect" and rename the chapter "The Wings".
  - Modern adult mayflies have mouthparts that don't work and live for a day or so. **Don't claim this for the fossil.** Use it only as
    the modern contrast, and only if sourced.
  - Fallback if Bojophlebia doesn't hold up: a giant Carboniferous scorpion such as *Gigantoscorpio* (verify the size). It must stay
    smaller than item 3.
- Analogy idea: a wingspan about as wide as a microwave oven (source the width) or a standard keyboard.
- Twist: **the giant mayfly isn't the danger. The ground under it is a swamp the size of a country.**
- Doug beat: Doug follows the mayfly across the coal swamp, looking up, and walks into peat. Death 205 (sinks to the cap, cartoon). The cap
  floats on the peat. The mayfly is fine.

**3. The Spider** (death 206)
- Facts:
  - *Megarachne servinei*, Argentina, about 300 million years old. It was described in 1980 as the largest spider ever (Hünicken 1980).
  - In 2005 a re-study (Selden, Corronca & Hünicken, *Biology Letters*) showed it was a **sea scorpion** (eurypterid), not a spider.
  - Size: about 54 cm long (verify body length versus leg span, and use the source's number).
  - Spiders of that time were small (verify one source on Carboniferous spider size, or cut the line).
- Analogy idea: about the length of a skateboard (source).
- Twist: **the biggest spider that ever lived wasn't a spider.** (This is our 019 "drawn wrong" flavour, used once here; don't make it a
  running theme.)
- Doug beat: Doug arrives with the household method: a drinking glass and a postcard, to put it outside. The glass covers one leg. Death
  206 (cartoon, cut on the first pinch). The cap is under the upturned glass.

**4. The Beak** (Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - Palaeodictyoptera: Paleozoic insects with a sucking beak used to drink plant juices, and a small extra pair of winglets in front of
    the wings (verify both; Labandeira or a review on Paleozoic insect feeding).
  - *Mazothairos enormis*, Mazon Creek, Illinois, wingspan estimated at about 50-55 cm from a partial fossil (Kukalová-Peck & Richardson
    1983; verify, and say "estimated from a fragment").
  - They were among the first insects to feed on plants by piercing them (verify the wording).
- Analogy idea: the beak against a drinking straw (source the comparison, or use none).
- Twist: **it has a beak built for stabbing, and it only uses it on plants.**
- Doug beat: Doug wears a leaf hat to blend in ("hi friend?"). The insect lands on him, tests him, and leaves for a real plant. **No
  piercing shown; it lands and leaves.** "Doug is fine." The narrator is quietly disappointed, and the leaf hat is now "slightly
  insulted".

**5. The Griffinfly** (death 207)
- Facts:
  - *Meganeura monyi*, Commentry, France, about 300 million years old, wingspan about 65-70 cm (verify).
  - It wasn't a true dragonfly but a griffinfly (order Meganisoptera), with spiny legs for catching prey (verify).
  - Its Permian cousin *Meganeuropsis permiana* (Kansas/Oklahoma) is the largest insect known, at about 71 cm (verify).
  - **The oxygen debate starts here:** insects breathe through tubes (tracheae), and more oxygen may have let bodies grow bigger
    (Harrison et al. 2010, *Proc. R. Soc. B*). Clapham & Karr 2012, *PNAS*: maximum insect size tracked oxygen for about 150 million
    years, then fell away when birds appeared. Other researchers argue that predators or temperature mattered more. **The script says
    plainly that scientists still argue about it.**
- Analogy idea: **not** a front door (007 used it). A wingspan about as wide as a standard acoustic guitar is long (source), or an
  adult's outstretched forearms.
- Twist: **it hunted in the air, and nothing else did yet.** There were no birds, bats or pterosaurs (verify: the first flying vertebrates
  came later).
- **The one callback (007):** "Doug has met one of these before. It flew away." No other episode callbacks.
- Doug beat: Doug waves a net. The griffinfly grabs the net, with Doug still on it. It can't carry him (honest: it caught insects) and
  drops him from the top of a scale tree (`scale_tree.json`). Death 207 (cartoon fall, cut before landing). The cap drifts down after him.

**6. The Scorpion** (death 208)
- Facts:
  - *Pulmonoscorpius kirktonensis*, East Kirkton, Scotland, about 335 million years old, about 70 cm long (verify).
  - It lived on land and breathed with book lungs (the name means "lung scorpion"; verify).
  - Diet: probably insects and maybe small vertebrates. **Only state the diet if a source says so; otherwise cut it.**
- Analogy idea: as long as a full-size guitar or a standard broom handle (source).
- Twist: **the biggest scorpion that ever lived on land breathed with lungs like pages in a book** (or a sourced diet twist, if
  stronger).
- Doug beat: Doug lifts a log to look underneath it, the way people do with modern scorpions, and the scorpion is about as long as the log.
  Death 208 (cartoon sting, cut to black on contact). The cap on the log.

### Doug-sized.

**7. The Sea Scorpion That Walked** (Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - *Hibbertopterus scouleri*, Scotland, about 330 million years old, up to about 1.6 m long (verify).
  - In 2005 a trackway about 6 m long was described from Fife, Scotland, and it shows a giant sea scorpion walking on land (Whyte 2005,
    *Nature*; verify the site and length).
  - It raked mud and water for small prey with its front limbs (a sweep feeder; verify).
- Analogy idea: about the length of a single bed, or about Doug's height (source the bed length).
- Twist: **a sea scorpion the size of Doug, out of the water, walking.**
- Doug beat: Doug lies down in the trackway to measure it. It walks straight over him. "Doug is fine." The narrator is quietly
  disappointed (the second and last time this episode). The tape measure reads DOUG-SIZED.

**8. The Thick Tadpole** (death 209)
- Facts:
  - *Crassigyrinus scoticus*, Scotland, about 330 million years old. It was an early four-limbed animal that lived in water, with huge
    jaws and tiny limbs; estimates put it at about 1.5-2 m (verify, and if it comes out under item 7, swap 7 and 8).
  - The name means "thick tadpole" (verify).
  - It was an ambush predator in swamps and lakes (verify the wording).
- Analogy idea: its head against a standard microwave or a carry-on bag (source).
- Twist: **not everything that got big was a bug. Some of it was eating the bugs' neighbours.** (Only if a source supports what it ate;
  otherwise use "its legs were too small to walk on, and it never needed to leave the water".)
- Doug beat: Doug wades in to get a better look at the "big tadpole" ("hi friend?"). Death 209 (cartoon gulp, cut to black). The cap
  floats in a ring of ripples.

**9. The Millipede** (the boss; death 210, **played flat**)
- Facts:
  - *Arthropleura*, the largest land arthropod known. The Northumberland specimen (found in a fallen sandstone block on a beach in 2018;
    Davies et al. 2021, *Journal of the Geological Society*) is estimated at up to about 2.6 m long and around 50 kg (verify the
    weight estimate, or cut it).
  - 2024: the first well-preserved heads, from juveniles found in France, suggested a millipede body with some centipede-like head
    features (Lhéritier et al. 2024, *Science Advances*; verify).
  - Diet: probably plants or rotting plant matter, though this is still debated (verify the wording).
  - Arthropleura lived for about 45 million years and disappeared in the early Permian (verify the dates).
- Analogy idea: about the length of a small car or a sofa (source), or "longer than Doug is tall". The tape measure passes the Doug
  silhouette.
- Twist: **the biggest bug that ever lived probably didn't want to eat anything with a pulse. It didn't have to. It was the size of
  a car.**
- **The resolution:** the narrator gives the honest version of the oxygen idea in two or three lines. It probably helped, but scientists
  still argue about how much, and something else changed too: once flying vertebrates arrived, insects never got this big again (Clapham
  & Karr 2012). **No claim stronger than the sources.**
- Doug beat: Doug tries to ride it ("hi friend?", a saddle made from a fern). It doesn't notice him and walks on, over the spot where he
  was standing. Death 210 (cartoon, flattened silhouette, X eyes, **flat delivery, no milestone bit**). The cap ends up on Arthropleura's
  back, riding off into the coal forest.
- **Closing image:** the time machine returns to 2026. Doug steps out and takes one deep breath of 21% air, relieved. The tape measure
  shrinks back to "DOUG". Kicker (one line, roughly): "Doug has never been so happy to breathe less."
- **Outro:** 30 words or fewer, the final counter (210) and one dry line. No recap, no subscribe pitch.

Deaths: 7 (204-210). Doug survives The Beak and The Sea Scorpion That Walked. 9 items.

## 7. Risks
- **Over-claiming the science (HIGH, the main risk of this episode):** the oxygen-gigantism link is a real but **debated** hypothesis.
  The script must:
  - give oxygen as an estimated range, never one exact figure;
  - say once, plainly, that scientists still argue about how much oxygen explains the giants;
  - give every size as "about" or "estimated", from the source's own number (several of these fossils are fragments);
  - never say a giant bug hunted or killed people. There were no people. The deaths are Doug's cartoon premise, and the boss's diet is
    stated as "probably" or "debated", following the source.
- **Fire (MEDIUM):** item 1's death is a cartoon soot gag. No burns, no injury, no real wildfire victims, no 2026 wildfire news.
- **Gore and phobia (MEDIUM):** no bites or stings shown on contact (cut to black), no piercing in item 4, no bugs crawling on skin, no
  gross-out swarms. Creepy is fine; disgusting is not.
- **Kid-appeal (MEDIUM):** giant bugs and prehistoric animals are big kids' topics (the "carboniferous" autocomplete is clean, but
  "giant insects" content often isn't). Countermeasures: the dry adult narrator, adult-coded lines (the leaf hat that is "slightly
  insulted", the candle), creatures drawn at the detailed tier and heavy, no bright mascot colours, no kids' tags, made_for_kids false.
- **Facts that need strong sources (cut if unsourced):**
  - The Oxygen: today's 21%; the Carboniferous estimate range and its models; the fire experiments; fossil charcoal.
  - The Mayfly: Bojophlebia's size, age and current classification; the modern mayfly contrast.
  - The Spider: the 1980 description; the 2005 reinterpretation; the size; small contemporary spiders.
  - The Beak: palaeodictyopteran beaks and winglets; Mazothairos's size estimate and basis; plant-piercing.
  - The Griffinfly: Meganeura's size, site and age; Meganisoptera vs dragonflies; spiny legs; Meganeuropsis's size; no flying vertebrates
    yet; Harrison 2010; Clapham & Karr 2012; the counter-arguments.
  - The Scorpion: Pulmonoscorpius's size, site and age; book lungs; diet (cut if unsourced).
  - The Sea Scorpion That Walked: Hibbertopterus's size; the 2005 Fife trackway; sweep feeding.
  - The Thick Tadpole: Crassigyrinus's size, the meaning of its name, its habitat and diet.
  - The Millipede: Arthropleura's size and weight estimates; the 2018/2021 Northumberland find; the 2024 heads; diet; time range.
- **Myths to avoid:** "insects were giant because of oxygen" as settled fact; "Meganeura was a dragonfly"; "Megarachne was a giant
  spider" stated as true; "Arthropleura was a predator" as fact; one-number oxygen claims ("35% oxygen") as fact; Arthropleura "could
  kill a human"; any documentary with AI imagery as a source.
- **Duplication:** don't reuse Mr. Science's wording, structure or cold open. Our twists carry the video:
  - more oxygen made wet forests burn
  - the danger under the giant mayfly is the swamp
  - the biggest spider wasn't a spider
  - a stabbing beak, only used on plants
  - it hunted in the air, and nothing else did yet

  - a Doug-sized sea scorpion, walking
  - not everything that got big was a bug
  - the biggest bug didn't need to eat anything with a pulse
