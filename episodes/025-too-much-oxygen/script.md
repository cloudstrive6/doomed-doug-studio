## The Oxygen

The Oxygen.

Right now, roughly one breath in every five you take is oxygen, and that number feels as permanent as the color of the sky. It isn't.

[A crude pie chart: AIR TODAY. One slice in five is labeled "OXYGEN, ABOUT 21%".]

Around three hundred million years ago, swamp forests covered the low, wet land near the equator, and a great deal of their wood never rotted. It sank into waterlogged ground and slowly became coal. Rotting uses up oxygen, so with all that wood buried instead, oxygen piled up in the air.

[A cutaway: trees falling into black water, layers stacking up underneath, the bottom layer turning black. Label: "COAL". A big red arrow points up at the sky: "O2".]

Here's the problem. Oxygen doesn't only feed lungs, it also feeds fire.

[The pie chart again: AIR THEN. A much bigger slice, labeled "ABOUT 27 TO 35%", with a red "?" next to it.]

How high it went is still argued over, but the main estimates put the peak somewhere between about twenty-seven and thirty-five percent. At the top of that range, one breath in three would be oxygen. Researchers who study ancient fires estimate that once oxygen passes about twenty-five percent, even the plants in a wet swamp could catch fire.

The coal from this time backs that up. In some seams, up to forty percent of a layer is fossil charcoal, the burnt leftovers of swamps that went up in flames. Charcoal barely rots, so all those fires may even have helped keep the oxygen high, a loop of burning and burying. It was a very different kind of world in which to light a candle.

[A coal seam cross-section with black charcoal streaks. Label: "UP TO 40% CHARCOAL".]

[The time machine door opens. Doug steps out holding a cupcake with one birthday candle. Speech bubble: "happy trip!"]

Doug has brought a cupcake with a single birthday candle on it, to celebrate his first trip to this world, and being Doug, he lights it immediately.

[The flame jumps far too high. Cut to Doug, soot-black from cap to shoes, X eyes, a thin curl of smoke above him. DOUG DEATHS: 204. The cap, smoking slightly, lands on a fern.]

Doug has died. The cupcake survived. The cap landed on a fern, which, in this air, was a brave place to land.

A quick word on "bugs", which is a loose word here. Some of what you're about to meet are insects, others are relatives of scorpions and millipedes, and one of them isn't a bug at all. Everything on this list grew up breathing this air.

Arm-sized.

## The Wings

The Wings.

[The time machine dial reads 309. The door opens onto a flat, steaming swamp with a shallow lake. Overhead, a large insect with four broad wings drifts past.]

About three hundred and nine million years ago, over a swamp in what is now the Czech Republic, something with a wingspan of nearly forty centimeters went drifting past. That's about as wide as a bowling pin is tall.

[The insect beside a bowling pin. Label: "NEARLY 40 CM". The tape measure jumps to "40 CM".]

Its name is Bojophlebia, and when it was first described in nineteen eighty-five, it was called a gigantic mayfly. A re-study in twenty fifteen found multiple errors in that original description. Today it's treated as an early relative of mayflies and dragonflies, sitting on its own lonely branch near the base of their family tree.

The original specimen is kept at the National Museum in Prague. The volcanic rock around it has been dated, using the radioactive decay of argon, to that same three hundred and nine million years. That rock also tells us what kind of place it lived in. The ash settled into a peat swamp with a shallow lake, so its world was flat, warm, wet and quiet.

The unsettling part is that the insect is not the danger here. The ground is.

[A cutaway under the swamp surface: a thin green skin of moss and leaves over a deep brown column. Label: "PEAT".]

Peat forms when dead plants pile up in waterlogged ground faster than bacteria can break them down, because the still water runs short of oxygen and slows the rotting. From above, it looks like a forest floor. Underneath, it's mostly soggy, half-rotted plants, and modern tropical peat on Sumatra can be more than fifteen meters thick.

[Label beside the brown column: "MORE THAN 15 M". A tiny Doug silhouette for scale at the top.]

The swamps of this era were not small, either. In eastern Kentucky alone, they left behind around forty-five to fifty major coal beds, stacked one on top of another, from swamps that kept spreading and drowning again.

[Doug follows the insect across the swamp, head tilted back, mouth open in a hopeful smile. He steps off a log onto what looks like solid ground.]

Doug follows the wings across the swamp, looking up the whole time, which is the one direction that peat never comes from.

[Doug sinks, slowly, straight down. Only the cap is left, floating on the brown surface. DOUG DEATHS: 205.]

Doug has died. Again. Bojophlebia never noticed, because it was only ever passing through.

## The Spider

The Spider.

[The time machine dial reads 300. The door opens onto a muddy riverbank in a wide floodplain. A dark, broad shape sits half in the shallows.]

In nineteen eighty, a fossil from Argentina was announced to the world as the largest spider that had ever lived.

[An old museum-style card: "LARGEST SPIDER EVER, 1980". A drawing of a huge hairy spider.]

The rock it came from is about three hundred million years old. The scientist who described it, Mario Hunicken, studied it with X-rays and read its shape as a spider's body, with an estimated leg span of about fifty centimeters. A round structure at the back of the fossil was interpreted as a spider's abdomen, and a bump at the front as its fangs. For comparison, the biggest spider alive today, a giant tarantula from South America, manages about thirty. The fossil was named Megarachne, and it quickly became famous, to the point that museums built giant spider models of it.

[The giant spider model in a museum, with a small crowd of stick-figure heads looking up at it.]

But that's not the disturbing part.

In two thousand five, a second, more complete fossil turned up at the same site, and a team that included Hunicken himself took another look at both of them. Megarachne was not a spider. It was a eurypterid, the kind of animal usually called a sea scorpion.

[A red X stamped across the old card. The model is wheeled away. A new drawing appears: a short, broad sea scorpion with bladed front limbs. Label: "EURYPTERID".]

Even that name turns out to be misleading, because the rocks show it lived in fresh water on a river floodplain, nowhere near the sea. It was about fifty-four centimeters long, slightly longer than a soprano ukulele. Its front limbs carried blades for raking through the soft mud, hunting for small invertebrates.

[The sea scorpion beside a ukulele. The tape measure reads "54 CM".]

Doug has brought the household method for spiders: a drinking glass and a postcard, so that he can put it outside.

[Doug holds a glass over Megarachne. The glass covers exactly one leg. Speech bubble: "hold still"]

The glass covers one leg. Megarachne walks into the river wearing the glass like a boot, and Doug, who refuses to let go of the glass, goes with it.

[A splash. Cut to the riverbank: the upturned glass on the mud, the cap underneath it. DOUG DEATHS: 206.]

The biggest spider that ever lived was never a spider, and it never noticed Doug either.

## The Beak

The Beak.

[The time machine dial reads 309. The door opens into a forest of tree ferns. A large insect with long, straight wings perches on a frond, a stiff, pointed beak under its head.]

Some insects of this world came with a built-in drinking straw, and the straw was sharp.

They belong to an extinct group called the Palaeodictyoptera. Instead of chewing jaws, they had a long, beak-like set of mouthparts made of sharp stylets, built to pierce plant tissue. Many of them also had a pair of small extra flaps in front of their wings, which is why they are sometimes called six-winged insects.

[A close-up of the beak with a red arrow. Label: "PIERCING BEAK".]

One of the largest is Mazothairos, from the Mazon Creek fossil beds in Illinois, which are about three hundred and nine million years old. Back then, Mazon Creek was part of a river delta with a tropical climate. Its wingspan is estimated at about fifty-five centimeters, almost exactly the height of a carry-on suitcase. That estimate comes from a single, badly broken fossil, so it's a careful guess rather than a measurement.

[Mazothairos beside a carry-on suitcase. The tape measure reads "55 CM".]

Here comes the strange part. A beak built for stabbing, and as far as anyone can tell, this group mostly used it to drink plant juices. A few of them may have fed on other animals, but the main menu was plants.

We even have the evidence. Fossil tree-fern stems from Illinois still carry the tiny holes where an insect from this group pushed its beak in. The holes lead straight into the plant's internal plumbing, the tissues that carry water and sugar. Around each wound, the plant grew reaction tissue, which means the fern was still alive while it was being drunk.

[Diagram: the beak sliding into a fern stem, then a small ring of scar tissue. Label: "PLANT JUICE".]

Doug has assembled a camouflage hat out of overlapping leaves, on the theory that anything which drinks from plants will mistake him for a plant.

[Doug in a leafy hat, the red cap poking through the top. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

The insect lands on him, carefully examines the hat with the tip of its beak, and considers it for a long moment. Then it leaves for a real plant.

[Mazothairos perched on Doug's leaf hat, beak lowered, then lifting off toward a tree fern. DOUG DEATHS stays at 206.]

Doug is fine.

The narrator is quietly, professionally disappointed. The leaf hat, on the other hand, is now slightly insulted.

## The Griffinfly

The Griffinfly.

[The time machine dial reads 300. The door opens into a coal forest. A huge shadow crosses the ground, then Meganeura sweeps past, wings spread wide.]

This is Meganeura, and it's often called a giant dragonfly, which is close, but not quite right.

It belongs to an extinct group called the griffinflies, which look like dragonflies but sit on a separate branch of the family tree. Its fossils come from coal rocks at Commentry, in France, about three hundred million years old. Its wingspan was roughly sixty-five to seventy-five centimeters, about as wide as a road bike wheel, while the widest wings on any living dragonfly relative span about nineteen centimeters.

[Meganeura beside a bicycle wheel. The tape measure reads "ABOUT 70 CM".]

It was a predator, and its legs carried big spines that worked like a trap for grabbing other insects in the air.

Doug has met one of these before. It flew away.

This is also where the oxygen idea comes in. Insects don't have lungs. They breathe through a network of tiny air tubes that runs through the whole body, and the idea is that richer air lets those tubes supply a bigger body. In laboratory experiments, many living insects raised in low oxygen grow up smaller, and some raised in extra oxygen grow larger. One large study found that the biggest insects grew and shrank along with oxygen for about the first hundred and fifty million years of insect history. Other scientists point to predators, temperature, or the limits of an armored body instead. Scientists still argue about it.

[A crude line chart: "OXYGEN" and "BIGGEST INSECT" rising and falling together, then a big red "?" over the chart.]

And here's the catch. When Meganeura hunted in the air, nothing up there could hunt it back. There were no birds, no bats, and not even flying reptiles, because the first pterosaurs wouldn't show up for tens of millions of years.

[An empty sky. Label: "NO BIRDS YET". Meganeura alone in it.]

Doug has brought a bug net, and he waves it at the sky.

[Doug swings the net. Meganeura grabs the net in its spiny legs and lifts. Doug is still holding the handle.]

Meganeura grabs the net with Doug still attached. It hunted insects, so it can't actually carry him for long. It gets as far as the top of a scale tree, and then it lets go.

[Cut before landing. The cap drifts down alone, rocking side to side like a leaf. DOUG DEATHS: 207.]

It was the only predator in the sky, and it was trying to put Doug back.

## The Scorpion

The Scorpion.

[The time machine dial reads 341. The door opens onto a hillside near a warm lake, with fallen logs among ferns.]

This is Pulmonoscorpius, one of the largest scorpions known, and the biggest individual is estimated at about seventy centimeters long. That's roughly the size of a house cat.

[Pulmonoscorpius beside a sitting house cat. The cat leaves the frame. The tape measure reads "ABOUT 70 CM".]

Its fossils come from East Kirkton Quarry, in West Lothian, Scotland, from rocks recently dated to about three hundred and forty million years ago. The same quarry is famous for its fossils of early four-legged animals. Most of what's been found of this scorpion is loose pieces of its outer skin, plus a few more complete specimens, and many of those are young ones.

What did it eat? Experts think it probably preyed on smaller arthropods, and possibly on small four-legged animals. Its pincers were fairly small for a scorpion of its size, which suggests it either went after smaller prey or relied heavily on its venom.

The surprising part is where it breathed.

[A cutaway of the scorpion's underside with four pairs of small slits. Label: "BOOK LUNGS".]

Its name comes from the Latin word for lung. Modern scorpions carry four pairs of book lungs on their underside. Each one is a stack of thin plates arranged like the pages of a book, with air flowing in between them. This quarry holds the earliest scorpions shown for certain to have lived on land. Their well-preserved fossils suggest that book lungs developed directly from gills, with a covering plate sealing up to leave small openings for air. Book lungs mean this animal could breathe on dry land, so a scorpion almost as long as Doug's arm was not waiting in a lake. It was walking around the hillside.

[Diagram: a closed book, then the pages fanning open into thin gray plates.]

Doug knows that scorpions like to hide under things, so he lifts a log to have a look.

[Doug lifts the end of a log. Underneath, Pulmonoscorpius is almost exactly as long as the log.]

The scorpion is about as long as the log.

[Cut to black on contact. Then the log, back in place, with the cap sitting on top. DOUG DEATHS: 208.]

Doug was right, because it was hiding under the log. Unfortunately, that was the whole of his plan.

Doug-sized.

## The Sea Scorpion That Walked

The Sea Scorpion That Walked.

[The time machine dial reads 330. The door opens onto a sandy riverbank in Scotland. Across the sand runs a long, wide trail: two bands of footprints with a groove down the middle.]

A geologist out walking near Saint Andrews, in Fife, Scotland, once spotted something strange in the sandstone. It was a trackway, about six meters long and almost a meter wide, made roughly three hundred and thirty million years ago, and he described it in two thousand five.

[A crude map of Scotland with Fife highlighted. Label: "6 M OF FOOTPRINTS".]

The tracks were made by a giant sea scorpion, probably Hibbertopterus. The animal that left them was about one point six meters long, the length of a standard bathtub, and about a meter wide. Other Hibbertopterus fossils suggest the species could grow even bigger than that.

[Hibbertopterus beside a bathtub. The tape measure passes the Doug silhouette's shoulder and reads "1.6 M".]

It was not a hunter of big prey. It lived in freshwater swamps and rivers, using the spiny blades on its front limbs to rake through the mud for small invertebrates, like an enormous, extremely slow garden rake.

But here's what makes it famous. The tracks show it walking on land, with a short, slow stride, and a groove where it dragged its tail along behind it. A sea scorpion the size of Doug, out of the water, walking.

According to the geologist who found them, it was the largest trackway of a walking arthropod found at the time. How the animal breathed out there is still unknown. One idea is that its gills kept working as long as they stayed wet. Even the geologist who found the tracks doubts that these animals could ever have adapted to living on land full time. The trackway itself is fragile, so it still lies out in the rock, and silicone copies were made to preserve it.

[A red "?" over the animal's underside. Label: "GILLS? ON LAND?"]

Doug decides to measure the trackway himself, so he lies down inside it, holding the tape measure against his own shoulder for scale.

[Doug flat on his back inside the trackway, arms at his sides, the tape measure laid next to him. A large shadow falls over him.]

Hibbertopterus walks straight over him, one slow step at a time, and keeps on going.

[Doug sits up, flattened slightly, but with dot eyes, not X eyes. The tape measure reads "DOUG-SIZED". DOUG DEATHS stays at 208.]

Doug is fine.

The narrator checked twice, and was a little disappointed both times. That's the second survival today, and there will not be a third.

## The Tadpole From Hell

The Tadpole From Hell.

[The time machine dial reads 330. The door opens onto a dark swamp pool. Two large eyes sit just above the surface.]

Not everything that got big in this world was a bug.

London's Natural History Museum calls this one the tadpole from hell. Its real name is Crassigyrinus, and it lived in coal swamps in what is now Scotland and parts of North America, more than three hundred million years ago.

[A crude map: Scotland and a patch of North America highlighted. Label: "COAL SWAMPS".]

It was an early four-limbed animal, an early cousin of the creatures that went on to walk on land, but it didn't do much walking itself. Its body was flat, its limbs were very short, and it lived in the water.

Every known fossil of it was found badly crushed, including the original specimen kept in Edinburgh. For almost a century, it was drawn with a very tall, narrow head, a bit like a moray eel. Then in twenty twenty-three, researchers rebuilt the skull digitally from scans, and it came out low and wide, shaped much more like a crocodile's. The lead researcher puts the whole animal at around two to three meters long, longer than a standard door is tall.

[Crassigyrinus beside a door. The tape measure's solid reading stays where item 7 left it, at the Doug silhouette's height. Instead, a dashed bracket appears beside the tape, spanning 2 to 3 meters, labeled "2 TO 3 M?".]

That rebuilt skull was full of huge teeth, and ridges along it helped spread the force of the bite across all of them. It also had large eyes for seeing in the dim coal swamps, and lines along the head for sensing vibrations in the water.

The disturbing part is the way it hunted. Researchers think it behaved a lot like a modern crocodile, lying just below the surface and grabbing whatever came too close. According to the museum, its jaws let it eat practically anything that crossed its path.

[The pool surface. Only the eyes show. A small fish swims near, and the eyes do not move at all.]

Doug has only ever seen tadpoles in garden ponds, where they are small, harmless and mostly tail, so he wades in to get a closer look.

[Doug knee-deep in the pool, waving. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

[Cut to black on a huge gulp. Then the pool, with the cap floating in the middle of a ring of ripples. DOUG DEATHS: 209.]

Technically, the museum calls it a tadpole. Nothing about it was small, except the legs.

## The Millipede

The Millipede.

[The time machine dial reads 326. The door opens onto open woodland near a coast. Something enormous and segmented moves between the trees, too long to fit in the frame.]

In January twenty eighteen, a large block of sandstone fell from a cliff onto the beach at Howick Bay, in Northumberland, England. It cracked open, and a former Cambridge student spotted what was inside: a piece of the largest land bug that ever lived.

[A boulder on a beach, split open, with a long segmented fossil inside. Label: "HOWICK BAY, 2018".]

It belonged to Arthropleura, a giant relative of millipedes. The fossil is a section about seventy-five centimeters long, probably part of a shell the animal shed as it grew. From that section, researchers estimate the whole animal at up to about two point six meters long and around fifty kilograms. The University of Cambridge, which announced the find, called it as big as a car.

[Arthropleura beside a small car. The tape measure's solid reading finally climbs up past the top of the Doug silhouette, and stops at "ABOUT 2.6 M", inside the dashed "2 TO 3 M?" bracket left over from the tadpole. The bracket stays dashed; the reading is solid.]

The tadpole from hell may have been just as long. But this is the biggest bug, the largest land arthropod known.

Arthropleura lasted around forty-five million years, then disappeared in the Permian. Nobody is sure why.

For a long time, nobody knew what its head looked like either. In twenty twenty-four, scientists scanned two tiny young specimens from France, each about four centimeters long, and finally saw one. The body is a millipede's, but the head has a few features you'd normally expect on a centipede.

[Two small fossils in a scanner, then a head appearing on screen: antennae, eyes on stalks. Label: "HEAD, FINALLY".]

As for its diet, the twenty twenty-four team thinks it was a slow scavenger of rotting plants, although one of the Howick Bay researchers had wondered if it might even hunt. No adult head has been found yet, so the debate continues.

What nobody expected was the timing. This fossil is older than the oxygen peak, and its own researchers say oxygen cannot be the only explanation for its size. In twenty twenty-six, a study in Nature found that in living insects, the tiny air tubes take up only about one percent of the flight muscle. That suggests oxygen supply wasn't what held them back. And once birds arrived, insect size stopped following oxygen. The honest answer is that the extra oxygen may have helped, but nobody can yet say how much.

[The oxygen chart from The Griffinfly, now with a bird silhouette added and the two lines splitting apart. Label: "IT'S COMPLICATED".]

The biggest land bug that ever lived probably wasn't interested in eating anything with a pulse. It didn't have to be. It was the size of a car.

[Doug approaches with a saddle made from a fern. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug has brought a saddle made out of a fern, because he would like to ride it.

[Arthropleura doesn't turn its head. It walks on, over the spot where Doug is standing. Doug lies flat on the ground behind it, a thin outline with X eyes. DOUG DEATHS: 210.]

Arthropleura didn't notice him. It walked on, across the spot where he was standing.

Doug has died.

[The cap is on Arthropleura's back, riding off into the woods.]

The cap went with it.

[The time machine reappears in a 2026 park. Doug steps out, closes his eyes, and takes one slow, deep breath. The tape measure shrinks back to "DOUG". The pie chart reads "OXYGEN, ABOUT 21%".]

Back home, in ordinary twenty-one percent air, Doug takes one long, careful breath. He has never been so happy to breathe less.

[DOUG DEATHS: 210 holds on screen.]

Doug deaths: two hundred and ten. Bugs that noticed him: almost none. Which vanished world should Doug visit next? Tell us in the comments.
