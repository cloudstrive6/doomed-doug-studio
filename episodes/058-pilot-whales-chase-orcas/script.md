# Why Orcas Run From Pilot Whales (draft 2)

[Opening frame: the thumbnail. A tight wall of black, round-headed pilot whales charging left to right. Ahead of them a large orca flees out of frame with a sweat drop. Tiny Doug in the orca costume, cap on top, between the two, looking back. DOUG DEATHS: 420. WHO RAN: PILOT WHALES | EVERYONE ELSE, both columns empty.]

When scientists played killer whale hunting sounds off Norway, one kind of whale swam toward the speaker.

By the end, Doug is dressed as an orca, and the real orcas leave without him.

## The Speaker

The Speaker. Four out of five tagged pilot whales that heard recorded killer whales turned around and headed straight for the sound.

[Underwater speaker on a cable, red sound waves. Label "KILLER WHALE SOUNDS". A pilot whale turns toward it. Red curved arrow.]

The study ran in Vestfjord, off northern Norway. Researchers played long-finned pilot whales the calls of killer whales that had been eating herring, usually from about two and a half kilometers away.

Here's the strange part. Other animals tested this way did the opposite: in earlier playback studies, gray whales and belugas moved away from killer whale sounds, and a beaked whale did the same thing. Killer whales eat whales, so leaving is the sensible choice.

The pilot whales came looking.

[Red "?" over the speaker. A crowd of round black heads gathering around it.]

It wasn't only the tagged whales that came over. Many other pilot whales in the area moved toward the speaker as well, and the groups got bigger while the sound was playing. When the team played a harmless control sound instead, the whales just carried on the way they had been going.

The researchers offered two explanations for this: the whales may simply have been drawn to a meal, since those killer whales were eating fish when they were recorded. Or they may have been mobbing, which means a group bunching up and moving toward a predator together.

It's the way small birds gang up on an owl, except that each bird weighs about as much as a car.

[Doug in the new orca costume, cap on top, holding the speaker. He looks pleased.]

Doug has found the speaker, and he has also found an orca costume, and he thinks his impression is very good.

[The wall of black heads arrives. Cut away. DOUG DEATHS: 421. Over the black: the speaker, still playing. WHO RAN: one tick under PILOT WHALES.]

Doug has died. Again. The speaker is still playing.

And whatever these whales normally chase, they chase it a kilometer down, in total darkness, at full speed.

Prey.

## The Sprint

The Sprint. A short-finned pilot whale can hunt at about nine meters a second, close to a kilometer below the surface of the sea.

[Ocean background, getting darker with depth. A pilot whale streaks downward. Label "9 M PER SECOND".]

Researchers off the Canary Islands attached sound-recording tags to twenty-three of these whales and followed them on their dives. The whales would drop to around five hundred meters, listen for a target, and then sprint after it as deep as about a thousand meters.

Nine meters a second works out to about thirty-two kilometers an hour. On land, that's a cyclist in a hurry, but down there it's pitch black, and the cyclist is holding its breath the whole time.

[A crude bicycle, and a pilot whale beside it with a dive mask on its forehead. Dotted line down to "1,000 M".]

Most other deep-diving whales that scientists had tagged cruised along at only one or two meters a second. So the team gave pilot whales a nickname that stuck: the cheetahs of the deep sea.

What they're sprinting after is mostly squid, and the big ones seem to be their favorite.

But that's not the odd part. The researchers described these as all-or-nothing hunting dives. A sprint that deep, on a single breath, with no promise of a meal at the end of it, is a gamble, and these whales take it again and again.

That's the thing to know about pilot whales. They don't run from things. They go and look.

[Deep, near-black background. Doug in the reused squid costume, cap on top, waving. Speech bubble: "hi friend?"]

Doug is wearing the squid costume, it's his first time this deep, and he would very much like to make a friend.

[A round black head arrives very fast. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 422. The squid costume floats up, flat, cap still on it. WHO RAN: second tick.]

Doug has died, at thirty-two kilometers an hour.

Squid can't complain about any of this, but fishermen can, and they do.

People.

## The Longline

The Longline. Along the Atlantic coast of the United States, the animal blamed most for stealing fish off longline hooks is the short-finned pilot whale.

[A fishing boat paying out a long line of baited hooks. Label "TUNA AND SWORDFISH".]

A longline is one long main line, hung with baited hooks and left in open water to catch tuna and swordfish. The pilot whales take the bait, or the catch itself, straight off those hooks.

It's like someone eating your groceries out of the cart while you're still pushing it around the store.

The worst part is that it doesn't look like an accident. A team reviewing this fishery in two thousand twenty-one wrote that many small and mid-sized dolphin species have learned to exploit chances like this, and pilot whales are on their list. A tuna that's already on a hook is a big, rich meal that takes very little effort to get, with no deep dive and no sprint required.

[Red arrow from a round black head to a hook with half a fish on it. Red "!".]

The same review says whales that raid longlines can remove large amounts of catch, at real cost to the people fishing.

The cheetahs of the deep sea have found the drive-through.

[Doug in a fisherman's hat over the cap, in a small boat. He reels in an empty hook. Then another. Then another. Beside the boat, a black round head surfaces and watches.]

Doug has reeled in three empty hooks, and the whale floating beside his boat has not offered an explanation.

Doug is fine. His dinner has been reassigned.

[WHO RAN: third tick.]

Fishermen follow the fish, but something with a lot more teeth follows the pilot whales.

Sharks.

## The Whitetip

The Whitetip. Oceanic whitetip sharks have been seen following pods of short-finned pilot whales across the open ocean.

[A pilot whale pod swimming left to right. Behind it, a big shark with white-tipped fins, keeping its distance. Label "FOLLOWING".]

The oceanic whitetip is a big shark of the open sea. A few of them reach about four meters long, and divers describe them as persistent, circling a swimmer and coming back again. They eat tuna, sea turtles, seabirds, squid, and nearly anything that gets thrown off a boat.

The Florida Museum records whitetips swimming with short-finned pilot whales in Hawaiian waters, in a report from nineteen eighty-eight. Nobody is completely sure why they do it, but the leading idea is food. Pilot whales are very good at finding squid, and whitetips happen to eat squid too.

So the shark tags along behind, like a dog trailing whoever is carrying the groceries.

Here's the problem for the shark. In these reports, it doesn't challenge the pod or try to take its food by force, and instead it keeps its distance and follows. A four-meter shark with a fearsome reputation has decided that the smart move is to let the pilot whales go first.

[Doug, treading water between the pod and the shark. He gives the pod a thumbs up.]

Doug has placed himself in the gap between the pod and the shark. He thinks of it as the middle seat.

[The pod swims off. The shark does not. Cut away. DOUG DEATHS: 423. The cap floats on the surface, alone. WHO RAN: fourth tick.]

The pod left, and the shark stayed exactly where it was. Doug has died.

The shark follows them around, but the next animal is several times their weight, and the pilot whales go after it.

Giants.

## The Sperm Whale

The Sperm Whale. Off the coast of Ecuador, pilot whales and sperm whales run into each other often, and sometimes the pilot whales are the aggressive ones.

[A huge sperm whale, side on. A short-finned pilot whale beside it, much smaller. Labels "13.5 TONNES" and "3 TONNES".]

The size gap is not small: a female sperm whale can weigh up to about thirteen and a half tonnes, while the biggest short-finned pilot whales weigh around three.

That's like picking a fight with a bus because it parked a little too close to you.

The sperm whales don't shrug it off, either, and several times off Ecuador they pulled into the same defensive formation they use against killer whales. This channel has already watched a group of twelve do it in the Gulf of Mexico.

What nobody has managed to show is that pilot whales are a real danger to sperm whales. A whale research group in Quebec puts it plainly: there's no evidence that pilot whales are a real threat to them. And still, sperm whales react to pilot whales in much the same way they react to killer whales.

[A thermometer labelled "ACTUAL DANGER" near the bottom. Beside it, a second one labelled "SPERM WHALE REACTION", near the top.]

So the evidence points to a three-tonne whale that harasses a giant, and a giant that treats it like the top predator of the ocean.

[Doug in a small rubber dinghy, floating between the sperm whales and the pilot whales. Both groups pass around him. Nothing happens.]

Doug is in a rubber dinghy between the two groups. Nobody touches him, and nobody even seems to notice he's there.

Doug survives. Nobody is more surprised than the narrator, and nobody is less pleased.

[WHO RAN: fifth tick.]

They harass giants, which leaves one animal they should want nothing to do with, and in Iceland, the relationship is complicated.

Orcas.

## The Calf

The Calf. In August twenty twenty-one, off western Iceland, a female killer whale was seen swimming with a newborn long-finned pilot whale at her side.

[An adult orca, and tucked against her flank a small grey-black pilot whale calf. Plain dot eye. Label "NOT HERS". Red arrow.]

The orca's name is Sædís, and people on a whale-watching boat noticed the small calf tucked in beside her and realised it wasn't an orca at all. Researchers watched the pair for twenty-one minutes, with the calf holding the spot just beside and behind her, where a baby saves energy and stays sheltered. There were no other pilot whales anywhere nearby, and Sædís had never been recorded with a calf of her own.

It's like finding a lamb asleep in the wolf's bed, and the wolf acting as if that's normal.

The paper, published in twenty twenty-three, called it the first account of a killer whale apparently caring for another species' calf. Whether the calf was orphaned or whether Sædís took it, the researchers couldn't tell. Other orca groups off Iceland were then seen with other pilot whale calves in twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-three. Scientists have suggested play, practice, or some kind of nurturing, and nobody knows what happened to those calves afterwards.

[Red "?" stamped three times.]

Now here's why that matters. Researchers have pointed out that pilot whales have been reported chasing killer whales off Iceland many times. If some orcas are taking pilot whale calves, then the pilot whales would have a very good reason to chase them off. That's a suggestion, not a finding.

[Doug, cap on, holds out a fish to the calf. The calf ignores it.]

Doug offers the calf a fish, and it ignores him completely, which is the politest thing an animal has done to him all year.

Doug is fine.

[WHO RAN: sixth tick.]

That calf had a family out there somewhere, and pilot whale families are big, and they stay together.

## The Family

The Family. A pod of long-finned pilot whales is one big extended family.

[A crude family tree drawn in chalk, every branch ending in an identical round black head. Label "ONE FAMILY".]

That came from DNA work published in the journal Science in nineteen ninety-three. Scientists now generally think that both the sons and the daughters stay in the group they were born into. The males probably find partners when different groups meet up for a short time, rather than by moving away.

Pods can run to dozens of animals, and older females that have stopped breeding help look after the calves.

It's a family reunion where every cousin turns up, every single time, and nobody ever goes home.

[The family tree fills in. Some heads get tiny glasses.]

Their warm-water cousins take it one step further. Until twenty eighteen, only three mammals were known to live a large part of their lives after they stop breeding in the wild: humans, killer whales and short-finned pilot whales. In that species, a mother's last calf may keep nursing for as long as fifteen years.

So instead of having babies, the oldest members of the group help to run the family.

The unsettling part is what that means for anyone on the other side. An orca that meets a pod isn't meeting a few whales. It's meeting grandmothers, aunts, cousins and sons who all grew up together, and none of them are leaving.

[Doug in the orca costume, cap on top, swimming up to a large old pilot whale with a grey patch. He waves.]

Doug has come to introduce himself to the grandmother. He's still wearing the orca costume, and nobody told him.

[Cut away. DOUG DEATHS: 424. For one beat the cap sits on the grandmother's head. Then it floats up alone. WHO RAN: seventh tick.]

Doug has died. The cap suited her.

Off Iceland, real orcas meet this family every summer, and most of the time, the orcas are the ones who leave.

## The Chase

The Chase. Off the coast of Iceland, when long-finned pilot whales approach killer whales, the killer whales usually leave.

[Iceland coastline map, two red dots: the Westman Islands in the south, a wide bay in the west. A pod of round black heads moving toward a group of orcas.]

A team led by Anna Selbmann went through fourteen years of sightings from research boats and whale-watching tours. Pilot whales only showed up in summer, and on days when both species were in the same area, they interacted on twenty-four days out of thirty-four.

In every one of those interactions, it was the pilot whales that moved in, usually fast. In almost every encounter, the killer whales moved away, and in over a quarter they left at high speed, with both species porpoising, which means leaping clear of the water as they race.

[Orcas porpoising out of frame. Pilot whales porpoising after them. Label "HIGH SPEED".]

And the orcas are the bigger animal, by a long way. A killer whale can weigh up to about ten tonnes, while a big long-finned pilot whale weighs about two and a quarter.

It's like a whole pub emptying out because one family walked in.

Here's the detail that gives it away. When the orcas fled the fastest, the pilot whale groups averaged around eighty-five animals. When the orcas simply moved off, the groups averaged about thirty-four. Nobody ever saw an obvious physical attack, and the orcas were often feeding when the pilot whales arrived, so they gave up their meal to leave.

As for why, the researchers couldn't settle it. One idea is competition over food or space, and the other is mobbing, with the pilot whales treating orcas as a predator worth chasing off. They haven't been able to rule out either one.

[A red "?" between two thought bubbles: a fish, and an owl surrounded by small birds.]

So the top predator of the ocean leaves the bay. Doug, the slowest orca there has ever been, does not.

[Doug, in the orca costume, zips it back up. He's in the middle of the fleeing orca pod.]

Doug did not agree to this.

[The orcas porpoise away. Doug tries to porpoise and gets about a centimeter of air. The wall of black heads turns toward him. Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 425. WHO RAN: a big red "ORCAS" written under EVERYONE ELSE.]

[Closing: the empty bay. The orca costume floats flat on the surface with the cap on top. The pilot whale pod circles it once, slowly, and leaves.]

The pilot whales came over to look at the cap, and then they left it right where it was. The cap did not move, and around here, that puts it ahead of the orcas.

Doug deaths: four hundred and twenty-five. Orcas run from the pilot whales. This number runs toward them every time. Subscribe before Doug finds a worse pod.
