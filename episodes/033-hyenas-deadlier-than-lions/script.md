Dusk.

## The Lie

The Lie.

[Sunset over the savanna: acacia trees, an orange sky turning purple. Night clock: about 7 PM. A hyena slinks along with a cartoon "THIEF" sign hung round its neck.]

Hyenas are famous for living on other animals' kills, but in Kenya's Masai Mara, researchers found that spotted hyenas kill about ninety-five percent of the food they eat.

[A week planner with twenty-one meal boxes. Twenty fill with a hyena paw print, and one gets a takeaway box.]

If you ate three meals a day for a week, that would be twenty meals you cooked yourself and a single takeaway.

[Old documentary style: a lion standing on a carcass outline and a hyena skulking at the edge. Label: "THE PICTURE".]

For decades, the accepted picture was simple: lions did the hunting, and hyenas hung around the edges and stole. Then, in Tanzania, a biologist named Hans Kruuk started watching what hyenas actually did at night.

[Night. A stick-figure scientist in a jeep, binoculars up, headlights off.]

Here's where the picture flips.

[A three-panel strip. Panel 1: hyenas bring down a wildebeest silhouette. Panel 2: a lion strolls in. Panel 3: the hyenas back off and the lion eats. A red arrow runs from panel 1 to panel 3.]

Kruuk kept finding the same scene, in which the hyenas had made the kill and the lions had strolled in afterwards and chased them off. In the Ngorongoro Crater, where hyenas outnumbered lions about ten to one, it was usually the lions eating hyena kills.

[The "THIEF" sign slides off the hyena's neck and lands on the lion. The lion looks at it.]

The thief in the famous picture was the lion.

[The cracked LION | HYENA chalkboard fills the frame. The narrator's hand holds the chalk.]

The narrator has used this chalkboard before. Every tick is the narrator's call, not a study.

Tonight's question is which one is worse to be near, on foot, until dawn.

[Doug sets a plate of leftovers on the grass and sits beside it, cap on, hopeful smile. Sign: "FOR THE SCAVENGERS".]

Doug has brought a plate of leftovers for the scavengers, and sat down beside it to watch.

[Dusk deepens. Eyes appear in the grass. The hyenas walk straight past the plate toward Doug.]

The hyenas walk straight past it.

[Cut to black. The cap sits beside the untouched plate. DOUG DEATHS: 261. The field guide's SPOTTED HYENA page gets a red "NO". Chalkboard: HYENA 1.]

Nobody touched the leftovers, because hyenas prefer to start from scratch, and they have the teeth to finish anything.

## The Bite

The Bite.

[Night clock: about 8 PM. A hyena skull in cross-section, with huge cheek teeth highlighted in red.]

A spotted hyena doesn't just eat the meat off a skeleton. It eats the skeleton.

[A bone diagram with a red arrow pointing at the cheek teeth. Label: "BONE CRUSHERS".]

Its heavy cheek teeth can crush large bones, and at a carcass a hyena will swallow the meat, the bone and even the hair. When scientists measured the bite of one captive hyena, it reached about four thousand five hundred newtons, and hyenas in the wild probably bite even harder.

[Six identical stick men stacked on a giant cartoon jaw. Label: "4,500 NEWTONS".]

That's roughly the weight of six grown men, all squeezed into one set of jaws.

[A dusty track. White blobs on the ground, seen from a moving jeep. A label reads "CALCIUM". Keep it diagrammatic, nothing gross.]

Then the bone gets digested, and hyena droppings come out white because they carry the minerals from all that bone. One study found between one thousand and twenty thousand times more calcium and phosphorus in them than in the soil around them.

Here's the difference.

[Split panel. Left: a lion walking away from an outline carcass. Right: a hyena waiting under a tree, watching it go. Red dotted arrow from the hyena to the carcass.]

When lions are around a carcass, hyenas often just wait for them to abandon it, then come back later for whatever remains.

The lion eats the meal. The hyena eats the plate it came on.

[Doug holds out a pet-shop chew bone with a bow on it, hopeful smile. The hyena ignores it, takes Doug's walking stick, and bites it cleanly in half. The halves drop.]

Doug has brought the hyena a gift, a chew bone from a pet shop, with a bow on it. The hyena takes his walking stick instead, and bites it cleanly in half.

[Cut to black. The cap sits next to the two halves of the stick. DOUG DEATHS: 262. Chalkboard: HYENA 2.]

Doug has died.

The hyena turned down the leftovers and the gift, and broke the stick. Doug decided to run before it got round to the cap.

## The Chase

The Chase.

[Night clock: about 9 PM. A lion bursts from the grass, misses a gazelle, stops and sits down. A hyena behind it doesn't stop.]

A lion that misses on its first rush usually gives up, but a hyena that misses just keeps on running.

[Top-down map. The lion's dotted path is a short creep, then a short dash. Label: "30 M".]

Lions hunt by stalking and seldom charge until they're within about thirty meters, and they can't hold that pace for more than a hundred to two hundred meters. Even then, a lion hunting alone by day succeeds less than one time in five.

[The hyena's dotted path is a long, winding line running off the edge of the map. Label: "5 KM".]

Hyenas work differently, and Hans Kruuk watched them run down adult wildebeest in chases of about five kilometers, at speeds of up to sixty kilometers an hour. In the Kalahari Desert, a typical hyena chase ran from half a kilometer to two and a half.

[A park-run finish funnel beside a running track with half a lap highlighted in red.]

Five kilometers is a whole park run, while the lion's limit is half a lap of a running track.

[Chalkboard: the narrator's hand adds one tick to LION and one to HYENA.]

Over the first sprint they're both fast, so the lion gets a tick, for honesty.

The worst part is the plan. Against a lion, the plan is "don't be close." Against a hyena, the plan is "don't get tired."

Doug is always tired.

[Doug sprinting, gritted teeth, a thought bubble of a lion falling behind. He stops, hands on knees. Distance sign: "200 M". He looks back.]

Doug has outrun the lion, at least in his head, and at two hundred meters he stops to catch his breath and look back at the empty grass behind him.

[A hyena trotting toward him, not even hurrying. Doug's speech bubble: "still?"]

[Cut to black. The cap rolls on a little further and stops. DOUG DEATHS: 263. Chalkboard: LION 1, HYENA 3.]

Doug has died. Again.

The cap kept rolling for a while, and then, somewhere out in the dark, something started laughing.

## The Laugh

The Laugh.

[Night clock: about 11 PM. Deep navy. A hyena with its mouth open in a giggle, not a smile, and jagged sound lines.]

A hyena's laugh isn't laughter. It's the sound of a hyena under stress, and it tells everyone listening how old it is and where it ranks.

[A crude sound graph. One line drops in pitch with a label "OLDER". A steady line says "TOP RANK", and a wobbly line says "LOW RANK".]

Researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, recorded hundreds of giggles from captive hyenas and found that the pitch drops as a hyena gets older. Dominant hyenas giggle in a steady, confident way, while low-ranking ones sound more variable, and each animal even has its own voice.

[Two hyenas tugging at the same scrap, both giggling. Then a lion chases a hyena off, and the hyena giggles as it runs.]

They giggle in tense moments, like a squabble over food or when lions chase them off a kill, and the researchers think the wobble in the call carries frustration.

But the giggle is not the sound to worry about.

[A hyena with its head low, giving a long rising call. Rings spread out across a map of the savanna. Label: "UP TO 5 KM".]

That's the whoop, a long-range call that hyenas use to recruit the rest of the clan, and it carries up to about five kilometers. That's nearly fifty soccer fields laid end to end.

[Doug laughs back, mouth wide, hand on his belly. The giggling gets louder and higher. One low-ranking hyena backs off, ears flat, and slinks away.]

Doug laughs back, to be friendly, and the giggling gets louder and higher until one low-ranking hyena backs off, frustrated, and leaves.

[Doug alone, hopeful smile. DOUG DEATHS stays at 263. The field guide's SPOTTED HYENA page: "MAYBE" pencilled in. Chalkboard: HYENA 4.]

Doug is fine.

He has no idea what he said to it, and the narrator, watching from a safe distance, had hoped for a different result.

[Far off on the dark horizon, one hyena lifts its head and whoops. Rings spread.]

The hyena that left was whooping now, and that is the call that brings the others.

Midnight.

## The Clan

The Clan.

[Night clock: midnight. Torchlight sweeps across the grass, and pair after pair of eyes lights up.]

At rich sites like the Masai Mara and the Ngorongoro Crater, a single hyena clan can reach around a hundred and thirty animals.

[A yellow school bus packed with hyenas, a second bus behind it. Label: "72 SEATS".]

A hundred and thirty hyenas wouldn't fit on a school bus rated for seventy-two passengers, so they would need two.

[A pyramid of hyena heads. A big female at the top, males at the bottom. A red arrow runs from a mother down to her young one beside her.]

Every one of them has a rank, and the rank is inherited, so young hyenas take their place in the order from their mother. Females outrank all the males, and to bring down big prey, a clan hunts in groups of roughly eight to twenty-five.

Now do the arithmetic.

[Map of the Ngorongoro Crater, a ring of hills. On one side, a dense crowd of hyena dots. On the other, a small cluster of lion dots. Labels: "UP TO 550" and "25 TO 40".]

In the Ngorongoro Crater, up to five hundred and fifty hyenas share the crater floor with just twenty-five to forty adult lions.

[Split panel. Left: a pride resting under a tree like a family photo. Right: a crowded hyena town with a queue sign, "WHO EATS FIRST".]

A lion pride is a family, but a hyena clan is a town, and it has strict rules about who gets to eat first.

[Chalkboard: the narrator's hand adds a tick to LION.]

Prides are groups too, so the lion gets its last tick.

The last hyena Doug met was the size of a lion. This one is smaller. That did not help.

[Doug holding the torch, counting on his fingers. Bubble: "1... 2..."]

[More eyes appear and more, and Doug's fingers run out. Bubble: "12?" He gives up.]

Doug counts the eyes in his torch beam, gets to twelve, and gives up.

[Cut to black. The torch lies in the grass, still on, lighting up the cap. DOUG DEATHS: 264. The field guide's "MAYBE" is rubbed out. Chalkboard: LION 2, HYENA 5.]

Doug has died. Again.

The clan sat down to eat, and the narrator went to fetch a stopwatch.

## The Stomach

The Stomach.

[Night clock: about 1 AM. An empty patch of grass with a big cartoon stopwatch above it. Label: "36 MINUTES". Nothing is drawn being eaten.]

Thirty-five hyenas can finish an entire adult zebra in thirty-six minutes.

[A second stopwatch and another empty patch. Label: "UNDER 15 MINUTES".]

Hans Kruuk also watched the remains of a hundred-kilogram young wildebeest disappear in less than fifteen minutes. A single hyena can eat at least fourteen and a half kilograms of meat in one meal, which for a small adult is more than a third of its own body weight.

[A tower of burger patties taller than Doug. Label: "120+".]

That's more than a hundred and twenty quarter-pound burgers, eaten in one sitting.

[A crowd of hyenas around an empty patch, each one eating as fast as it can. A smaller male at the edge, gulping. A red label: "SPEED, NOT FIGHTING".]

At a carcass, hyenas compete mostly by eating faster, not by fighting the way lions do, and a low-ranking male has to gorge quickly before the females push him aside.

And that's where Doug's plan falls apart, because there is no "come back for it later." Nothing is left to come back for.

[A water hole at night. A hyena dives under the surface, and a bubble trail rises. Label: "12 SECONDS".]

Water doesn't stop them either, because hyenas have been seen diving under floating carcasses to take a bite, staying under for up to twelve seconds.

[Doug lowers his lunchbox into the water hole on a string, hopeful smile. Sign: "FOR LATER". A hyena goes straight in after it. Bubbles.]

Doug has hidden his packed lunch in the water hole, on a string, for later, and the hyena goes in after it first.

[Cut to black. The cap floats on the still water hole. DOUG DEATHS: 265. Chalkboard: HYENA 6.]

Doug has died. Again.

Doug wanted somewhere with people in it, and the narrator knew a city where the hyenas come in for dinner.

Before dawn.

## The Town

The Town.

[Night clock: about 3 AM. An old city wall at night, whitewashed and lit by a lamp. Low in the wall, a small opening. Label: "HYENA HOLE".]

The old walled city of Harar, in Ethiopia, has holes in its wall for hyenas. Sixteen of them.

[A crude map of the walled town with sixteen small red dots around its edge.]

Spotted hyenas have roamed Harar's streets for at least five hundred years, coming and going through those openings and cleaning up the city's scraps. The old town, Harar Jugol, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site covering forty-eight hectares, about the size of Vatican City, the smallest country in the world.

[A simple, respectful drawing. A man sits on a low stool, holding out a strip of meat, and a hyena takes it gently. Lamplight.]

In the evenings, men known as the hyena men feed wild hyenas by hand. Each year, people there also set out porridge for the hyenas, in a ritual believed to keep anyone from being attacked in the year ahead.

[A map of northern Ethiopia with a red dot at Mekelle. A butcher's bin, then a calendar showing "55 DAYS", then a donkey with a red "!" above it.]

Further north, around the city of Mekelle, hyenas mostly scavenge waste from butchers and households. During the fifty-five-day fast before Easter, when Orthodox Christians stop eating meat, researchers found donkey hair in more than twice as many of the hyenas' droppings.

The remarkable part is what all of this says about hyenas.

They notice where the food comes from and change with it, and one city chose to deal with them by inviting them to dinner.

[Doug walks in through the city gate with his torch. The hyena man is calmly feeding a hyena. Doug holds out a piece of meat too. The hyena takes it politely.]

Doug walks into Harar at night and finds hyenas taking meat from a man's hand, so he holds out a piece too. It is taken, politely.

[Doug with a hopeful smile and a thumbs up. DOUG DEATHS stays at 265. Chalkboard: HYENA 7.]

Doug is fine.

The narrator suspects it only worked because a professional was present, and Doug walked back out of town to sleep.

## The Sleepers

The Sleepers.

[Night clock: about 4 AM. A village at the edge of the bush, people sleeping on mats outside their huts on a hot night. Simple and quiet, no hyenas in this frame.]

Lions are the animal people fear in the dark, but there are many records of spotted hyenas attacking people while they sleep.

[A hyena standing far off at the edge of the village, with a long dotted line showing the distance it keeps.]

Hyenas are usually shy around people, and they can run from a person at up to three hundred meters, further than any other African carnivore. That's roughly the height of the Eiffel Tower, laid on its side.

Even so, they can prey on people who are sleeping outdoors, and they sometimes break into huts and tents.

[Sober card: white text on black, no drawings. "MANY RECORDS OF ATTACKS ON SLEEPING PEOPLE" / "MOZAMBIQUE RESERVE: 7 OF 13 ATTACKS ON PEOPLE SLEEPING OUTSIDE".]

In one reserve in Mozambique, more than half of the recorded hyena attacks happened while people were sleeping in the open.

Here's who is actually out there in the dark.

[A town edge at night: a rubbish heap, a butcher's bin and a hyena silhouette walking past a doorway. A lion silhouette is far off in the bush, small.]

Lions are what people picture, but hyenas are the ones that come right into towns, and even into cities, to eat what people throw away.

[Doug lying on a mat outside under the stars, cap on, smiling. A hand-painted sign beside him: "LIONS: NO". His torch rolls slowly away into the dark and goes out.]

Doug is sleeping outside because of the heat, on a mat, beside a hand-painted sign that says "Lions: no", and in the night his torch rolls away.

[Cut to black. The cap sits on top of the sign. DOUG DEATHS: 266. Chalkboard: HYENA 8.]

Doug has died. Again.

The sign was right, and no lions came, so for the last hour of the night, Doug went out to find some.

## The Mob

The Mob.

[Night clock: about 5 AM. The sky is starting to grey. Three lionesses on an outline carcass. Around them in the dark, glowing eyes, then more eyes.]

Hyenas can take a kill away from lions, and in the Ngorongoro Crater they manage it when they bring in at least six times as many hyenas as there are lions.

[An ice-hockey rink: six hyenas in helmets, one of them in goalie pads, facing a single lioness.]

Six to one is a full hockey team, goalie included, against a single player.

[A big adult male lion with a dark mane walks in. Every hyena backs off at once. Red label: "EXCEPT HIM".]

That's against lionesses and young males, though, because when an adult male lion is there, the lions always keep the kill, however many hyenas show up.

[A sober card in the same white-on-black style: "IN THE CRATER: LIONS ARE THE MAIN CAUSE OF DEATH FOR HYENAS".]

This rivalry runs both ways, and in the crater, lions are the main cause of death for hyenas.

[A map of Tanzania. Label: "560+ PEOPLE". A second, smaller label by the Serengeti: "MOST ATTACKS: LIONS".]

Here's the honest part. Lions are the bigger danger to people. In Tanzania, lions killed more than five hundred and sixty people between nineteen ninety and two thousand four. In villages beside the Serengeti, lions caused most of the reported attacks on people, ahead of leopards and hyenas.

[The chalkboard, with a sticky note pinned to it: "DOUG. ALONE. ON FOOT. ALL NIGHT."]

But this scoreboard was never about people in general, because it's about Doug, alone, on foot, all night. A lion might be the worse animal to meet once, but a hyena is the worse animal to be near for a whole night.

[Doug crouched behind three lionesses on their kill, hopeful smile, cap on.]

So, with dawn still an hour away, Doug hides behind a pride of lionesses on their kill, because lions are the scary ones.

[Whoop rings fill the sky from every direction. Eyes, then shapes, then a ring of hyenas, many more than the lions.]

Then the whooping starts, and the clan arrives out of the grey light, and it outnumbers them by a lot.

[The lionesses stand up, look at each other and walk away. Doug's speech bubble: "wait".]

The lions leave.

Doug does not.

[Cut to black. DOUG DEATHS: 267. The field guide opens on its two-page spread. The LION page is still empty, with no NO. The SPOTTED HYENA page has seven. Chalkboard final: LION 2, HYENA 9.]

Doug has died. Again.

[Closing image: dawn. On the left, a pride of lions eating, peaceful. On the right, one hyena trots away toward the distant whitewashed wall of Harar with the red cap in its mouth.]

The cap is heading to Harar, where someone will feed it by hand.

[End slate. DOUG DEATHS: 267.]

Doug deaths: two hundred and sixty-seven. Killed by lions: none. The next place Doug isn't welcome is on your screen now.
