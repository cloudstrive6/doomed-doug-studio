## Goose

Goose.

In the American poll, sixty-one percent of adults said they could beat a goose. In Britain it was forty-five percent, which suggests the British have met more geese.

[A Canada goose on the pond bank, neck low, mid-hiss. Doug beside it, confident.]

A big Canada goose weighs up to about six kilograms, a little less than the heaviest legal bowling ball, so a grown man outweighs it more than ten times over.

In spring, when geese are nesting, they defend the spot in a fixed order: first the hiss, then the honk, and then the wings go out and the goose charges.

Geese rarely bite. The weapon is the wing, and a wildlife biologist says a hit from it can knock a full-grown adult off their feet.

Here's the thing nobody in the poll stopped to think about. The goose is not trying to win, it is trying to make you leave, and the whole display is built to end the moment you do.

[Field guide page: "GOOSE". The "FRIEND?" box stays empty. A new box: "WIN?".]

Doug did not leave, and he stood his ground for four full seconds.

[Dust cloud. When it clears, Doug is flat on his back on the grass, eyes open, alive. The goose waddles off toward the pond wearing the red cap.]

[Next shot: the cap back on Doug's head, slightly bent. DOUG DEATHS: 93.]

## House Cat

House Cat.

This is one of the most confident lines in the whole poll: sixty-nine percent of Americans and sixty-six percent of Britons said they could beat a house cat in a fight.

Only the rat scored higher, which means millions of adults looked at a house cat and decided it was just slightly tougher than a rat.

[A tabby on a garden wall, ears flat back, tail lashing. Label: "HOUSE CAT". Doug in front of it, fists up.]

An average house cat weighs about four and a half kilograms, roughly the weight of two house bricks, so on paper this is the easiest fight Doug will have all day.

It is also the only fight on this list where the animal does not need to win on the day.

## Red Kangaroo

Red Kangaroo.

About one in six American men told the poll they could beat a kangaroo, and that number should be a lot lower than it is.

[Outback at dusk, dusty orange. A big male red kangaroo stands upright, chest out. Doug beside it for scale. A front door beside both. Label: "RED KANGAROO".]

Big male red kangaroos weigh up to about ninety kilograms, as heavy as a large grown man. Some stand more than one point eight meters tall, which is nearly the top of a front door. The males are about twice the weight of the females, and the big ones are the ones Doug is about to meet.

Males spend much of their lives sparring with other males to hold their place in the pecking order, so for a big male, fighting is not an emergency. It is routine.

[Diagram: kangaroo side view. Arrow from the tail to the ground. Label: "LEG NUMBER FIVE".]

In twenty fourteen, researchers measured what a red kangaroo's tail does when the animal walks slowly. It plants the tail on the ground like a fifth leg, and the tail pushes the kangaroo forward as hard as its front and back legs combined.

Here's the problem. In a fight, that fifth leg becomes a stand.

According to a kangaroo expert at the Australian Museum, fighting males balance on their tails and kick out with both back legs at the same time. Doug brought two fists, and the kangaroo brought five limbs.

Fatal attacks on people are extremely rare. One in Western Australia, in twenty twenty-two, was reported as the first in the whole country since nineteen thirty-six.

[Doug in red boxing gloves, classic stance, bouncing on his toes. Speech bubble: "come on then". The kangaroo leans back on its tail.]

Doug put on boxing gloves, because he had seen this on television, and he had a plan, which was to keep his guard up. The kangaroo leaned back.

[Dust cloud. Doug flat, X eyes. Ghost Doug floats up still wearing the gloves. The cap lands on a tuft of dry grass. Field guide: red "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 94.]

Doug has died. Again.

He is the first person in this video to lose a boxing match to something that was leaning on its own tail.

## Grey Wolf

Grey Wolf.

Roughly one in six American men also said they could beat a wolf. Notice the wording of the question, though, because it said a wolf. Singular.

[Forest at dusk, deep blue-green. One grey wolf in a clearing, head low. Label: "GREY WOLF".]

A grey wolf is not enormous. Males weigh from about thirty to eighty kilograms depending on where they live, so even a big one weighs about the same as three bags of cement.

What a wolf has is company. Grey wolves mainly hunt in packs, and the pack is how they bring down animals far bigger than any single wolf, like elk, moose and even bison. In Yellowstone National Park, the average pack has about ten wolves in it.

[A wolf next to a moose. Then five wolf silhouettes next to the same moose.]

Young wolves start hunting with the pack at about ten months old, so by the time a wolf is fully grown, it has spent most of its life practicing teamwork. Doug has spent most of his life practicing nothing in particular.

Now the honest part, because wolves get a worse reputation than they deserve. A report from the Norwegian Institute for Nature Research counted four hundred ninety-one victims of wolf attacks worldwide from two thousand two to twenty twenty.

Most of those attacks involved wolves with rabies. Across all of Europe and North America, over eighteen years, the report found two fatal attacks, and the researchers put the risk above zero but too small to put a number on.

But that's not the part that should worry Doug. The poll asked about one wolf, and a pack hunting something Doug's size does not show up one at a time.

[Doug squares up to the single wolf, fists raised. Speech bubble: "one on one". The camera pulls back. Four more wolves sit in the trees, watching.]

Doug squared up to one wolf, exactly as the question described. Then the camera pulled back.

[Dust cloud. X eyes. Ghost Doug floats up past five pairs of yellow eyes. The cap hangs on a low branch. Field guide: red "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 95.]

Doug was ready for one wolf. Nobody had told the other four about the poll.

## Cassowary

Cassowary.

This one was not in the poll either, and it looks like an easy win: a shy bird that mostly eats plants. It is also one of the very few birds known to have killed a person.

[Rainforest green. A tall cassowary with a blue neck and a tall casque on its head. Label: "CASSOWARY". Doug beside it, slightly shorter.]

The southern cassowary is the second-heaviest bird alive after the ostrich. The females are the big ones, averaging around fifty-eight kilograms, and an adult can stand about one point eight meters tall.

[Red arrow to one foot. A ballpoint pen beside the claw for scale. Label: "CLAW".]

Each foot carries a dagger-like claw about twelve centimeters long, nearly the length of a ballpoint pen, and a cassowary can jump about one and a half meters from a standstill.

When one attacks, it charges and kicks. In a study of attacks in Queensland, Australia, most victims were chased or charged, and far fewer were actually kicked.

The strange part is how most of those attacks started. The same study looked at one hundred fifty cassowary attacks on people, and three in four came from birds that people had fed before.

[Diagram: a cassowary, a sandwich, a person. Arrow: "PERSON = FOOD".]

The bird learns that humans mean food. When the food stops coming, the bird does not stop coming.

The researcher also noted that this kind of begging behaviour had not been recorded in his study area before nineteen eighty-five. The birds did not change on their own. They were taught.

Deaths are still very rare, and only two are well documented: one in Australia in nineteen twenty-six, and one in Florida in two thousand nineteen, on a farm where the birds were kept.

[Doug arrives with a paper bag labelled "BIRDSEED". Speech bubble: "hi friend?". The field guide's "FRIEND?" box gets a tick.]

Doug arrived with a bag of birdseed and the words hi friend. He fed the bird once. Then he ran out.

[Dust cloud. X eyes. Ghost Doug floats up. The bag lies on the forest floor with the cap on top of it. Field guide: red "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 96.]

The cassowary kept the birdseed, and the cap stayed on the bag, which is the most loyal thing anyone did today.

## Chimpanzee

Chimpanzee.

More than one in five American men told the poll they could beat a chimpanzee. You have probably heard that chimps are many times stronger than people, which would make those men extremely brave.

[A male chimpanzee sitting on a log in rainforest shade, arms long, expression flat. Label: "CHIMPANZEE".]

Chimp super strength has been reported since the nineteen twenties, and in twenty seventeen a team finally tested it properly, using real muscle samples and computer models of whole muscles.

Their answer was that chimp muscle produces about one point three five times the force and power of human muscle of the same size. The researchers called that advantage modest, which is not a word anyone had used about chimps before.

[A bar: human "1.00", chimp "1.35". Next to it, the crossed-out myth: "5x? 8x?".]

That is not super strength. If Doug can lift a twenty kilogram suitcase, a chimp with the same amount of muscle could lift about twenty-seven.

The advantage mostly comes from muscle fiber. Chimps carry more fast-twitch fiber, the kind built for short, explosive bursts, while humans lean toward the slower kind built for endurance.

Here's where the good news runs out. The myth was wrong, and it does not help Doug at all.

One point three five times stronger is still stronger, and chimps are not shy about using it. A twenty fourteen study pooled five decades of field records from eighteen chimpanzee communities and counted more than one hundred fifty killings, observed or suspected, of chimps by other chimps.

[Diagram: eight chimp silhouettes on one side, one on the other. Label: "8 vs 1".]

More than nine in ten of the attackers were males, and in a typical attack they outnumbered the victim about eight to one. Chimps do not go looking for a fair fight. They go looking for a sure one.

Wild chimpanzees have also killed people, in places where cleared forest has pushed them right up against villages and farms.

[Doug sitting on the same log, reading a paper titled "PNAS 2017". He relaxes, puts the paper down and holds out his hand, elbow on the log. Speech bubble: "arm wrestle?".]

Doug read the twenty seventeen study, relaxed, and offered to arm-wrestle.

[Off-screen: a single dust cloud rising from behind the log. The cap lands on the paper. Field guide: red "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 97.]

Doug was right about the science, and wrong about absolutely everything else.

[Big label: "NO CONTEST". Background shifts to a grey, misty jungle.]

No contest.

## Gorilla

Gorilla.

Eight percent of Americans said they could beat a gorilla. In Britain it was two percent, which is still a worrying number of people if you picture them all queuing up.

[A huge silverback, upright, mid chest-beat. Two grown men drawn beside it as outlines. Label: "SILVERBACK".]

An adult male western lowland gorilla weighs about one hundred thirty-six kilograms on average, and up to about two hundred twenty-seven, which is roughly two to three grown men in a single animal. A male counts as a full adult silverback from about thirteen years old, named for the pale saddle of hair across his back.

When a silverback feels threatened, he does not start with a fight. He stands upright, slaps his chest with cupped hands and roars, and if the intruder stays, he may drop to all fours and charge.

According to the Smithsonian's National Zoo, gorillas that charge generally do not hit the intruder, and these displays help keep order between groups while reducing the chance of injury. The charge is the message.

The surprising part is what the chest beat is really saying. A twenty twenty-one study of wild mountain gorillas in Rwanda found that bigger males make chest beats with a lower pitch, and the drumming can be heard more than a kilometer away.

[Diagram: a big gorilla with a low wavy line, a small gorilla with a high wavy line. Label: "BIG = LOW".]

The gorilla is announcing exactly how big it is, from far away, so that a rival can decide whether to back off before anybody has to find out up close.

Attacks by wild gorillas on people are very rare, and researchers who study them say they are usually defensive.

[Doug faces the silverback and beats his own chest. A tiny wavy line comes off him. Speech bubble: "me too".]

Doug beat his own chest in reply, and it sounded like someone tapping a cardboard box. The gorilla looked at him for a long moment, and walked away.

[The gorilla leaves. Doug alone in the clearing, chest puffed. Field guide: "MAYBE". DOUG DEATHS: 97. In the corner, a drawing of a flat Doug with X eyes is quietly crumpled up and dropped out of frame.]

Doug is fine. The narrator had the death drawn already.

The gorilla listened to Doug's chest beat, did the arithmetic, and decided Doug was not worth the walk.

## Saltwater Crocodile

Saltwater Crocodile.

This is the heaviest reptile alive, and it does not fight the way the poll imagined. There is no squaring up, and there is no first round.

[Muddy brown river. A huge crocodile half-submerged, only eyes and nostrils visible. A family car on the bank for scale, shorter than the crocodile. Label: "SALTWATER CROCODILE".]

The biggest males reach about six meters, which is longer than a family car, and can weigh as much as one point two tonnes.

In twenty twelve, researchers measured the bite force of every living species of crocodile and alligator. A saltwater crocodile about five meters long bit down with about three thousand seven hundred pounds of force, roughly sixteen thousand newtons.

[A family car hanging over a pair of jaws, as if being weighed. Label: "≈ 1 CAR".]

That is the strongest bite ever directly measured in a living animal, roughly the weight of a family car, delivered all at once. For comparison, National Geographic's report on the study put lions, tigers and hyenas at around a quarter of that.

It hunts by ambush. It lies in wait near the water's edge, barely moving, and then launches a sudden attack over a very short distance.

It can hold its breath for up to an hour and a half. Its brain is about the size of a walnut, which is plenty to decide whether to attack.

But here's the disturbing part. The strongest bite on Earth belongs to an animal that spends most of its time doing nothing at all, so it does not need to win a fight. It only needs Doug to come down to the water.

In Australia, a study of forty-two years of records counted eighty-seven saltwater crocodile attacks on people, and twenty-seven of them were fatal. One of the two biggest factors in who survived was how much bigger the crocodile was than the person.

[Doug walks the riverbank with binoculars, scanning the water. Speech bubble: "where are you". He sits down next to a large brown log at the water's edge.]

Doug went looking for the crocodile, and after an hour he sat down next to a log to rest.

[The log opens one eye. Splash. Dust and spray. Ghost Doug floats up over brown water. The cap floats alone on the surface, turning slowly. Field guide: red "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 98.]

The log was the crocodile, and it had been waiting a lot longer than Doug had.

## Polar Bear

Polar Bear.

The animal Americans felt least able to beat was the grizzly bear, and six percent still said yes. Nobody asked them about this one.

[Arctic sea ice, white and pale blue. A polar bear standing on its hind legs. A house ceiling line drawn behind it, below its head. Label: "POLAR BEAR".]

The polar bear is the largest land carnivore alive. Adult males normally weigh three hundred fifty to five hundred ninety kilograms, about as much as a concert grand piano, and standing up, a polar bear can reach three meters, well above a normal ceiling.

Scientists collected every polar bear attack on people they could find, from eighteen seventy to twenty fourteen, across the five countries where polar bears live. They counted seventy-three attacks, and twenty people died.

That is a small number for well over a century, and the researchers describe these attacks as extremely rare.

[Diagram: a bear, a person, an arrow. Label: "PREDATOR".]

But in most of those attacks, the researchers judged that the bear was acting as a predator. It was hunting.

Attacks by females were rare, and most of those were mothers defending cubs. That is the kind of attack people expect, and it is not the kind that should worry Doug.

And that leads to the worst part. The bears most likely to threaten people were adult males that were nutritionally stressed, so the dangerous polar bear is the hungry one. It is not defending anything. When hunting goes well, a polar bear can eat more than forty-five kilograms of seal blubber in one sitting.

As the sea ice shrinks, the researchers expect more hungry bears to spend longer on land, closer to the people who live there.

[Doug walks across the ice holding out a fish. Speech bubble: "for you".]

Doug agreed to this. He would like that noted.

He brought a fish as a peace offering. The bear looked at the fish, and then at Doug, and was not interested in the fish.

[Dust cloud of snow. X eyes. Ghost Doug floats up over the ice. The fish lies untouched next to the cap. Field guide: red "NO". DOUG DEATHS: 99.]

Doug deaths: ninety-nine. The narrator would just like to point out that the next one is a round number.

## Elephant

Elephant.

Eight percent of Americans said they could beat an elephant in an unarmed fight, and in Britain it was two percent. It is not zero in either country.

[Savanna at sunset, dark red sky. An African bush elephant, ears fully out, facing the camera. Four family cars lined up beside it on a scale bar. Label: "AFRICAN BUSH ELEPHANT".]

The African bush elephant is the largest land animal alive. An adult male typically weighs four to seven tonnes, about as much as four family cars, and stands three to three point seven meters at the shoulder.

It is also one of the most careful listeners on the planet. In Amboseli National Park in Kenya, researchers hid a loudspeaker and played recordings to forty-seven elephant families, and in each recording a person said the same calm sentence in their own language.

[A hidden loudspeaker in a bush. Sound waves going toward a group of elephants.]

Some voices were Maasai men. Maasai herders have sometimes speared elephants in conflicts over water and grazing land, while the Kamba, whose men recorded the other voices, are farmers who rarely clash with elephants at all.

When the elephants heard Maasai men, they bunched up tightly and sniffed the air far more often. Kamba voices got a much weaker reaction, and so did Maasai women and boys.

The oldest matriarchs were the calmest judges of all. Families led by a matriarch over forty-two years old never retreated from the voices of boys. They had heard enough humans to know which ones mattered.

The researchers even raised the pitch of the men's voices so they sounded like women. It did not fool the elephants at all.

Here's the part that should bother you. The elephant does not need to see you, because it can judge from your voice alone whether you are a threat.

It is not a gentle giant, either. In Zimbabwe, a study of deaths caused by large wild animals from twenty sixteen to twenty twenty-two found that elephants and crocodiles together caused more than four out of five of them.

[Doug walks up the dusty slope toward the elephant, waving. Speech bubble: "hi friend?".]

Doug walked up and said hi friend.

The elephant listened very carefully, and decided Doug was not dangerous. It was right.

It did not matter.

That's one hundred.

The cap sits on top of the guide, and out of everything that took part in this video, it is the only one with a winning record.
