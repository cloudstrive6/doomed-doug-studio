### One walrus.

**1. The Seal Eater** (hook item, one of the three strongest; death 275)
- Facts:
  - Most walruses eat clams and other bottom animals. **Some walruses, mostly bulls, also kill and eat seals.**
    Source: Lowry & Fay 1984, "Seal eating by walruses in the Bering and Chukchi Seas", *Polar Biology*. Verify how often it happens, which seal species, and what the paper says about how the seal is held and eaten.
  - These walruses eat the seal's skin and blubber, the fattiest parts. The writer gives the paper's wording, says it plainly, and describes no injury.
  - Walruses have also been seen killing seabirds (only with a source, for example Svalbard observations; otherwise cut it).
- Analogy: a bull is heavier than the seal it eats by about as much as a car outweighs a bicycle. Use sourced weights for both animals and check the ratio.
- **Twist by 0:45 (about 145 words):** the animal with the giant tusks and the clam diet sometimes eats seals, and it doesn't need to chew them.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug lies on a floe next to a resting bull, sunbathing.
  - **"Doug did not agree to this."** (here)
  - The bull rolls over and wraps its front flippers around him. "Doug thought it was a hug." Cut to black before anything happens. Death 275, flat.
  - The cap sits on the empty floe. Field guide: NO.

**2. The Suction** (Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - Walruses feed on the seafloor by suction. They pull a clam's body out of its shell and leave the shell behind. They can also blow jets of water to dig prey out of the mud.
    Sources: Fay 1982, *Ecology and Biology of the Pacific Walrus* (USFWS North American Fauna 74); Kastelein & Mosterd 1989 or Kastelein et al. 1994 on suction pressure.
  - Give the measured suction pressure, and how many clams a walrus can eat in one feeding bout, **only if sourced**.
  - The moustache is several hundred sensitive whiskers that find clams in the dark (USFWS/NOAA; verify the count).
- Analogy: the suction pressure compared with a household vacuum cleaner or a sink plunger. Use the source's kPa and a sourced figure for the appliance.
- Twist: the strongest thing on a walrus might be its mouth, and it uses it on clams.
- Doug beat: "hi friend?" (the only one this episode). Doug offers a clam on his palm. The clam's body is gone and the shell is left in his hand.
  Doug looks at his hand and it is still there. "Doug is fine." Field guide: "MAYBE".
  **Cartoon only: nothing touches Doug's skin.** The suction acts on the clam.

**3. The Tusks** (death 276)
- Facts:
  - Both sexes have tusks, which are long upper canine teeth. In Pacific bulls they can be up to about 1 m (NOAA/USFWS; verify the length and the sex difference).
  - Walruses dig their tusks into the ice to haul themselves out of the water. The genus name *Odobenus* means "tooth-walker" (verify the etymology source).
  - Bulls weigh up to about 1,500-1,800 kg (NOAA/USFWS; give the source's figure in kg).
- Analogy: a bull pulling itself onto the ice by its teeth is like someone doing a pull-up while holding the bar with their teeth, and weighing as much as a small hatchback.
  **Not "a small car"** if 034 or 005 used it for weight; pick a different everyday object, sourced.
- Twist: the tusks aren't mainly weapons. They're climbing gear, and the floe Doug is standing on is the thing being climbed.
- Doug beat: Doug stands on the edge of a small floe. The tusks come up out of the water on either side of his feet, then the rest of the walrus follows.
  The floe tips. Death 276, played as a cartoon slide off the floe, then a splash and cut to black. The cap floats.
  - Re-hook into "Two.": "And one walrus is the easy number."

    Reports say a mother was protecting her calf. Sources: WWF Russia, the Barents Observer and a major outlet such as the BBC.
    **Verify "mother with calf" and "nobody hurt"; no names.**
  - Lone walruses far from home have climbed onto small boats and sunk or damaged them.
    "Wally" in 2021 (Ireland, Wales, Isles of Scilly; RNLI and local reporting) is one example.
    "Freya" in 2022 (Oslo) is another. **Freya:** one line on the boats at most. If her death comes up at all, it gets one plain sentence with the Norwegian Directorate of Fisheries as the source and no joke. Leaving it out is better.

- Analogy: the bull from item 3 climbing into a rowing boat is like parking a hatchback in a canoe. Use the source's weight, and use a different object if item 3 already used the hatchback.

**5. The Fight** (death 278)
- Facts:
  - In the breeding season bulls fight with their tusks. Old bulls are covered in scars, and tusks can break (Fay 1982; NOAA).
  - A bull's neck and shoulder skin is very thick (up to several cm), with lumps called "bosses" that protect it from other bulls' tusks.
    Fay 1982 or a museum source; **verify the thickness**, and cut the number if it can't be pinned down.
  - Bulls are bigger and heavier than cows. Use the size difference from the source.
- Analogy: the neck skin compared with something everyday, such as a phone book or a slice of bread stacked N times, using the source's thickness.
- Twist: a walrus's skin is built to be stabbed by another walrus, and Doug's isn't.
- Doug beat: Doug holds a tape measure between two bulls "to settle it scientifically". Both bulls swing at once. Death 278, cut to black on the swing.
  **No tusk on Doug on screen.** The tape measure retracts on its own and the cap is on the end of it.
  - **Must not copy 034's referee boss** (no whistle, no stepping between two charging animals). This is a measurement gag, not a referee.

### Hundreds.

**6. The Sleep** (Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - Walruses can sleep in the water, either hanging from the ice by their tusks or floating upright. Air sacs in the throat (pharyngeal pouches) inflate to keep the head up (USFWS/NOAA; verify).
  - In a study of captive walruses, they could stay awake and swimming for long stretches, around 3.5 days (Pryaslova et al. 2009, *Behavioural Brain Research*; verify the number and the conditions).
  - In the breeding season, bulls use those pouches to make bell-like sounds underwater (Fay 1982; Stirling et al. 1987; verify).
- Analogy: the throat pouches work like built-in water wings, and three and a half days awake is about a long weekend without sleep.
- Twist: a herd of hundreds can hang off the ice edge asleep, by their teeth, and that is the safest moment in this whole video.
- Doug beat: Doug in orange inflatable armbands floats among sleeping walruses hanging by their tusks. He falls asleep too. He wakes on a different floe, three days later, with a sunburn on one side.
  "Doug is fine." The narrator expected more from hundreds of walruses.

**7. The Polar Bear** (Doug SURVIVES; "Doug is fine.")
- Facts:
  - Polar bears hunt walruses, but they usually go for calves. Adults are big and armed, and a bear tries to make a herd panic and run so that a calf is left behind
    (Kochnev's Chukotka observations, or a USGS/USFWS source; verify).
  - Healthy adult walruses can defend themselves against bears with their tusks. **Say only what a source supports**: no "walruses kill polar bears" unless it is cited.
- Analogy: from the bear's side, attacking a herd of hundreds of tusked adults is like walking into a room of N people holding broom handles. The writer can drop this; keep it simple.
- Twist: the most feared hunter in the Arctic walks around the walruses. It is waiting for them to run.
- Doug beat:
  - The one callback, one sentence, not in the first 30 s: "The last time Doug met a polar bear, it walked toward him on purpose." (writer rewords; 031 callback)
  - This bear walks straight past Doug. It wants the herd to move, and Doug is not worth the effort.
  - "Doug is fine." The narrator thinks he should be more offended.
  - **No calf is caught on screen.** The bear walks off out of frame.
  - Re-hook into "Thirty-five thousand.": "The bear wanted them to run. On a beach, they don't need a bear."

### Thirty-five thousand.

**8. The Haulout** (death 279)
- Facts:
  - In years when the sea ice pulls back far from the coast, Pacific walruses come ashore in huge haulouts. Point Lay, Alaska, has had tens of thousands at once
    (about 35,000 in September 2014, NOAA aerial survey count 35,036; the 2015 figure was also about 35,000. CD 11-02: the earlier "about 40,000" lead was unsourced and is withdrawn).
  - The shift onto land is linked to the summer sea ice retreating past the shallow feeding grounds (USGS; Jay et al.).
    **One plain sentence, no lecture.**
- Analogy: 35,000 walruses at about a tonne each weigh more than [an everyday sum the writer checks, for example a fully loaded cruise ship's passengers × N, or a big stadium crowd several times over]. Use real arithmetic, sourced on both sides.
- Twist: on the ice there was room to spread out. On the beach they lie on top of each other.
- Doug beat: Doug looks for a space to lie down on the beach. A cow lies down on the space, and Doug is in it. Death 279, played flat.
  The cap rides on her back. Death 280 is the boss.

**9. The Stampede** (the boss; death 280)
- Facts:
  - A low plane, a boat, a person or a polar bear can startle a crowded haulout. The walruses rush for the water, and the animals crushed in the rush are mostly calves and young.
    - **2009, Icy Cape, Alaska:** 131 dead, mostly young (USGS/USFWS; verify).
    - **2007, the Chukotka coast:** a much larger toll was reported (thousands; verify the figure and the source, or cut it).
  - Because of this, the agencies ask planes to keep high and far from haulouts, and the community of Point Lay asks visitors and media to stay away when the walruses are ashore
    (USFWS/FAA guidance, Point Lay requests; verify).
    **State it once as what the agencies and the town do, not as advice to the viewer.**
- **Honesty line, required:** on these beaches the ones in danger are the walruses, mostly the youngest (USFWS guidelines; USGS Icy Cape). CD 11-02: "walruses very rarely hurt people" is cut (no agency source; ADF&G and FWS say the opposite). No packaging may claim walruses are harmless to people.
- **Sober card (required, before Doug's beat):** white on black, the Icy Cape number and the year only. No Doug, no joke, no drawing of young walruses dying.
- Twist (the promise paid off): Doug is standing between about thirty-five thousand walruses and the sea when something in the sky startles them, and they all leave at once.
- Doug beat:
  - Doug stands at the waterline with a hand-painted sign: "QUIET PLEASE".

  - Doug turns the sign round. The back says "nope". Death 280, played flat. Cut to black on the first row.
  - Field guide closes with six NOs and the sticky note "EATS CLAMS."
- **Closing image:** the empty beach, then the sea full of heads. One walrus hauls out alone and lies down on the cap. Kicker (the writer rewords):
  "The cap is on a beach with a walrus asleep on it. Nobody is going to ask for it back."
- **Outro:** 30 words or fewer. The final counter (280), one dry line, re-hooked to the end slate. No recap, no subscribe pitch.
